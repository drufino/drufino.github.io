2.7 Abelian Categories

Following [Fre64] we define an Abelian category without reference to additivity, however to make the presentation simpler we assume finite limits and colimits exist.

Definition 2.7.1

We say a category 𝒞︀ is Abelian if it

  1. is pointed,
  2. has finite limits and colimits, and
  3. is binormal
Remark 2.7.2 (Abelian is Self-Dual)
If 𝒞︀ is Abelian then so is 𝒞︀op. Indeed 𝒞︀op is again pointed with the same zero object (2.6.54); finite limits in 𝒞︀ become finite colimits in 𝒞︀op and vice versa (2.6.92), so 𝒞︀op again has finite limits and colimits; and 𝒞︀op is binormal, since monic/epic swap under passing to 𝒞︀op (2.6.33) while ker(𝑓)/coker(𝑓) swap (2.6.154), so a monomorphism being a kernel in 𝒞︀ corresponds to an epimorphism being a cokernel in 𝒞︀op and vice versa.
Lemma 2.7.3
Let 𝒞︀ be a normal pointed category and 𝑚:𝐼↪︎𝑌 a monomorphism with cokernel 𝑝:𝑌→coker(𝑚). Then 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 factors through 𝑚 if and only if 𝑝∘𝑓=0.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

Suppose 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑔 for some 𝑔:𝑋→𝐼. Then 𝑝∘𝑓=𝑝∘𝑚∘𝑔=0∘𝑔=0, since 𝑝∘𝑚=0 by definition of the cokernel (2.6.153).

Conversely, suppose 𝑝∘𝑓=0. As 𝒞︀ is normal and 𝑚 is monic, 𝑚 is a normal monomorphism (2.6.163), i.e. (𝑚,𝐼) is the kernel of some morphism (2.6.159). By (2.6.162) it follows that 𝑚=ker(coker(𝑚))=ker(𝑝). So by (2.6.150), 𝑝∘𝑓=0 gives a (unique) morphism 𝑔:𝑋→𝐼 such that 𝑚∘𝑔=𝑓, that is, 𝑓 factors through 𝑚. ∎

Lemma 2.7.4

Let 𝒞︀ be an Abelian category and 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 a morphism. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑓 is epic
  2. Im(𝑓)=𝑌 (i.e. (𝑌,𝑓,1𝑌) is an image for 𝑓)
  3. coker(𝑓)=0

Dually, the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑓 is monic
  2. Coim(𝑓)=𝑋 (i.e. (𝑋,1𝑋,𝑓) is a coimage for 𝑓)
  3. ker(𝑓)=0

Proof. We only prove the first half, with the second following immediately by duality: since 𝒞︀ is Abelian, so is 𝒞︀op (2.7.2), and by (2.6.169) and (2.6.154) the conditions a)–c) are respectively dual to monic, Coim(𝑓)=𝑋, and ker(𝑓)=0.

a) ⟹ c) This is (2.6.151).

c) ⟹ b) Suppose 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒 where 𝑚:𝐼↪︎𝑌 is a monomorphism. Consider the cokernel 𝑝:𝑌→coker(𝑚)

Then 𝑝∘𝑚=0⟹𝑝∘𝑓=0. As coker(𝑓)=0 there is a morphism 0→𝑍 such that the bottom right triangle commutes, i.e. 𝑝=0. Then 𝑝∘1𝑌=0 and so by (2.7.3) there exists 𝑗:𝑌→𝐼 such that 𝑚∘𝑗=1𝑌. As 𝑚 is monic this factorisation is unique and we conclude that Im(𝑓)=𝑌.

b) ⟹ a) Suppose that 𝛼∘𝑓=𝛽∘𝑓 and let 𝑚:eq(𝛼,𝛽)→𝑌 be the equaliser, which is therefore monic (2.6.135).

By the universal property of the equaliser there is a (unique) factorisation 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒. Since (𝑌,𝑓,1𝑌) is the image of 𝑓 (hypothesis b)) and (𝐼,𝑒,𝑚) is another factorisation of 𝑓 through a monomorphism, by the universal property of the image (2.6.167) there is a unique morphism 𝑗:𝑌→𝐼 such that 𝑚∘𝑗=1𝑌. Therefore 𝑚 is split-epic so by (2.6.36) it is an isomorphism. Since (𝐼,𝑚)=eq(𝛼,𝛽), by (2.6.136) we conclude 𝛼=𝛽. ∎

2.7.1 Images, Coimages and the First Isomorphism Theorem

In case of modules we have coker(𝑓)=𝑌/Im(𝑓) and ker(coker(𝑓))=Im(𝑓) so we might expect this relation to hold in the abstract setting.

Proposition 2.7.5 (Im(𝑓)=ker(coker(𝑓)) and Coim(𝑓)=coker(ker(𝑓)))

Let 𝒞︀ be a pointed category with kernels and cokernels and 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 a morphism. Then there is a unique morphism 𝑒:𝑋→ker(coker(𝑓)) making the following diagram commute

When 𝒞︀ is Abelian 𝑒 is an epimorphism and Im(𝑓)=(ker(coker(𝑓)),𝑒,𝑚).

Dually, there is a unique morphism 𝑚′:coker(ker(𝑓))→𝑌 such that the following diagram commutes

When 𝒞︀ is Abelian 𝑚′ is a monomorphism and Coim(𝑓)=(coker(ker(𝑓)),𝑒′,𝑚′).

Proof. Let 𝑝:𝑌→coker(𝑓) be the cokernel of 𝑓, so by (2.6.153) 𝑝∘𝑓=0. Then by (2.6.150), applied to the kernel 𝑚=ker(𝑝), 𝑒 exists and is unique.

Dually, let 𝑖:ker(𝑓)→𝑋 be the kernel of 𝑓, so by (2.6.150) 𝑓∘𝑖=0. Then by (2.6.153), applied to the cokernel 𝑒′=coker(𝑖), 𝑚′ exists and is unique.

Now suppose additionally that 𝒞︀ is Abelian. We first show that (ker(coker(𝑓)),𝑒,𝑚)=Im(𝑓). By (2.6.135) 𝑚 is a monomorphism. Consider another factorisation 𝑓=𝑚″∘𝑒″ with 𝑚″ a monomorphism

Let 𝑟≔coker(𝑚″). Then 𝑟∘𝑓=𝑟∘𝑚″∘𝑒″=0. Therefore, by the universal property of coker(𝑓) (2.6.153), there is a unique morphism ℎ:coker(𝑓)→coker(𝑚″) such that ℎ∘𝑝=𝑟, and we deduce that 𝑟∘𝑚=ℎ∘𝑝∘𝑚=ℎ∘0=0. Therefore by (2.7.3) 𝑚 factors through 𝑚″ as required. Uniqueness follows because 𝑚″ is monic and therefore (ker(coker(𝑓)),𝑒,𝑚) is an image for 𝑓

Next we show 𝑒 is an epimorphism. By (2.7.4) applied to 𝑒 (with codomain 𝐼≔ker(coker(𝑓))), it suffices to show Im(𝑒)=𝐼, i.e. that (𝐼,𝑒,1𝐼) is the image of 𝑒. Suppose 𝑒=𝑚1∘𝑒1 for some monomorphism 𝑚1:𝐼1→𝐼. Then 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒=(𝑚∘𝑚1)∘𝑒1 is a factorisation of 𝑓 through the monomorphism 𝑚∘𝑚1, so by the universal property of the image (𝐼,𝑒,𝑚) of 𝑓 (2.6.167) (established above) there is a unique morphism 𝑔:𝐼→𝐼1 such that (𝑚∘𝑚1)∘𝑔=𝑚; as 𝑚 is monic this gives 𝑚1∘𝑔=1𝐼. This is precisely the factorisation required for (𝐼,𝑒,1𝐼) to be the image of 𝑒, so Im(𝑒)=𝐼, and therefore 𝑒 is an epimorphism.

The remaining claims for the dual statement now follow by duality, applying the above to 𝑓op in 𝒞︀op, which is again Abelian (2.7.2): by (2.6.154), ker𝒞︀op(coker𝒞︀op(𝑓op)) is identified with coker(ker(𝑓)), matching (by uniqueness of the first paragraph, dualized) our 𝑚′ and 𝑒′, and by (2.6.33) and (2.6.169) the epic/image conclusions in 𝒞︀op translate to 𝑚′ monic and (coker(ker(𝑓)),𝑒′,𝑚′)=Coim(𝑓) in 𝒞︀. ∎

In the category of modules we have coker(ker(𝑓))=𝑋/ker(𝑓) and a canonical isomorphism 𝑋/ker(𝑓)→∼Im(𝑓)=ker(coker(𝑓)), so we may expect something similar to hold true in the Abelian setting.

Proposition 2.7.6 (First Isomorphism Theorem)

Let 𝒞︀ be a pointed category with kernels and cokernels and 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 a morphism. Then there is a unique morphism 𝜃:coker(ker(𝑓))→ker(coker(𝑓)) such that the following diagram commutes

When 𝒞︀ is an Abelian category then 𝜃 is an isomorphism. Furthermore both (coker(ker(𝑓)),𝑒′,𝑚∘𝜃) and (ker(coker(𝑓)),𝜃∘𝑒′,𝑚) are epi-mono factorisations of 𝑓 and both an image and coimage.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

By (2.7.5) there is a unique morphism 𝑚′:coker(ker(𝑓))→𝑌 such that 𝑓=𝑚′∘𝑒′.

Since 𝑞∘𝑓=0, where 𝑞:𝑌→coker(𝑓) is the cokernel of 𝑓 (2.6.153), we have 𝑞∘𝑚′∘𝑒′=𝑞∘𝑓=0; as 𝑒′ is epic (2.6.135), 𝑞∘𝑚′=0. So by the universal property of 𝑚=ker(𝑞) (2.6.150), there is a unique morphism 𝜃:coker(ker(𝑓))→ker(coker(𝑓)) such that 𝑚∘𝜃=𝑚′. Combining, 𝑚∘𝜃∘𝑒′=𝑚′∘𝑒′=𝑓, as required.

For uniqueness, suppose 𝜃′ also satisfies 𝑚∘𝜃′∘𝑒′=𝑓. Then 𝑚∘(𝜃∘𝑒′)=𝑓=𝑚∘(𝜃′∘𝑒′), and since 𝑚 is monic (2.6.135), 𝜃∘𝑒′=𝜃′∘𝑒′; as 𝑒′ is epic (2.6.135), 𝜃=𝜃′.

Suppose now 𝒞︀ is Abelian, so that 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒 is the image factorisation and 𝑓=𝑚′∘𝑒′ is the coimage factorisation (2.7.5). Since 𝑚∘𝜃∘𝑒′=𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒 and 𝑚 is monic (2.6.135), we may conclude that 𝜃∘𝑒′=𝑒.

Since 𝑚∘𝜃=𝑚′ is monic, 𝜃 is monic, and since 𝜃∘𝑒′=𝑒 is epic, 𝜃 is epic. As 𝒞︀ is Abelian it is binormal (2.7.1), hence balanced (2.6.164), so 𝜃, being both monic and epic, is an isomorphism.

Finally, applying (2.6.172) to the coimage (coker(ker(𝑓)),𝑒′,𝑚′), and dually to the image (ker(coker(𝑓)),𝑒,𝑚), via the isomorphism 𝜃 shows each factorisation is both an image and a coimage of 𝑓. ∎

Proposition 2.7.7 (Epi-Mono Factorisation in an Abelian Category)

Let 𝒞︀ be an Abelian category and 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 a morphism. Then 𝑓 factors as 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒 for some epimorphism 𝑒 and monomorphism 𝑚

and this factorisation is unique up to isomorphism: if 𝑓=𝑚1∘𝑒1 is another such factorisation, with 𝑒1:𝑋→𝐼1 epic and 𝑚1 monic, then there is a unique morphism 𝜃:𝐼→𝐼1 such that the following diagram commutes, and 𝜃 is (moreover) an isomorphism

Moreover, every epimorphism and every monomorphism of 𝒞︀ is regular, so any such factorisation 𝑓=𝑚1∘𝑒1 is simultaneously a regular epi-mono and an epi-regular-mono factorisation of 𝑓, and hence (𝐼1,𝑒1,𝑚1) is both an image and a coimage of 𝑓 (2.6.174).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

Regularity. Since 𝒞︀ is Abelian it is binormal (2.7.1), so every monomorphism is normal and every epimorphism is conormal (2.6.163), hence every monomorphism and every epimorphism of 𝒞︀ is regular (2.6.160). In particular, for any epi-mono factorisation 𝑓=𝑚1∘𝑒1, 𝑒1 is a regular epimorphism and 𝑚1 is a regular monomorphism, so 𝑓=𝑚1∘𝑒1 is simultaneously a regular epi-mono and an epi-regular-mono factorisation of 𝑓, and by (2.6.174) (both halves), (𝐼1,𝑒1,𝑚1) is both an image and a coimage of 𝑓.

Existence. By (2.7.5), taking 𝐼≔ker(coker(𝑓)) with structure maps 𝑒,𝑚, we obtain a factorisation 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒 with 𝑚 monic and, since 𝒞︀ is Abelian, 𝑒 epic.

Uniqueness up to isomorphism. Let 𝑓=𝑚1∘𝑒1 be any other such factorisation, 𝑒1:𝑋→𝐼1. By the Regularity paragraph, 𝑒 and 𝑒1 are regular epimorphisms, so 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒 and 𝑓=𝑚1∘𝑒1 are regular epi-mono factorisations of 𝑓 (2.6.173), and (2.6.178) gives a unique isomorphism 𝜃:𝐼→𝐼1 with 𝜃∘𝑒=𝑒1 and 𝑚1∘𝜃=𝑚.

Alternatively, uniqueness may be shown directly from the universal property of the image (2.6.167), without invoking regular epi-mono factorisations at all.

Uniqueness up to isomorphism. Let 𝑓=𝑚1∘𝑒1 be any other epi-mono factorisation, 𝑒1:𝑋→𝐼1. Since (𝐼,𝑒,𝑚)=Im(𝑓) (2.7.5) and 𝑚1 is monic, the universal property of the image (2.6.167) gives a unique morphism 𝜃:𝐼→𝐼1 such that 𝑚1∘𝜃=𝑚, and (as noted there) 𝜃∘𝑒=𝑒1.

We show 𝜃 is an isomorphism. If 𝜃𝑘1=𝜃𝑘2 then 𝑚1𝜃𝑘1=𝑚1𝜃𝑘2, i.e. 𝑚𝑘1=𝑚𝑘2, and since 𝑚 is monic, 𝑘1=𝑘2; so 𝜃 is monic. Since 𝜃∘𝑒=𝑒1 is epic, 𝜃 is epic. As 𝒞︀ is Abelian it is binormal (2.7.1), hence balanced (2.6.164), so 𝜃, being both monic and epic, is an isomorphism. ∎

2.7.2 Exact Sequences

Lemma 2.7.8 (Cancellation for Kernels and Cokernels)

Let 𝒞︀ be a pointed category with kernels and cokernels.

  1. If ℎ=𝜓∘𝑝 with 𝜓 monic, then (𝐾,𝑖) is a kernel of 𝑝 if and only if it is a kernel of ℎ.
  2. If 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒 with 𝑒 epic, then (𝑄,𝑞) is a cokernel of 𝑚 if and only if it is a cokernel of 𝑓.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

a) For any 𝑘 into the common domain of ℎ,𝑝, ℎ∘𝑘=𝜓∘𝑝∘𝑘; since 𝜓 is monic, 𝜓∘𝑝∘𝑘=0⟺𝑝∘𝑘=0, so ℎ∘𝑘=0⟺𝑝∘𝑘=0. In particular 𝑝∘𝑖=0⟺ℎ∘𝑖=0, and for any 𝑖′, 𝑝∘𝑖′=0⟺ℎ∘𝑖′=0; so the defining conditions of (2.6.150) hold for (𝐾,𝑖) and 𝑝 if and only if they hold for (𝐾,𝑖) and ℎ, that is, (𝐾,𝑖) is a kernel of 𝑝 if and only if it is a kernel of ℎ.

b) Dually, for any 𝑞′ out of the common codomain of 𝑓,𝑚, 𝑞′∘𝑓=𝑞′∘𝑚∘𝑒; since 𝑒 is epic, 𝑞′∘𝑚∘𝑒=0⟺𝑞′∘𝑚=0, so 𝑞′∘𝑓=0⟺𝑞′∘𝑚=0; in particular 𝑞∘𝑚=0⟺𝑞∘𝑓=0, and for any 𝑞′, 𝑞′∘𝑚=0⟺𝑞′∘𝑓=0; so the defining conditions of (2.6.153) hold for (𝑄,𝑞) and 𝑚 if and only if they hold for (𝑄,𝑞) and 𝑓, that is, (𝑄,𝑞) is a cokernel of 𝑚 if and only if it is a cokernel of 𝑓. ∎

Proposition 2.7.9 (Criteria for Exactness)

Let 𝒞︀ be an Abelian category, 𝑋⟶𝑓𝑌⟶𝑔𝑍 a pair of composable morphisms, 𝑓=𝑚∘𝑒 an epi-mono factorisation of 𝑓 and 𝑔=𝑚′∘𝑒′ an epi-mono factorisation of 𝑔. Then the following are equivalent:

  1. 𝑚=Im(𝑓)=ker(𝑔) (i.e. exactness in the sense of (2.6.200));
  2. 𝑔∘𝑓=0, and the canonical morphism 𝜑:Im(𝑓)→ker(𝑔) is epic;
  3. coker(𝑓)=Coim(𝑔)=𝑒′.
  4. 𝑔∘𝑓=0, and the canonical morphism 𝜑:coker(𝑓)→Coim(𝑔) is monic.

This does not depend on the choice of factorisation of either 𝑓 or 𝑔.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

Write 𝑘:ker(𝑔)→𝑌 for a kernel of 𝑔.

Independence of factorisation of 𝑓. By (2.6.200) (independence of representatives), applied with 𝒞︀ Abelian – so every epi-mono factorisation of 𝑓 is (in particular) an image of 𝑓 (2.7.7) – exactness of the pair at 𝑌, in particular the condition 𝑚=ker(𝑔), does not depend on the choice of epi-mono factorisation of 𝑓: if 𝑓=𝑚1∘𝑒1 is another, then 𝑚=ker(𝑔) if and only if 𝑚1=ker(𝑔).

Independence of factorisation of 𝑔. If 𝑔=𝑚1′∘𝑒1′ is another epi-mono factorisation of 𝑔, (2.7.7) gives an isomorphism 𝜙:𝐼′→𝐼1′ with 𝜙∘𝑒′=𝑒1′, so by (2.6.138) (applied to 𝜙 and to 𝜙−1), coker(𝑓)=𝑒′ if and only if coker(𝑓)=𝑒1′.

Preliminary. By (2.7.7), (𝐼′,𝑒′,𝑚′) is both an image and a coimage of 𝑔; in particular Coim(𝑔)=𝑒′.

a) ⟹ b) Taking 𝜑≔1𝐼 gives 𝑘∘𝜑=𝑘=𝑚; as 𝑔∘𝑘=0 (2.6.150), 𝑔∘𝑓=𝑔∘𝑚∘𝑒=𝑔∘𝑘∘𝑒=0. So (by uniqueness) 𝜑=1𝐼 is the morphism of b), and is epic, being an isomorphism.

b) ⟹ a) Since 𝑚=𝑘∘𝜑 and 𝑚 is monic, 𝜑 is monic: if 𝜑∘𝑎=𝜑∘𝑏 then 𝑚∘𝑎=𝑘∘𝜑∘𝑎=𝑘∘𝜑∘𝑏=𝑚∘𝑏, so 𝑎=𝑏. As 𝜑 is also epic (b)), and 𝒞︀ is Abelian hence balanced (2.6.164), 𝜑 is an isomorphism; so 𝑚=𝑘∘𝜑 exhibits 𝑚=ker(𝑔), that is, a).

a) ⟹ c) By (2.7.8) b) (as 𝑒 is epic), coker(𝑓)=coker(𝑚); since 𝑚=ker(𝑔) (a)), coker(𝑚)=coker(ker(𝑔))=Coim(𝑔) (2.7.5), which by the Preliminary equals 𝑒′. So coker(𝑓)=𝑒′, that is, c).

c) ⟹ a) By hypothesis coker(𝑓)=𝑒′; since 𝑔=𝑚′∘𝑒′, (2.7.8) a) (with 𝑚′ in the role of the monomorphism and 𝑒′=coker(𝑓) in the role of 𝑝) gives ker(𝑔)=ker(coker(𝑓)). By (2.7.5), Im(𝑓)=ker(coker(𝑓)), i.e. (as Im(𝑓)=(𝐼,𝑒,𝑚)) 𝑚=ker(coker(𝑓))=ker(𝑔), that is, a).

c) ⟺ d) By duality: apply a) ⟺ b), established above for an arbitrary composable pair in an Abelian category, to 𝑔op:𝑍→𝑌 and 𝑓op:𝑌→𝑋 in 𝒞︀op (Abelian, by (2.7.2)), using the epi-mono factorisation 𝑔op=(𝑒′)op∘(𝑚′)op of 𝑔op (dual to 𝑔=𝑚′∘𝑒′). This gives: (𝑒′)op=ker𝒞︀op(𝑓op) if and only if 𝑓op∘𝑔op=0 and the canonical morphism Im𝒞︀op(𝑔op)→ker𝒞︀op(𝑓op) is epic in 𝒞︀op.

Translating via (2.6.154) (ker𝒞︀op(𝑓op) corresponds to coker(𝑓)), (2.6.169) (Im𝒞︀op(𝑔op) corresponds to Coim(𝑔)), and (2.6.33) (epic in 𝒞︀op corresponds to monic in 𝒞︀), while 𝑓op∘𝑔op=(𝑔∘𝑓)op vanishes if and only if 𝑔∘𝑓=0: this reads exactly as 𝑒′=coker(𝑓) if and only if 𝑔∘𝑓=0 and the canonical morphism coker(𝑓)→Coim(𝑔) is monic, that is, c) ⟺ d).

Independence of choice. a) does not depend on the choice of epi-mono factorisation of 𝑓 (shown above), nor of 𝑔 (as a) only references the fixed kernel 𝑘, not the chosen factorisation of 𝑔); so, by the equivalences just established, neither do b), c), d), each being equivalent to a). ∎

Remark 2.7.10
Conditions b)–d) give further equivalent characterisations of this same notion of exactness at 𝑌, in particular the self-dual form coker(𝑓)=Coim(𝑔).

Proposition 2.7.12 (Finite Biproducts Exist)
Let 𝒞︀ be an Abelian category. Then 𝒞︀ has all finite biproducts (2.6.103).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

By (2.6.105) it suffices to show that binary biproducts exist. Fix 𝑋,𝑌∈𝒞︀; by (2.6.104) there is a unique comparison morphism 𝜓:𝑋∐𝑌→𝑋×𝑌, with 𝜋1,𝜋2 the projections of 𝑋×𝑌 and 𝑠1,𝑠2 the injections of 𝑋∐𝑌, and it suffices to show 𝜓 is an isomorphism.

By (2.7.11), 𝜓 is monic. Since 𝒞︀op is again Abelian (2.7.2), in which 𝑋×𝑌 (with 𝜋1,𝜋2) and 𝑋∐𝑌 (with 𝑠1,𝑠2) play the roles of the coproduct and product respectively (2.6.92), the canonical comparison morphism for 𝑋,𝑌 in 𝒞︀op is exactly 𝜓op, since both are characterised by the same defining equations 𝜋𝑖∘(−)∘𝑠𝑗=𝛿𝑖𝑗. So applying (2.7.11) to 𝒞︀op, 𝜓op is monic in 𝒞︀op, i.e. 𝜓 is epic in 𝒞︀ (2.6.33).

As 𝒞︀ is binormal it is balanced (2.6.164), so 𝜓, being both monic and epic, is an isomorphism. By (2.6.104), a biproduct 𝑋⊕𝑌 therefore exists. ∎

Proposition 2.7.13 (Mor(𝑋,𝑌) is an Abelian Group)
Let 𝒞︀ be an Abelian category and 𝑋,𝑌∈𝒞︀. Then every 𝑓∈Mor(𝑋,𝑌) has an additive inverse; combined with (2.6.121) (applicable since 𝒞︀ has all finite biproducts (2.7.12)), (Mor(𝑋,𝑌),+) is an abelian group.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino. Following [Fre64] Theorem 2.39.

The underlying idea: in a category of modules, the inverse of the unitriangular matrix (10𝑓1) is (10−𝑓1), so the additive inverse of 𝑓 can be read off from the inverse of this matrix; we therefore try to show that the same matrix 𝑀 is an isomorphism in any Abelian category.

Fix 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 and let 𝑀≔(1𝑋0𝑓1𝑌):𝑋⊕𝑌→𝑋⊕𝑌 (2.6.108). By (2.6.122), matrices now multiply in the usual way, entry-by-entry, dot-product style.

𝑀 is monic. For 𝑔=(𝑔1𝑔2):𝑍→𝑋⊕𝑌

𝑀∘𝑔=(1𝑋0𝑓1𝑌)∘(𝑔1𝑔2)=(𝑔1𝑓∘𝑔1+𝑔2)

so 𝑀∘𝑔=0 gives 𝑔1=0 and 𝑓∘𝑔1+𝑔2=0; substituting, 𝑓∘0+𝑔2=0, i.e. 𝑔2=0, so 𝑔=0. By (2.6.166), ker(𝑀)=0, so by (2.7.4), 𝑀 is monic.

𝑀 is epic. Dually, for ℎ=(ℎ1ℎ2):𝑋⊕𝑌→𝑍

ℎ∘𝑀=(ℎ1ℎ2)∘(1𝑋0𝑓1𝑌)=(ℎ1+ℎ2∘𝑓ℎ2)

so ℎ∘𝑀=0 gives ℎ2=0 and ℎ1+ℎ2∘𝑓=0, i.e. ℎ1=0, so ℎ=0. Dually, coker(𝑀)=0, so by (2.7.4), 𝑀 is epic.

𝑀 is invertible. Since 𝒞︀ is Abelian it is binormal (2.7.1), hence balanced (2.6.164), so 𝑀, being monic and epic, is an isomorphism.

Extracting the inverse. Write 𝑀−1=(𝑝𝑞𝑟𝑠). Then

(1𝑋0𝑓1𝑌)∘(𝑝𝑞𝑟𝑠)=(𝑝𝑞𝑓∘𝑝+𝑟𝑓∘𝑞+𝑠)=(1𝑋001𝑌)

gives 𝑝=1𝑋, 𝑞=0, 𝑓∘𝑝+𝑟=0 and 𝑓∘𝑞+𝑠=1𝑌. Substituting 𝑝=1𝑋 into the third equation gives 𝑓+𝑟=0: so 𝑓 has additive inverse −𝑓≔𝑟. ∎
