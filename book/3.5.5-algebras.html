3.5.5 Algebras

Lemma 3.5.29 (Localisation is a Base Change)

Let 𝐴 be a ring, 𝑆 a multiplicative subset, and 𝑀 an 𝐴-module (3.5.11). Then there is a natural isomorphism of 𝑆−1𝐴-modules

𝑆−1𝐴⊗𝐴𝑀⟶∼𝑆−1𝑀,𝑎𝑠⊗𝑚↦𝑎𝑚𝑠.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

The map Φ:𝑆−1𝐴×𝑀→𝑆−1𝑀, Φ(𝑎𝑠,𝑚)≔𝑎𝑚𝑠, is well-defined: if 𝑎𝑠=𝑎′𝑠′ in 𝑆−1𝐴 then 𝑢(𝑎𝑠′−𝑎′𝑠)=0 in 𝐴 for some 𝑢∈𝑆, hence 𝑢(𝑎𝑠′−𝑎′𝑠)𝑚=0 in 𝑀, i.e. 𝑢(𝑠′(𝑎𝑚)−𝑠(𝑎′𝑚))=0, i.e. 𝑎𝑚𝑠=𝑎′𝑚𝑠′ in 𝑆−1𝑀. Φ is clearly bi-additive, and 𝐴-balanced since Φ(𝑎𝑠⋅𝑏,𝑚)=𝑎𝑏𝑚𝑠=Φ(𝑎𝑠,𝑏𝑚) for 𝑏∈𝐴; so by the universal property of the tensor product (3.4.4) it induces a group homomorphism

𝜙:𝑆−1𝐴⊗𝐴𝑀→𝑆−1𝑀,𝑎𝑠⊗𝑚↦𝑎𝑚𝑠,

which is visibly 𝑆−1𝐴-linear.

Conversely define 𝜓:𝑆−1𝑀→𝑆−1𝐴⊗𝐴𝑀 by 𝜓(𝑚𝑠)≔1𝑠⊗𝑚. This is well-defined: if 𝑚𝑠=𝑚′𝑠′ in 𝑆−1𝑀 then 𝑢(𝑠′𝑚−𝑠𝑚′)=0 for some 𝑢∈𝑆, and then, using 1𝑠⊗𝑚=𝑢𝑠′𝑢𝑠𝑠′⊗𝑚=1𝑢𝑠𝑠′⊗𝑢𝑠′𝑚 (moving 𝑢𝑠′∈𝐴 across the tensor),

1𝑠⊗𝑚−1𝑠′⊗𝑚′=1𝑢𝑠𝑠′⊗𝑢𝑠′𝑚−1𝑢𝑠𝑠′⊗𝑢𝑠𝑚′=1𝑢𝑠𝑠′⊗𝑢(𝑠′𝑚−𝑠𝑚′)=0.

Then 𝜙(𝜓(𝑚𝑠))=𝜙(1𝑠⊗𝑚)=𝑚𝑠, so 𝜙∘𝜓=𝟏. Also 𝜓(𝜙(𝑎𝑠⊗𝑚))=𝜓(𝑎𝑚𝑠)=1𝑠⊗𝑎𝑚=𝑎𝑠⊗𝑚 (again moving 𝑎 across the tensor), and since elementary tensors generate 𝑆−1𝐴⊗𝐴𝑀, 𝜓∘𝜙=𝟏. Hence 𝜙 is an isomorphism, with inverse 𝜓. ∎

Corollary 3.5.30 (Localisation of an Algebra is a Base Change)

Let 𝐴 be a ring, 𝑆 a multiplicative subset, and 𝐵 an 𝐴-algebra. Then 𝑆−1𝐵 is naturally an 𝑆−1𝐴-algebra (via 𝑆−1𝐴→𝑆−1𝐵, 𝑎𝑠↦𝑎𝑏0𝑠 where 𝑏0∈𝐵 is the image of 1𝐴, the unique extension of 𝐴→𝐵→𝑆−1𝐵 along 𝑖𝑆 (3.5.5), as 𝑖𝑆(𝑆)⊂(𝑆−1𝐵)⋆), and the isomorphism of 𝑆−1𝐴-modules furnished by (3.5.29) (with 𝑀≔𝐵),

𝑆−1𝐴⊗𝐴𝐵⟶∼𝑆−1𝐵,𝑎𝑠⊗𝑏↦𝑎𝑏𝑠,

is moreover an isomorphism of rings, where 𝑆−1𝐴⊗𝐴𝐵 carries the ring structure of (3.4.34).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Write 𝜙 for the 𝑆−1𝐴-module isomorphism of (3.5.29). It remains only to check 𝜙 is multiplicative on elementary tensors, since it is already additive:

𝜙((𝑎𝑠⊗𝑏)⋅(𝑎′𝑠′⊗𝑏′))=𝜙(𝑎𝑎′𝑠𝑠′⊗𝑏𝑏′)=𝑎𝑎′𝑏𝑏′𝑠𝑠′=𝑎𝑠⋅𝑏⋅𝑎′𝑠′⋅𝑏′=𝜙(𝑎𝑠⊗𝑏)⋅𝜙(𝑎′𝑠′⊗𝑏′).

As every element of 𝑆−1𝐴⊗𝐴𝐵 is a sum of elementary tensors, and 𝜙 is additive, 𝜙 is a ring homomorphism; being also a bijection, it is a ring isomorphism. ∎

Corollary 3.5.31 (Localisation of a Domain is a Base Change to the Fraction Field)

Let 𝐴⊆𝐵 be an inclusion of integral domains, 𝐾≔Frac(𝐴), and 𝑆≔𝐴∖{0}, so that 𝑆−1𝐵 is naturally a subring of Frac(𝐵) (as 𝑆⊂𝐵∖{0} too). Then there is a natural isomorphism of rings

𝐵⊗𝐴𝐾⟶∼𝑆−1𝐵,𝑏⊗𝑥↦𝑏𝑥,

where the right-hand side is computed in Frac(𝐵), regarding 𝐵,𝐾⊆Frac(𝐵).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

By (3.5.30) (applied to the 𝐴-algebra 𝐵, noting 𝑆−1𝐴=𝐾) there is a ring isomorphism 𝐾⊗𝐴𝐵⟶∼𝑆−1𝐵, 𝑎𝑠⊗𝑏↦𝑎𝑏𝑠. Composing with the symmetry isomorphism 𝐵⊗𝐴𝐾≅𝐾⊗𝐴𝐵, 𝑏⊗𝑥↦𝑥⊗𝑏 (3.4.7) (which is also a ring isomorphism, since swapping factors clearly preserves the componentwise multiplication of (3.4.34)), yields a ring isomorphism 𝐵⊗𝐴𝐾⟶∼𝑆−1𝐵 sending 𝑏⊗𝑎𝑠↦𝑎𝑏𝑠; taking 𝑎=1 this is 𝑏⊗𝑥↦𝑥⋅𝑏=𝑏𝑥 for 𝑥=1𝑠, and since 𝑥=𝑎𝑠=𝑎⋅1𝑠 for general 𝑥∈𝐾, bilinearity gives 𝑏⊗𝑥=𝑎𝑏⊗1𝑠↦𝑎𝑏𝑠=(𝑎𝑏)⋅1𝑠=𝑏⋅(𝑎⋅1𝑠)=𝑏𝑥 on every elementary tensor.

Finally 𝑎𝑏𝑠∈𝑆−1𝐵⊆Frac(𝐵), computed there, agrees with the product 𝑏𝑥 computed in Frac(𝐵) (as 𝐵,𝐾⊆Frac(𝐵)). ∎

Remark 3.5.32
(3.5.30) recovers (3.5.41) (relative localisation at a prime) as the special case 𝑆≔𝐴∖𝔭 of a general ring homomorphism 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵: the module localisation 𝑆−1𝐵 (with 𝐵 regarded as an 𝐴-module via 𝜙) is exactly 𝜙(𝑆)−1𝐵=𝐵𝔭, since the two defining equivalence relations on fractions agree termwise.
