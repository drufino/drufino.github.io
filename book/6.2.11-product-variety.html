6.2.11 Product Variety

We construct the product in the category of varieties, but note that the topology of the product does not coincide with the usual product topology.

We first show that the product, if it exists, is unique in concrete sense.

Lemma 6.2.60 (Uniqueness of Product)

Let 𝑋,𝑌 be varieties (resp. affine varieties), then there is at most one topology on 𝑋×𝑌 and local space of functions 𝒪︀𝑋×𝑌 for which the projection maps are regular and the following map

Mor(𝑍,𝑋×𝑌)⟶∼Mor(𝑍,𝑋)×Mor(𝑍,𝑌)𝜙→(𝜋𝑋∘𝜙,𝜋𝑌∘𝜙)

is a well-defined bijection for all varieties (resp. affine varieties) 𝑍, natural in 𝑍.

Proof. Suppose we have two such structures, denote them by 𝑋×𝑌 and 𝑋×𝑌̃, then we have a natural bijection

Mor(𝑍,𝑋×𝑌)→Mor(𝑍,𝑋×𝑌̃)𝜙→𝜙

which is the identity on the underlying set function because 𝜙=𝜙′⟺𝜋𝑋∘𝜙=𝜋𝑋∘𝜙′and𝜋𝑌∘𝜙=𝜋𝑌∘𝜙′. By the dual version of the Yoneda Lemma (2.6.58) the identity map 1:𝑋×𝑌→𝑋×𝑌̃ is a regular isomorphism. Therefore the topology is the same and so are the sheaf of functions. ∎

Proposition 6.2.61

Let 𝑋,𝑌 be varieties, then the categorical product 𝑋×𝑌 exists, and coincides with the set-theoretic product. More precisely

  1. there exists a unique topology on 𝑋×𝑌 and sheaf of functions 𝒪︀𝑋×𝑌 such that the projection maps 𝜋𝑋:𝑋×𝑌→𝑋 and 𝜋𝑌:𝑋×𝑌→𝑌 are regular and there is a natural bijection for all varieties 𝑍

    Mor(𝑍,𝑋×𝑌)⟶∼Mor(𝑍,𝑋)×Mor(𝑍,𝑌)𝜙→(𝜋𝑋∘𝜙,𝜋𝑌∘𝜙)

  2. If 𝑋 and 𝑌 are affine (resp. separated), then so is 𝑋×𝑌, and there is an isomorphism

    (𝒪︀𝑋(𝑋)⊗𝑘𝒪︀𝑌(𝑌))red→∼𝒪︀𝑋×𝑌(𝑋×𝑌)
  3. If 𝑈⊂𝑋 and 𝑉⊂𝑌 are open then so is 𝑈×𝑉, and 𝒪︀𝑈×𝑉=𝒪︀𝑋×𝑌|𝑈×𝑉
  4. If 𝑇⊂𝑋 and 𝑆⊂𝑌 are closed then 𝑇×𝑆 is a closed subset of 𝑋×𝑌

Proof. Suppose 𝑋,𝑌 are both affine. Then 𝑋×𝑌 is given the structure of an affine variety in (6.1.96). This means the following map

Mor(𝑍,𝑋×𝑌)→Mor(𝑍,𝑋)×Mor(𝑍,𝑌)𝜙→(𝜋𝑋∘𝜙,𝜋𝑌∘𝜙)

is bijective when 𝑍 is affine. Consider the case 𝑍=⋃𝑖=1𝑛𝑈𝑖 is a variety. Injectivity is obvious, for surjectivity suppose 𝑓:𝑍→𝑋 and 𝑔:𝑍→𝑌 are regular. Then 𝑓|𝑈𝑖 and 𝑔|𝑈𝑖 are regular, and by the affine case so is 𝑓|𝑈𝑖×𝑔|𝑈𝑖. By (4.3.27) 𝑓×𝑔 is also regular.

Consider the general case 𝑋=⋃𝑖=1𝑛𝑈𝑖 and 𝑌=⋃𝑗=1𝑚𝑉𝑗 are varieties with given affine coverings. Define 𝑊𝑖𝑗≔𝑈𝑖×𝑉𝑗, then by the affine case 𝑊𝑖𝑗 may be given the structure of an (affine) variety with regular set-theoretic projections 𝜋𝑋𝑖𝑗:𝑊𝑖𝑗→𝑈𝑖 and 𝜋𝑌𝑖𝑗:𝑊𝑖𝑗→𝑉𝑗 such that the following map is a bijection

Mor(𝑍,𝑊𝑖𝑗)→∼Mor(𝑍,𝑈𝑖)×Mor(𝑍,𝑉𝑗)

for all varieties 𝑍. Then consider 𝑍=𝑊𝑖𝑗∩𝑊𝑘𝑙=(𝜋𝑋𝑘𝑙)−1(𝑈𝑖∩𝑈𝑘)∩(𝜋𝑌𝑘𝑙)−1(𝑉𝑗∩𝑉𝑘) regarded as an open subvariety of 𝑊𝑘𝑙. With this structure the projection maps 𝜋𝑋:𝑍→𝑈𝑖 and 𝜋𝑌:𝑍→𝑉𝑗 may be shown to be regular by

  • changing the codomain of 𝜋𝑋𝑘𝑙:𝑊𝑘𝑙→𝑈𝑘 (resp. 𝜋𝑌) to 𝑋, (6.2.35)
  • restricting the domain to 𝑍 (6.2.36),
  • changing the codomain to 𝑈𝑖 (resp. 𝑉𝑗) (6.2.35)

Consequently the identity map 𝑊𝑖𝑗∩𝑊𝑘𝑙→𝑊𝑖𝑗 is regular and therefore so is 𝑊𝑖𝑗∩𝑊𝑘𝑙→𝑊𝑖𝑗∩𝑊𝑘𝑙 (6.2.35) when considered as open subvarieties of 𝑊𝑘𝑙 and 𝑊𝑖𝑗 respectively. By symmetry this is a regular isomorphism. Therefore the 𝑊𝑖𝑗 satisfy the criteria of (6.2.37) and there exists a unique structure of a variety on 𝑋×𝑌 for which 𝒪︀𝑋×𝑌|𝑊𝑖𝑗=𝒪︀𝑊𝑖𝑗. By (…) the set-theoretic projections 𝜋𝑋:𝑋×𝑌→𝑋 and 𝜋𝑌:𝑋×𝑌→𝑌 are regular. As before we need to show the following map is bijective

Mor(𝑍,𝑋×𝑌)→Mor(𝑍,𝑋)×Mor(𝑍,𝑌)𝜙→(𝜋𝑋∘𝜙,𝜋𝑌∘𝜙)

with injectivity obvious. Suppose 𝑓:𝑍→𝑋 and 𝑔:𝑍→𝑌 are regular. Define 𝑇𝑖𝑗≔𝑓−1(𝑈𝑖)∩𝑔−1(𝑉𝑗). Then by (6.2.36) 𝑓𝑖𝑗≔𝑓|𝑇𝑖𝑗 and 𝑔𝑖𝑗≔𝑔|𝑇𝑖𝑗 are regular. Therefore by the universal property for 𝑊𝑖𝑗 the maps 𝑓𝑖𝑗×𝑔𝑖𝑗 are regular. Clearly they are compatible so these glue to give a regular map 𝑓×𝑔:𝑍→𝑋×𝑌 (6.2.35) (4.3.27) as required. Further 𝑋×𝑌=⋃𝑖,𝑗𝑊𝑖𝑗 so it is indeed a variety. Uniqueness of the structure follows from (6.2.60).

For 𝑏) we already demonstrated that the affine product is also a product in the category of varieties, so it follows from uniqueness. For separatedness this is an easy exercise.

For 𝑐) we have 𝑈×𝑉=𝜋𝑋−1(𝑈)∩𝜋𝑌−1(𝑉) is open, and the universal property naturally restricts to the subset 𝑈×𝑉. The identity then follows from uniqueness (6.2.60).

For 𝑑) we have 𝑇×𝑆=𝜋𝑋−1(𝑇)∩𝜋𝑌−1(𝑆) is closed. ∎

Proposition 6.2.62
Let 𝑋 be an integral variety and 𝑌 a geometrically integral affine variety (see (6.1.84)). Then 𝑋×𝑌 is an integral variety.
Proof. By definition 𝑋=⋃𝑖=1𝑛𝑈𝑖 for 𝑈𝑖 affine. By (6.2.61) 𝑋×𝑌=⋃𝑖=1𝑛𝑈𝑖×𝑌 and 𝑈𝑖×𝑌 is affine. By (6.1.98) 𝑈𝑖×𝑌 is integral. By (4.1.59) 𝑈𝑖∩𝑈𝑗≠∅ whence (𝑈𝑖×𝑌)∩(𝑈𝑗×𝑌)≠∅. Therefore by (4.1.74) we conclude that 𝑋×𝑌 is integral. ∎
