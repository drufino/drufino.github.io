2.6.12 Functorial Limits

Having fixed a single diagram at a time so far, we now let the diagram vary. By (2.6.96), taking the limit in one variable of a bifunctor is functorial in the other – so a family of limits indexed by 𝒞︀ assembles into a genuine functor 𝒞︀→ℰ︀. Specialising this to the evaluation bifunctor recovers the more familiar statement, (2.6.97): the limit lim𝐽(−) is itself a functor out of (a subcategory of) the functor category 𝒞︀𝐽, so a natural transformation of diagrams induces a morphism of their limits, compatibly with composition and identities – and in particular carries isomorphisms of diagrams to isomorphisms of limits. We also record that limits are invariant under isomorphism of the indexing category, that a limit over a product of two index categories may be computed either simultaneously or iteratively one variable at a time (2.6.99), with the two iterated orders agreeing whenever both make sense (2.6.100), and dual statements for colimits, together with a comparison morphism relating a colimit of limits to a limit of colimits, which need not be an isomorphism in general.

Proposition 2.6.96 (Pointwise Limits are Functorial)

Let 𝐽 be a small category and 𝐹:𝒞︀×𝐽→ℰ︀ a bifunctor.

Suppose that for every 𝑋∈𝒞︀ there is a limit (lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(𝑋,𝑗),{𝜆𝑋,𝑗}𝑗∈𝐽) for 𝐹(𝑋,−).

Then lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(−,𝑗) is functorial in the sense there is a unique morphism making the following diagram commute

for all 𝑓:𝑋→𝑋′ and 𝛽:𝑗→𝑗′.

Therefore this determines a functor lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(−,𝑗):𝒞︀→ℰ︀.

Proof. Recall that (lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(𝑋,𝑗),{𝜆𝑋,𝑗}𝑗∈𝐽) is a terminal object in the category of cones (Δ↓𝐹(𝑋,−)). Suppose 𝑓:𝑋→𝑋′ is a morphism. Since 𝐹 is a bifunctor (2.6.19), for every 𝛽:𝑗→𝑗′ in 𝐽

𝐹(𝑋′,𝛽)∘𝐹(𝑓,1𝑗)∘𝜆𝑋,𝑗=𝐹(𝑓,𝛽)∘𝜆𝑋,𝑗=𝐹(𝑓,1𝑗′)∘𝐹(𝑋,𝛽)∘𝜆𝑋,𝑗=𝐹(𝑓,1𝑗′)∘𝜆𝑋,𝑗′

so (lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(𝑋,𝑗),{𝐹(𝑓,1𝑗)∘𝜆𝑋,𝑗}𝑗∈𝐽) is a cone over 𝐹(𝑋′,−).

Therefore there is a unique morphism ℎ:lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(𝑋,𝑗)→lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(𝑋′,𝑗) into the terminal object of (Δ↓𝐹(𝑋′,−)) with 𝜆𝑋′,𝑗∘ℎ=𝐹(𝑓,1𝑗)∘𝜆𝑋,𝑗 for every 𝑗∈𝐽. For general 𝛽:𝑗→𝑗′, the target cone condition and the bifunctoriality identity above give

𝜆𝑋′,𝑗′∘ℎ=𝐹(𝑋′,𝛽)∘𝜆𝑋′,𝑗∘ℎ=𝐹(𝑋′,𝛽)∘𝐹(𝑓,1𝑗)∘𝜆𝑋,𝑗=𝐹(𝑓,𝛽)∘𝜆𝑋,𝑗

as required.

Writing lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(𝑓,𝑗)≔ℎ, the identity and composition laws needed for functoriality of lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(−,𝑗) follow directly from this uniqueness: for 𝑓=1𝑋, ℎ=1lim𝑗𝐹(𝑋,𝑗) has the defining property of lim𝑗𝐹(1𝑋,𝑗), so equals it by uniqueness; and for 𝑓:𝑋→𝑋′, 𝑓′:𝑋′→𝑋″, the morphism lim𝑗𝐹(𝑓′,𝑗)∘lim𝑗𝐹(𝑓,𝑗) satisfies the same defining property (compatibility with the 𝜆𝑋″,𝑗) as lim𝑗𝐹(𝑓′𝑓,𝑗), so the two agree by uniqueness (this is the same pattern used for image factorisations, see later). ∎

Corollary 2.6.97 (Limits are Functorial in the Diagram)

Let 𝐽 be a small category and 𝒞︀ a category, and let 𝒞︀lim𝐽 be the full subcategory of the functor category 𝒞︀𝐽 (2.6.14) spanned by those diagrams 𝐷:𝐽→𝒞︀ for which lim𝐽𝐷 exists. Then

lim𝐽(−):𝒞︀lim𝐽→𝒞︀,𝐷↦lim𝐽𝐷

is a functor. Consequently, a natural transformation 𝜂:𝐷⇒𝐷′ of 𝒞︀lim𝐽 induces lim𝐽𝜂:lim𝐽𝐷→lim𝐽𝐷′, satisfying the composition and identity laws of a functor; and if 𝜂 is a natural isomorphism, lim𝐽𝜂 is an isomorphism, with inverse lim𝐽(𝜂−1).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

Define the evaluation bifunctor ev:𝒞︀lim𝐽×𝐽→𝒞︀ by ev(𝐷,𝑗)≔𝐷(𝑗) on objects and, for a morphism (𝜂,𝛽):(𝐷,𝑗)→(𝐷′,𝑗′) of 𝒞︀lim𝐽×𝐽 (so 𝜂:𝐷⇒𝐷′ a natural transformation and 𝛽:𝑗→𝑗′ in 𝐽), ev(𝜂,𝛽)≔𝐷′(𝛽)∘𝜂𝑗 (=𝜂𝑗′∘𝐷(𝛽), by naturality of 𝜂 at 𝛽). This preserves identities (ev(1𝐷,1𝑗)=𝐷(1𝑗)∘(1𝐷)𝑗=1𝐷(𝑗)) and composition: for a further morphism (𝜂′,𝛽′):(𝐷′,𝑗′)→(𝐷″,𝑗″),

ev(𝜂′,𝛽′)∘ev(𝜂,𝛽)=𝐷″(𝛽′)∘𝜂𝑗′′∘𝐷′(𝛽)∘𝜂𝑗=𝐷″(𝛽′)∘𝐷″(𝛽)∘𝜂𝑗′∘𝜂𝑗=ev((𝜂′𝜂,𝛽′𝛽)),

using naturality of 𝜂′ at 𝛽 (i.e. 𝜂𝑗′′∘𝐷′(𝛽)=𝐷″(𝛽)∘𝜂𝑗′) and functoriality of 𝐷″; so ev is a bifunctor.

By definition of 𝒞︀lim𝐽, lim𝑗∈𝐽ev(𝐷,𝑗)=lim𝐽𝐷 exists for every 𝐷∈𝒞︀lim𝐽, so (2.6.96) applies to ev (with 𝒞︀lim𝐽 in the role of the first factor), giving that lim𝑗∈𝐽ev(−,𝑗)=lim𝐽(−):𝒞︀lim𝐽→𝒞︀ is a functor, with lim𝐽𝜂 its action on a morphism 𝜂, satisfying the composition and identity laws by functoriality. If 𝜂 is a natural isomorphism, i.e. an isomorphism of 𝒞︀lim𝐽, then lim𝐽𝜂 is an isomorphism with inverse lim𝐽(𝜂−1) by (2.6.42). ∎

Proposition 2.6.98 (Limits are Invariant under Isomorphism of the Indexing Category)
Let 𝜎:𝐽→𝐼 be an isomorphism of categories with inverse 𝜎−1, and 𝐹:𝐼→𝒞︀ a diagram. Then (𝐿,{𝜆𝑖}𝑖∈𝐼) is a limit for 𝐹 if and only if (𝐿,{𝜆𝜎(𝑗)}𝑗∈𝐽) is a limit for 𝐹𝜎:𝐽→𝒞︀.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

Define Σ:(Δ↓𝐹)→(Δ↓𝐹𝜎), (𝐿,{𝜆𝑖}𝑖∈𝐼)↦(𝐿,{𝜆𝜎(𝑗)}𝑗∈𝐽), acting as the identity on the underlying morphism of apexes; this is well-defined since {𝜆𝜎(𝑗)}𝑗∈𝐽 is a cone over 𝐹𝜎 whenever {𝜆𝑖}𝑖∈𝐼 is a cone over 𝐹 (the cone condition for 𝐹𝜎 at a morphism 𝛽 of 𝐽 is exactly the cone condition for 𝐹 at 𝜎(𝛽)), and Σ is manifestly functorial.

Dually define Σ−1:(Δ↓𝐹𝜎)→(Δ↓𝐹), (𝐿,{𝜇𝑗}𝑗∈𝐽)↦(𝐿,{𝜇𝜎−1(𝑖)}𝑖∈𝐼). Since 𝜎−1𝜎=1𝐽 and 𝜎𝜎−1=1𝐼, we have Σ−1Σ=1 and ΣΣ−1=1 on the nose, so Σ is an isomorphism of categories, in particular an equivalence of categories (2.6.25) (with identity natural isomorphisms).

By (2.6.52), (Δ↓𝐹) has a terminal object iff (Δ↓𝐹𝜎) does, and Σ carries a terminal object of (Δ↓𝐹), i.e. a limit of 𝐹, to a terminal object of (Δ↓𝐹𝜎), i.e. a limit of 𝐹𝜎, as required. ∎

Proposition 2.6.99 (Iterated Limits)

Let 𝐼,𝐽 be small categories and 𝐹:𝐼×𝐽→𝒞︀ a bifunctor. Suppose that for every 𝑖∈𝐼 there is a limit (𝐿(𝑖),{𝜆𝑖,𝑗}𝑗∈𝐽) for 𝐹(𝑖,−), so that by (2.6.96) this determines a functor 𝐿:𝐼→𝒞︀.

Then lim(𝑖,𝑗)∈𝐼×𝐽𝐹(𝑖,𝑗) exists if and only if lim𝑖∈𝐼𝐿(𝑖) exists, and in this case

  1. given a limit (𝑀,{𝜈𝑖}𝑖∈𝐼) for 𝐿, the cone (𝑀,{𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜈𝑖}(𝑖,𝑗)∈𝐼×𝐽) is a limit for 𝐹;
  2. conversely, given a limit (𝑁,{𝜋𝑖,𝑗}(𝑖,𝑗)∈𝐼×𝐽) for 𝐹, for each 𝑖∈𝐼 there is a unique morphism 𝜎𝑖:𝑁→𝐿(𝑖) such that 𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜎𝑖=𝜋𝑖,𝑗 for all 𝑗∈𝐽, and (𝑁,{𝜎𝑖}𝑖∈𝐼) is a limit for 𝐿.

Symmetrically, suppose that for every 𝑗∈𝐽 there is a limit (𝑅(𝑗),{𝜇𝑖,𝑗}𝑖∈𝐼) for 𝐹(−,𝑗), so that by (2.6.96) this determines a functor 𝑅:𝐽→𝒞︀. Then lim(𝑖,𝑗)∈𝐼×𝐽𝐹(𝑖,𝑗) exists if and only if lim𝑗∈𝐽𝑅(𝑗) exists, and in this case

  1. given a limit (𝑀′,{𝜉𝑗}𝑗∈𝐽) for 𝑅, the cone (𝑀′,{𝜇𝑖,𝑗∘𝜉𝑗}(𝑖,𝑗)∈𝐼×𝐽) is a limit for 𝐹;
  2. conversely, given a limit (𝑁,{𝜋𝑖,𝑗}(𝑖,𝑗)∈𝐼×𝐽) for 𝐹, for each 𝑗∈𝐽 there is a unique morphism 𝜏𝑗:𝑁→𝑅(𝑗) such that 𝜇𝑖,𝑗∘𝜏𝑗=𝜋𝑖,𝑗 for all 𝑖∈𝐼, and (𝑁,{𝜏𝑗}𝑗∈𝐽) is a limit for 𝑅.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

We construct mutually inverse functors Ξ:(Δ↓𝐿)→(Δ↓𝐹) and Θ:(Δ↓𝐹)→(Δ↓𝐿); as Θ∘Ξ=1, Ξ∘Θ=1, Ξ is in particular an equivalence of categories (2.6.25) (with identity natural isomorphisms), so by (2.6.52), (Δ↓𝐹) has a terminal object iff (Δ↓𝐿) does, and Ξ carries a terminal object of (Δ↓𝐿), i.e. a limit of 𝐿, to a terminal object of (Δ↓𝐹), i.e. a limit of 𝐹, as required.

The functor Ξ. Given a cone (𝑋,{𝜂𝑖}𝑖∈𝐼) over 𝐿, define Ξ(𝑋,{𝜂𝑖})≔(𝑋,{𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜂𝑖}(𝑖,𝑗)∈𝐼×𝐽). This is a cone over 𝐹: for (𝛼,𝛽):(𝑖,𝑗)→(𝑖′,𝑗′), writing (𝛼,𝛽)=(𝛼,1𝑗′)∘(1𝑖,𝛽),

𝐹(𝛼,𝛽)∘𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜂𝑖=𝐹(𝛼,1𝑗′)∘𝐹(1𝑖,𝛽)∘𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜂𝑖=𝐹(𝛼,1𝑗′)∘𝜆𝑖,𝑗′∘𝜂𝑖=𝜆𝑖′,𝑗′∘𝐿(𝛼)∘𝜂𝑖=𝜆𝑖′,𝑗′∘𝜂𝑖′

using in turn that 𝜆𝑖,− is a cone over 𝐹(𝑖,−), the defining property of 𝐿(𝛼) (2.6.96), and that 𝜂 is a cone over 𝐿. On morphisms Ξ is the identity on the underlying morphism of apexes, which is trivially functorial.

The functor Θ. Given a cone (𝑋,{𝜌𝑖,𝑗}(𝑖,𝑗)∈𝐼×𝐽) over 𝐹, for each 𝑖∈𝐼 the family {𝜌𝑖,𝑗}𝑗∈𝐽 is a cone over 𝐹(𝑖,−), so by the universal property of (𝐿(𝑖),{𝜆𝑖,𝑗}𝑗) there is a unique morphism 𝜃𝑖:𝑋→𝐿(𝑖) such that 𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜃𝑖=𝜌𝑖,𝑗 for all 𝑗∈𝐽. Define Θ(𝑋,{𝜌𝑖,𝑗})≔(𝑋,{𝜃𝑖}𝑖∈𝐼).

We check {𝜃𝑖} is a cone over 𝐿. Fix 𝛼:𝑖→𝑖′ in 𝐼; then for every 𝑗′∈𝐽

𝜆𝑖′,𝑗′∘𝐿(𝛼)∘𝜃𝑖=𝐹(𝛼,1𝑗′)∘𝜆𝑖,𝑗′∘𝜃𝑖=𝐹(𝛼,1𝑗′)∘𝜌𝑖,𝑗′=𝜌𝑖′,𝑗′=𝜆𝑖′,𝑗′∘𝜃𝑖′

using the defining property of 𝐿(𝛼), the defining property of 𝜃𝑖, that 𝜌 is a cone over 𝐹 applied to (𝛼,1𝑗′):(𝑖,𝑗′)→(𝑖′,𝑗′), and the defining property of 𝜃𝑖′. As this holds for all 𝑗′∈𝐽, the universal property of (𝐿(𝑖′),{𝜆𝑖′,𝑗′}𝑗′) gives 𝐿(𝛼)∘𝜃𝑖=𝜃𝑖′.

On morphisms Θ again acts as the identity on apexes: if 𝜙:(𝑋,{𝜌𝑖,𝑗})→(𝑋′,{𝜌𝑖,𝑗′}) is a morphism of cones, then 𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜃𝑖′∘𝜙=𝜌𝑖,𝑗′∘𝜙=𝜌𝑖,𝑗=𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜃𝑖 for all 𝑗, so by uniqueness in the universal property of 𝐿(𝑖), 𝜃𝑖′∘𝜙=𝜃𝑖, i.e. 𝜙 is also a morphism of cones (𝑋,{𝜃𝑖})→(𝑋′,{𝜃𝑖′}).

Mutually inverse. Given a cone (𝑋,{𝜂𝑖}) over 𝐿, applying Ξ then Θ recovers, for each 𝑖, the unique morphism 𝑋→𝐿(𝑖) factoring (𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜂𝑖)𝑗 through {𝜆𝑖,𝑗}𝑗; since 𝜂𝑖 already satisfies this by definition, uniqueness gives Θ(Ξ(𝑋,{𝜂𝑖}))=(𝑋,{𝜂𝑖}). Conversely, given a cone (𝑋,{𝜌𝑖,𝑗}) over 𝐹, applying Θ then Ξ gives (𝑋,{𝜆𝑖,𝑗∘𝜃𝑖})=(𝑋,{𝜌𝑖,𝑗}) directly from the defining property of 𝜃𝑖. Both functors act as the identity on the underlying morphism of apexes, so Ξ and Θ are mutually inverse isomorphisms of categories.

Finally, the symmetric statement follows by applying the above to the bifunctor 𝐹𝑇:𝐽×𝐼→𝒞︀, 𝐹𝑇(𝑗,𝑖)≔𝐹(𝑖,𝑗) (swapping the roles of 𝐼,𝐽, with 𝑅 playing the role of 𝐿), and reindexing via (2.6.98) along the swap isomorphism 𝐼×𝐽≅𝐽×𝐼. ∎

Corollary 2.6.100 (Comparison of Iterated Limits)

Let 𝐼,𝐽 be small categories and 𝐹:𝐼×𝐽→𝒞︀ a bifunctor, and suppose (as in (2.6.99)) that 𝐿(𝑖)≔lim𝑗∈𝐽𝐹(𝑖,𝑗) exists for every 𝑖∈𝐼 and 𝑅(𝑗)≔lim𝑖∈𝐼𝐹(𝑖,𝑗) exists for every 𝑗∈𝐽. Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.
