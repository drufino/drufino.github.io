3.37 Algebraic Function Fields

We develop the theory of “function fields”, closely related to the notion of complete (or projective) regular algebraic curves (…).

3.37.1 Relation to the Literature

These treatments fall into two methodological groups:

  • Weil’s répartition/adele method, in the tradition initiated by Chevalley [Che51]: used by Chevalley himself, by Stichtenoth [Sti09], by Lang [Lan82] (all three over a general constant field), and, at its computational core, even by Serre [Ser59] – whose own bibliographic note attributes the proof to Weil, though he additionally erects the formalism of sheaf cohomology around it, identifying 𝐻1(𝑋,𝒪︀𝑋) with the classes of repartitions. Lang’s book, unlike the others in this group, has a different overall aim: a bridge to the classical complex-analytic theory of abelian and theta functions, which the algebraic function-field theory here does not pursue.
  • the classical algebraic-geometric (scheme-free, ringed-space) approach shared by Hartshorne [Har13] and Bump [Bum98], the latter specialised to curves with a dedicated treatment of differentials, residues and ramification.

I have interpolated between these, guided by four principles.

Dedekind domains over bare valuation theory. I use Dedekind domain theory for the key results on function fields and the Weil form of Riemann-Roch, rather than the purely valuation-theoretic development of [Che51] and [Sti09]. In the same spirit, finite extensions of 𝑘((𝑡)) (3.33.4) are analysed directly via the integral closure in a Dedekind domain and a direct power-series computation, rather than via the 𝑃-adic completion machinery both of those references build on; this also yields the result in every characteristic, where [Che51]‘s analogous Puiseux-expansion treatment (Ch. II, §6) is restricted to the tame case, as noted in the remark opening the Extensions of Power Series Rings section. We also use the theory of affine algebras to simplify the proof of some further technical results.

A general constant field, reduced to the algebraically closed case only once. Riemann-Roch and the Residue Theorem are proved over a general constant field, matching Riemann-Roch (though not the Residue Theorem, which is stated only over an algebraically closed field) in [Sti09], but going beyond [Ser59], [Har13], [Bum98] and [Lan82], whose treatments of Riemann-Roch (and, for [Bum98], the Residue Theorem too) all assume an algebraically closed constant field throughout. The general-field Residue Theorem ((3.37.67)) is not proved directly: it is deduced from the algebraically closed case ((3.37.44)) by base extension to 𝑘― and a Galois-descent argument summing conjugate residues, rather than by redoing the local theory over a general field from scratch.

Concrete constructions over general machinery, wherever one suffices. Like [Che51], [Sti09] and [Lan82], I work with Chevalley and Weil’s répartition/adele-style methods directly, and connect this to cohomology only after the fact, rather than building up the general machinery of coherent sheaf cohomology as [Ser59] does around the same underlying répartition computation. Likewise, the Residue Theorem is proved without recourse to local completions, unlike the concrete, differential-level residue theorems of [Ser59] and [Sti09], which both depend on the 𝑃-adic completion; for the rational function field this is done via a short, self-contained elementary proof by partial fractions, rather than as a special case of the general machinery. The Trace Formula for Residues ((3.37.52)) likewise avoids completions, via the same Laurent-series finite-extension lemma above, rather than by appeal to the Complete Discrete Valuation Rings machinery developed alongside it (the Extensions of Power Series Rings and Complete Discrete Valuation Rings sections). And residues are defined directly from the uniformiser derivation without ever constructing the module of Kähler differentials Ω𝐾/𝑘, in contrast to [Ser59] and [Sti09], which both identify their (Weil-differential-based) residues with genuine differentials in this sense.

Characteristic 𝑝 handled uniformly, via universal ℤ-polynomial identities. Invariance of the residue of a differential under change of uniformiser – notoriously tricky in characteristic 𝑝, where the naive derivative-of-a-power argument breaks down whenever 𝑝 divides the relevant exponent – is settled once, universally, over ℤ[𝑎1,𝑎1−1,𝑎2,𝑎3,…] and specialised ((3.37.36)), the same device used by [Ser59] and [Sti09], rather than the Cartier-operator route of [Bum98] unlike [Ser59] and [Sti09], this is done over a general constant field rather than an algebraically closed one. The same device is reused for the Trace Formula for Residues, which holds without any tameness hypothesis ((3.37.50)) by the identical method [Ser59] uses (there attributed to Hasse) for the analogous local identity Res𝑡(𝑓𝑑𝑢)=Res𝑢(Tr(𝑓)𝑑𝑢) underlying its own residue formula; it is exactly this universal-identity device that lets the wildly ramified case go through in both results.

Two further points of departure concern scope and connections to the classical picture, rather than proof technique: the dictionary between the function field and the regular algebraic curve – established even over a non-algebraically closed field, generalising the algebraically-closed-field statement of [Har13] – is proved later, in (6.2.117) of the Algebraic Geometry chapter’s treatment of regular algebraic curves; and, in the classical case of a compact Riemann surface ((3.37.29)), Weil differentials are shown to coincide canonically with classical meromorphic 1-forms, reconnecting the algebraic formalism with the analytic theory it generalises.

3.37.2 Basic Definitions

Proposition 3.37.1

Let 𝐾/𝑘 be a field extension. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝐾/𝑘 is finitely generated with transcendence degree 1.
  2. There exists a transcendental element 𝑥∈𝐾/𝑘 such that 𝐾/𝑘(𝑥) is finite.

In this case we say that 𝐾/𝑘 is an algebraic function field. We typically assume that 𝑘 is perfect (either a number field or a finite field).

Proof. 𝑎)⟹𝑏) By (3.19.153) a transcendence base consists of a single element 𝑥. By assumption 𝐾/𝑘(𝑥) is algebraic and finitely generated, hence also finite (3.19.58).

𝑏)⟹𝑎) Evidently {𝑥} is a transcendence base. By assumption 𝐾/𝑘(𝑥) is finite and therefore certainly finitely generated, whence so is 𝐾/𝑘. ∎

Definition 3.37.2 (Algebraic Function Field)

Let 𝐾/𝑘 be an algebraic function field. Denote by 𝑘̃ the field of constants, the relative algebraic closure of 𝑘 in 𝐾:

𝑘̃≔{𝑥∈𝐾|𝑥algebraic over𝑘}

(a subfield of 𝐾 by (3.19.59)). By (3.19.60), 𝐾/𝑘̃ is relatively algebraically closed. We say that 𝐾/𝑘 is geometrically integral if 𝑘=𝑘̃ (c.f. (3.36.41)).

Definition 3.37.3 (Place of an Algebraic Function Field)

Let 𝐾/𝑘 be an algebraic function field. By a place of 𝐾/𝑘 we refer to a valuation ring 𝒪︀𝑃 such that 𝑘⊊𝒪︀𝑃⊊𝐾. By (3.25.2) this is a local ring with unique maximal ideal 𝔪𝑃.

From (3.3.13) we verify immediately that 𝑘∩𝔪𝑃=(0).

For every 𝑥∈𝐾 we say that 𝑥 is regular at a place 𝑃 if 𝑥∈𝒪︀𝑃.

Proposition 3.37.4
Let 𝐾/𝑘 be a function field and 𝑘⊊𝒪︀𝑃⊊𝐾 a valuation ring. Then 𝑘̃⊂𝒪︀𝑃 and 𝑘̃∩𝔪𝑃=(0)
Proof. By definition 𝑘̃ is the integral closure of 𝑘 in 𝐾, so 𝑘̃⊂𝒪︀𝑃 by (3.25.14). Suppose 0≠𝑥∈𝑘̃ then 𝑥 is invertible in 𝒪︀𝑃 and therefore 𝑥 is not contained in any proper ideal, and in particular 𝑥∉𝔪𝑃. ∎

In order to use the theory of Dedekind domains and affine algebras we establish a connection between valuation rings and a certain “affine” subring.

Proposition 3.37.5

Let 𝐾/𝑘 be an algebraic function field and 𝑓∈𝐾∖𝑘̃ transcendental. Then the integral closure 𝑘[𝑓]― in 𝐾 is a Dedekind domain with quotient field 𝐾 and a finite 𝑘[𝑓]-module. There is a correspondence

{𝔅◃𝑘[𝑓]―}⟷{𝒪︀𝑃⊊𝐾|𝑓∈𝒪︀𝑃}𝔅⟶𝑘[𝑓]―𝔅𝔪𝑃∩𝑘[𝑓]―⟵𝒪︀𝑃

In particular every 𝒪︀𝑃 is a discrete valuation ring, and the residue degree satisfies

[𝑘(𝔪𝑃):𝑘]=[𝑘(𝔅):𝑘]≤[𝐾:𝑘(𝑓)]

Therefore we may associate to each place 𝑃 a discrete valuation

𝑣𝑃:𝐾⟶ℤ∪{∞}

which is zero on 𝑘 and 𝑣𝑃(𝑓)=∞⟺𝑓∉𝒪︀𝑃⟺𝑓−1∈𝔪𝑃.

Proof. By (3.32.2) 𝑘[𝑓]⊂𝐾 is a Dedekind domain and so by (3.35.61) 𝐵≔𝑘[𝑓]―𝐾 is a Dedekind domain and finite 𝑘[𝑓]-module. As 𝒪︀𝑃 is a valuation ring, it is integrally closed (3.25.2) and as it contains 𝑘[𝑓], also contains 𝐵. Then we may deduce this correspondence from (3.32.3) and the inequality from (3.32.15).

This covers every place 𝑃 with 𝑓∈𝒪︀𝑃. For a place 𝑃 with 𝑓∉𝒪︀𝑃 instead, 𝑓−1∈𝔪𝑃⊂𝒪︀𝑃 (3.25.2), and 𝑓−1 is transcendental too (else 𝑓=(𝑓−1)−1 would be algebraic); applying the above with 𝑓−1 in place of 𝑓 shows 𝒪︀𝑃 is again a discrete valuation ring. So every place 𝑃 of 𝐾/𝑘 is a discrete valuation ring, giving the associated valuation 𝑣𝑃. ∎

Corollary 3.37.6
Let 𝐾/𝑘 be an algebraic function field. Then the field of constants 𝑘̃/𝑘 has finite degree.
Proof. For any place 𝑃 we have an embedding 𝑘̃/𝑘↪︎𝑘(𝔪𝑃)/𝑘 which is finite (3.37.5). ∎

We prove a number of facts in analogy with algebraic curves.

Lemma 3.37.7 (Every function has a zero)
Let 𝐾/𝑘 be an algebraic function field and 0≠𝑓∈𝐾. If 𝑓∈𝑘̃ (i.e. 𝑓 is algebraic), then 𝑣𝑃(𝑓)=0 for every place 𝑃, so 𝑓 has no zeros or poles; otherwise (𝑓 transcendental) 𝑓 has between 1 and [𝐾:𝑘(𝑓)] zeros (resp. poles).

Proof. If 𝑓∈𝑘̃, then 𝑓≠0 gives 𝑓∈𝑘̃∖{0}, so 𝑓∉𝔪𝑃 for every place 𝑃 (3.37.4), that is 𝑣𝑃(𝑓)=0.

Otherwise, clearly 𝑘[𝑓] is a polynomial ring with maximal ideal (𝑓). Therefore by (3.25.12) there is a valuation ring 𝒪︀𝑃⊃𝑘[𝑓] such that 𝔪𝑃∩𝑘[𝑓]=(𝑓), which means 𝑓 has at least one zero.

Recall by (3.37.5) that zeros of 𝑓 correspond to the maximal ideals of 𝑘[𝑓]― containing 𝑓. These are precisely the prime divisors of (𝑓) (see (3.32.11)), so the bound follows from (3.32.16).

The statement about poles of 𝑓 follows immediately because these are precisely zeros of 𝑓−1 and 𝑘(𝑓)=𝑘(𝑓−1). ∎

Proposition 3.37.8 (Places of the Rational Function Field)

Let 𝐾=𝑘(𝑥) with 𝑥 transcendental over 𝑘. Then the places of 𝐾/𝑘, that is the valuation rings 𝑘⊊𝒪︀⊊𝐾, are exactly the following pairwise distinct discrete valuation rings

  1. 𝒪︀𝑝(𝑥)≔𝑘[𝑥](𝑝(𝑥)) where 𝑝∈𝑘[𝑋] is an irreducible monic polynomial, with uniformiser 𝑝(𝑥) and residue field 𝑘[𝑋]/(𝑝), and
  2. 𝒪︀∞≔{𝑓(𝑥)𝑔(𝑥)|𝑔≠0,deg𝑓≤deg𝑔}, with uniformiser 𝑥−1 and residue field 𝑘.

If 𝑘 is algebraically closed then the irreducible monic polynomials are the 𝑋−𝜃 with 𝜃∈𝑘, so every place is rational, with uniformiser 𝑥−𝜃 or 𝑥−1.

Proof. Let 𝑡≔𝑥−1, which is again transcendental over 𝑘 with 𝑘(𝑡)=𝐾.

The listed rings are discrete valuation rings. As 𝑘[𝑥]≅𝑘[𝑋] is a PID (3.16.4), 𝒪︀𝑝(𝑥) is a discrete valuation ring (3.31.4), with maximal ideal 𝑝(𝑥)𝒪︀𝑝(𝑥) and residue field 𝑘[𝑥]/(𝑝(𝑥)) (3.6.27).

Next 𝒪︀∞=𝑘[𝑡](𝑡): if 𝑓,𝑔∈𝑘[𝑋] with 𝑔(0)≠0 and 𝑁≔max(deg𝑓,deg𝑔) then 𝑓(𝑡)/𝑔(𝑡)=𝐹(𝑥)/𝐺(𝑥) with 𝐹(𝑥)≔𝑥𝑁𝑓(1/𝑥), 𝐺(𝑥)≔𝑥𝑁𝑔(1/𝑥), deg𝐹≤𝑁=deg𝐺; conversely 𝐹(𝑥)/𝐺(𝑥) with deg𝐹≤deg𝐺≕𝑁 equals 𝑓(𝑡)/𝑔(𝑡) with 𝑓(𝑡)≔𝑡𝑁𝐹(1/𝑡) and 𝑔(𝑡)≔𝑡𝑁𝐺(1/𝑡), where 𝑔(0) is the leading coefficient of 𝐺. So 𝒪︀∞ is a discrete valuation ring with uniformiser 𝑡 and residue field 𝑘[𝑡]/(𝑡)=𝑘, as above.

Every place is listed. Let 𝒪︀ be a place with maximal ideal 𝔪. Suppose firstly that 𝑥∈𝒪︀. Then 𝑘[𝑥]⊂𝒪︀ and the ideal 𝑘[𝑥]∩𝔪≕(𝑝(𝑥)) is principal. There is an injective 𝑘-algebra homomorphism 𝑘[𝑥]/(𝑝(𝑥))↪︎𝑘(𝔪) whence (𝑝(𝑥)) is prime (3.3.58). As 𝑘(𝔪)/𝑘 is algebraic (3.37.5) and 𝑥 is transcendental, we must have 0≠𝑝, which we take monic, and therefore 𝑝 is irreducible (3.17.11). Suppose 𝑔(𝑥)∉(𝑝(𝑥)) then 𝑔(𝑥)∉𝔪⟹𝑔(𝑥)−1∈𝒪︀ (3.25.2). Therefore 𝒪︀𝑝(𝑥)⊂𝒪︀, and as 𝒪︀𝑝(𝑥) is a discrete valuation ring and 𝒪︀≠𝐾 we conclude that they are equal (3.31.5).

Alternatively suppose that 𝑥∉𝒪︀ then by (3.25.2) 𝑡=𝑥−1∈𝔪⊂𝒪︀. Applying the previous part with 𝑡 in place of 𝑥 gives 𝒪︀=𝒪︀𝑝(𝑡) where 𝔪∩𝑘[𝑡]=(𝑝(𝑡)). As 𝑡∈𝔪 we conclude that 𝑝(𝑡)=𝑡, and 𝒪︀=𝑘[𝑡](𝑡)=𝒪︀∞.

Distinctness. 𝑥∈𝒪︀𝑝(𝑥) but 𝑥∉𝒪︀∞, as deg𝑥>deg1. For distinct monic irreducible 𝑝≠𝑝′, 𝑝∉(𝑝′), so 𝑝(𝑥) is a unit of 𝒪︀𝑝′(𝑥) but not of 𝒪︀𝑝(𝑥).

Finally if 𝑘 is algebraically closed the irreducible monic polynomials are the 𝑋−𝜃 (3.19.72), with residue field 𝑘[𝑋]/(𝑋−𝜃)=𝑘. ∎

Lemma 3.37.9 (Restricting a Place is Proper)
Let 𝐿/𝑘′ be an algebraic function field, 𝑃′ a place of 𝐿/𝑘′, 𝑘⊆𝑘′ with 𝑘′/𝑘 algebraic, and 𝐾⊆𝐿 a subfield with trdeg𝑘(𝐾)≥1 (e.g. 𝐾/𝑘 itself an algebraic function field). Then 𝒪︀𝑃′∩𝐾 is a proper valuation ring of 𝐾 (and therefore a place.)

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

𝒪︀𝑃′∩𝐾 is a valuation ring of 𝐾 (3.25.3). Suppose it equals 𝐾, so 𝐾⊂𝒪︀𝑃′. Nonzero elements of 𝐾 are units of 𝒪︀𝑃′, hence not in 𝔪𝑃′, so 𝐾→𝒪︀𝑃′/𝔪𝑃′=𝑘′(𝔪𝑃′) is injective, and trdeg𝑘(𝐾)≤trdeg𝑘(𝑘′(𝔪𝑃′)) (3.19.150). But 𝑘′(𝔪𝑃′)/𝑘′ is algebraic (3.37.5) and 𝑘′/𝑘 is algebraic, so the right-hand side is 0, contradicting trdeg𝑘(𝐾)≥1. ∎

Lemma 3.37.10 (Places Restrict Correctly)
Let 𝐾/𝑘 be an algebraic function field, 𝑃 a place of 𝐾/𝑘, and 𝐿/𝐾 a field extension. Let 𝑘⊆𝑘″⊂𝐿 with 𝑘″/𝑘 algebraic and 𝐿/𝑘″ itself an algebraic function field, and 𝑃′ a place of 𝐿/𝑘″ with 𝒪︀𝑃⊂𝒪︀𝑃′. Then 𝒪︀𝑃′∩𝐾=𝒪︀𝑃; in particular 𝔪𝑃⊂𝔪𝑃′, and 𝜋𝑃∉𝑘″.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

𝒪︀𝑃′∩𝐾 is a valuation ring of 𝐾 containing 𝒪︀𝑃 (3.25.3), and proper (as 𝐾/𝑘 is an algebraic function field, so trdeg𝑘(𝐾)=1≥1) (3.37.9). By (3.31.5) (𝒪︀𝑃 being a DVR, (3.37.5)), a valuation ring of 𝐾 containing the DVR 𝒪︀𝑃 is either 𝒪︀𝑃 itself or all of 𝐾; as 𝒪︀𝑃′∩𝐾≠𝐾, this forces 𝒪︀𝑃′∩𝐾=𝒪︀𝑃.

For 0≠𝑥∈𝔪𝑃 we have 𝑥−1∈𝐾∖𝒪︀𝑃=𝐾∖𝒪︀𝑃′, so 𝑥∈𝔪𝑃′ by (3.25.2): thus 𝔪𝑃⊂𝔪𝑃′. In particular 𝜋𝑃∈𝔪𝑃′ is a non-unit of 𝒪︀𝑃′, so 𝜋𝑃∉𝑘″ (nonzero elements of the field 𝑘″⊂𝒪︀𝑃′ are units). ∎

Definition 3.37.21 (Genus of a Function Field)

Let 𝐾/𝑘 be an algebraic function field with 𝑘̃=𝑘. Define the genus by

𝑔≔max{deg(𝐷)−ℓ(𝐷)+1|𝐷∈Div(𝐾)}

Note as ℓ(0)=1 the genus 𝑔 is non-negative. For any divisor 𝐷∈Div(𝐾) define the index of specialty as follows

𝑖(𝐷)≔ℓ(𝐷)−deg(𝐷)+𝑔−1

which by definition is a non-negative integer. This satisfies a few key properties (3.37.16)

  1. 𝑖(0)=𝑔
  2. 𝑖(𝐷)≥0
  3. 𝐷∼𝐷′⟹𝑖(𝐷)=𝑖(𝐷′)
  4. 𝐷′≥𝐷⟹𝑖(𝐷′)≤𝑖(𝐷)

The Riemann-Roch theorem then takes the following form

ℓ(𝐷)−𝑖(𝐷)=deg(𝐷)−𝑔+1

We say that a divisor 𝐷 is non-special if 𝑖(𝐷)=0 (equivalently ℓ(𝐷)=deg(𝐷)−𝑔+1).

The full Riemann-Roch amounts to an explicit form of 𝑖(𝐷). In order to do this we identify it as the dimension of a certain vector space.

3.37.4 Meromorphic Differentials in the case 𝑘=ℂ

In this section we aim to provide a post-hoc motivation for the introduction of Weil Differentials, by showing that they are canonically isomorphic to the classical space of Meromorphic Differentials in the case 𝑘=ℂ. This relies on some techniques which are specific to the complex-analytic structure of Riemann Surfaces.

Proposition 3.37.28 (Residue Theorem for 𝑘=ℂ)

Let 𝑝 be a point of a Riemann surface and 𝜋 a local holomorphic coordinate centered at 𝑝. For 𝑓 meromorphic near 𝑝, define

Res𝜋(𝑓)≔Res𝑝(𝑓d𝜋)≔12𝜋𝑖∮𝛾𝑓d𝜋

for a small loop 𝛾 around 𝑝 – the classical residue of the 1-form 𝑓d𝜋. Writing 𝑔≔𝑓∘𝜓 for the re-expansion of 𝑓 relative to 𝜋′=𝜓−1(𝜋), and 𝑓=∑𝑛𝑎𝑛𝜋𝑛 for the Laurent series expansion of 𝑓 at 𝑝, then

  1. Res𝜋 is 𝑘-linear in 𝑓;
  2. Res𝜋(𝑓)=0 whenever 𝑓 is holomorphic at 𝑝 (i.e. has no pole there);
  3. Res𝜋(𝑓)=𝑎−1;
  4. Res𝜋′(𝑔⋅𝜓′)=Res𝜋(𝑓);

  5. if 𝑝 ranges over a compact Riemann surface 𝑋 and 𝜔 is a meromorphic 1-form on 𝑋 (so has only finitely many poles), then, writing 𝜔𝜋 for the coefficient of 𝜔 relative to a local coordinate 𝜋 at 𝑝 (so 𝜔=𝜔𝜋d𝜋 near 𝑝, and Res𝜋(𝜔𝜋)=Res𝑝(𝜔) by definition – independent of the choice of 𝜋 at each 𝑝, by d)),

    ∑𝑝Res𝜋(𝜔𝜋)=0

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

a). Immediate from linearity of the contour integral, ∮𝛾(𝛼𝑓+𝛽ℎ)d𝜋=𝛼∮𝛾𝑓d𝜋+𝛽∮𝛾ℎd𝜋 for 𝛼,𝛽∈𝑘.

b). Immediate from c) below: 𝑓 holomorphic at 𝑝 means 𝑎𝑛=0 for every 𝑛<0, in particular 𝑎−1=0, so Res𝜋(𝑓)=𝑎−1=0.

c). Term-by-term integration (valid by uniform convergence of the Laurent series on 𝛾) gives ∮𝛾𝑓d𝜋=∑𝑛𝑎𝑛∮𝛾𝜋𝑛d𝜋, and ∮𝛾𝜋𝑛d𝜋=2𝜋𝑖𝛿𝑛,−1 (𝑛≠−1: exact, 𝜋𝑛d𝜋=d(𝜋𝑛+1𝑛+1), so integrates to 0 around a closed loop; 𝑛=−1: winding number 1 around 𝜋=0), so Res𝜋(𝑓)=12𝜋𝑖∮𝛾𝑓d𝜋=𝑎−1.

d). By the chain rule, 𝑓d𝜋=𝑓(𝜓(𝜋′))𝜓′(𝜋′)d𝜋′=(𝑔⋅𝜓′)d𝜋′: these are the same 1-form 𝜔, written in the two coordinates 𝜋 and 𝜋′. The contour integral ∮𝛾𝜔 depends only on 𝜔 and 𝛾, not on the coordinate used to write it down – reparametrising 𝛾 by 𝜋′ instead of 𝜋 changes nothing about which loop is being integrated over – so Res𝑝(𝜔) computed from either expression gives the same number:

3.37.12 Tate’s Approach to Residues: A Sketch

The route taken above defines Res𝑃 through a uniformiser and the formal derivative, and proves the Residue Theorem (3.37.67) by reducing to 𝐾=𝑘(𝑥) along a finite separable extension. Tate [Tat68] gives a different, intrinsic route, which we only sketch here (nothing later depends on it): the residue is defined from linear algebra alone, and the Residue Theorem is deduced from the finiteness results of the Riemann-Roch theory.

  1. Traces of finite-rank operators. The trace of a finite-rank endomorphism 𝑇 of a 𝑘-vector space 𝑉 is its trace on any finite-dimensional subspace containing 𝑇(𝑉). It is additive, Tr(𝐴𝐵)=Tr(𝐵𝐴) whenever 𝐴:𝑉→𝑊, 𝐵:𝑊→𝑉 and one of them has finite rank, and Tr(𝑇)=Tr(𝑇|𝐻)+Tr(𝑇―) if 𝑇 preserves a finite-dimensional 𝐻, with 𝑇― the induced operator on 𝑉/𝐻. In particular Tr([𝑇,𝐹])=0 for 𝐹 of finite rank and 𝑇 arbitrary.
  2. The local residue. Let 𝜋 be a 𝑘-linear retraction of the local space onto the lattice 𝒪︀𝑃 (in Tate’s setting the completion 𝐾𝑃, with 𝜋 the projection killing the principal parts). For 𝑓 in the local space let 𝑇𝑓≔𝜋∘(𝑓⋅−)|𝒪︀𝑃 be the Toeplitz operator on 𝒪︀𝑃. Then [𝑇𝑓,𝑇𝑔] has finite rank, because (1−𝜋)(𝑓𝒪︀𝑃) lies in the finite-dimensional image of 𝑡−𝑛𝒪︀𝑃/𝒪︀𝑃, and Res𝑃(𝑓d𝑔)≔Tr([𝑇𝑓,𝑇𝑔]). Changing 𝜋 changes each 𝑇𝑓 by a finite-rank operator, which does not change the trace of the commutator. So no uniformiser, derivation or chain rule enters.
  3. Local properties. It is 𝑘-linear, vanishes on differentials regular at 𝑃, has Res𝑃(d𝑡/𝑡)=deg𝑃 (the trace is taken over 𝑘) and is invariant under automorphisms of 𝐾𝑃; it should agree with the trace of the coefficient of 𝑡−1 used above.
  4. Global input. The finiteness underlying Riemann-Roch: 𝐻≔𝐾∩𝒜︀𝐾(0)=𝐿(0) and 𝒜︀𝐾/(𝐾+𝒜︀𝐾(0)) are finite dimensional (3.37.25) and 𝒜︀𝐾(𝐷)/𝒜︀𝐾(0)=⨁𝑃𝑡𝑃−𝑛𝑃𝒪︀𝑃/𝒪︀𝑃 is finite dimensional for every divisor 𝐷=∑𝑃𝑛𝑃𝑃.

  5. The Residue Theorem as a trace identity. Write 𝒜︀≔𝒜︀𝐾 and 𝐿≔𝒜︀(0)=∏𝑃𝒪︀𝑃, and for 𝑓,𝑔∈𝐾 let 𝐷 be a divisor with 𝑓𝒜︀(0),𝑔𝒜︀(0)⊂𝒜︀(𝐷).

    1. For the retraction 𝜋𝐿≔∏𝑃𝜋𝑃:𝒜︀→𝐿 and 𝑇𝑓≔𝜋𝐿∘(𝑓⋅−)|𝐿, the commutator [𝑇𝑓,𝑇𝑔] is the direct sum over 𝑃 of the local commutators, of which only finitely many are nonzero (where 𝑓,𝑔 are regular the operators are multiplication by 𝑓,𝑔 and commute). So Tr([𝑇𝑓,𝑇𝑔])=∑𝑃Res𝑃(𝑓d𝑔).
    2. As in b), Tr([𝑇𝑓,𝑇𝑔]) does not depend on the retraction 𝜋:𝒜︀→𝐿: another retraction changes 𝑇𝑓 by 𝜓∘(1−𝜋)𝑓|𝐿, which has finite rank since (1−𝜋)(𝒜︀(𝐷)) is finite dimensional.
    3. Choose 𝜋 adapted to 𝐾: write 𝐾=𝐻⊕𝑊𝐾 and choose a finite-dimensional 𝐹 with 𝒜︀=𝐿⊕𝑊𝐾⊕𝐹 (possible by d)), and let 𝜋 be the projection onto 𝐿 along 𝑊𝐾⊕𝐹. For ℎ∈𝐻, 𝑓ℎ∈𝐾=𝐻⊕𝑊𝐾 so 𝑇𝑓ℎ∈𝐻: both 𝑇𝑓,𝑇𝑔 preserve 𝐻 and act there as operators on a finite-dimensional space, whose commutator has trace 0.
    4. On 𝐿/𝐻≅(𝐿+𝐾)/𝐾≕Λ⊂𝒜︀/𝐾 the induced operator 𝑇―𝑓 is the Toeplitz operator of multiplication by 𝑓 on 𝒜︀/𝐾 (where 𝑓,𝑔 commute exactly), with respect to the retraction 𝒜︀/𝐾=Λ⊕𝐹―→Λ. Writing 𝜋―=1−𝑝 with 𝑝 the projection onto 𝐹―, and using 𝑓𝑔=𝑔𝑓 on 𝒜︀/𝐾, one finds [𝑇―𝑓,𝑇―𝑔]=−𝜋―𝑓𝑝𝑔+𝜋―𝑔𝑝𝑓 on Λ, whose trace equals Tr𝐹―([𝑝𝑔|𝐹―,𝑝𝑓|𝐹―])=0, a commutator of operators on the finite-dimensional space 𝐹―.

    By (c), (d) and additivity of the trace, Tr([𝑇𝑓,𝑇𝑔])=0, so ∑𝑃Res𝑃(𝑓d𝑔)=0 by (a) and (b).

Nothing here uses the topology of 𝐾𝑃. The construction of b) uses only the subspace 𝒪︀𝑃⊂𝐾, a retraction 𝜋:𝐾→𝒪︀𝑃, and dim𝑘𝑡−𝑛𝒪︀𝑃/𝒪︀𝑃<∞ (as 𝑘(𝔪𝑃)/𝑘 is finite): “lattices” and “continuity” reduce to commensurability of subspaces and to the pole order of 𝑓, so the local residue may be defined on 𝐾 itself, and the global step on the repartitions 𝒜︀𝐾 of this book (components in 𝐾, not in the 𝐾𝑃) together with the finiteness of d). Compared with the proof above, this avoids the reduction to 𝑘(𝑥), the trace formula and separability, and works in any characteristic. What is left to check is that this Res𝑃 agrees with the Laurent-coefficient residue used above (for a place of degree 𝑑, Res𝑃(d𝑡/𝑡)=𝑑 with the trace taken over 𝑘); the global argument of e) is then complete.
