2.7.3 Biproducts

Lemma 2.7.11
Let 𝒞︀ be an Abelian category, 𝑋,𝑌∈𝒞︀, and 𝜓:𝑋∐𝑌→𝑋×𝑌 the canonical comparison morphism (2.6.104), where 𝜋1,𝜋2 are the projections of 𝑋×𝑌 and 𝑠1,𝑠2 are the injections of 𝑋∐𝑌. Then 𝜓 is monic.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

Write 𝑋1≔𝑋, 𝑋2≔𝑌.

Maps out of the coproduct. By (2.6.93) let 𝑞1≔[1,0]:𝑋∐𝑌→𝑋 and 𝑞2≔[0,1]:𝑋∐𝑌→𝑌, i.e. the morphisms induced by 𝑞𝑘∘𝑠𝑖=𝛿𝑘𝑖. Since 𝜋𝑖∘𝜓∘𝑠𝑗=𝛿𝑖𝑗=𝑞𝑖∘𝑠𝑗 for all 𝑗, by the uniqueness clause of (2.6.93), 𝜋𝑖∘𝜓=𝑞𝑖, that is, the following diagram commutes (which also displays the canonical injections 𝑠1,𝑠2)

coker(𝑠1)=𝑞2 and coker(𝑠2)=𝑞1. Indeed 𝑞1𝑠2=𝛿12=0 by construction. By (2.6.153), given ℎ:𝑋∐𝑌→𝑍 with ℎ𝑠2=0, we must produce a unique 𝑔:𝑋→𝑍 making the following diagram commute

By the uniqueness clause of (2.6.93), ℎ=[ℎ𝑠1,ℎ𝑠2] and, for any 𝑔:𝑋→𝑍, 𝑔∘[1,0]=[𝑔,0] (both sides restrict to the same maps along 𝑠1,𝑠2). So since ℎ𝑠2=0, ℎ=[ℎ𝑠1,0]=(ℎ𝑠1)∘[1,0]=(ℎ𝑠1)∘𝑞1. Setting 𝑔≔ℎ𝑠1 then ensures the diagram commutes. Since 𝑞1𝑠1=𝛿11=1𝑋, 𝑞1 is split-epic, hence epic (2.6.35), so 𝑔 is the unique such morphism. Therefore coker(𝑠2)=𝑞1 as required. By the symmetric argument, coker(𝑠1)=𝑞2.

𝑠1=ker(𝑞2) and 𝑠2=ker(𝑞1). Since 𝑞1𝑠1=𝛿11=1𝑋, 𝑠1 is split-monic, hence monic (2.6.35); similarly 𝑠2 is monic (via 𝑞2𝑠2=𝛿22=1𝑌). As 𝒞︀ is Abelian it is binormal (2.7.1), so the monomorphism 𝑠2 is normal (2.6.163), i.e. the kernel of some morphism (2.6.159), whence by (2.6.162), 𝑠2=ker(coker(𝑠2))=ker(𝑞1). Similarly 𝑠1=ker(coker(𝑠1))=ker(𝑞2).

ker(𝜓)=0. Since 𝜋1,𝜋2 jointly determine morphisms into 𝑋×𝑌 (2.6.81), 𝜓∘𝑘=0 iff 𝑞1𝑘=0 and 𝑞2𝑘=0, that is, ker(𝜓) is the intersection, as subobjects of 𝑋∐𝑌, of ker(𝑞1)=𝑠2 and ker(𝑞2)=𝑠1. Informally, ker(𝑞1) is the “𝑌-summand” of 𝑋∐𝑌 (as 𝑞1 is the identity on 𝑋 and kills 𝑌) and ker(𝑞2) is the “𝑋-summand”, so this intersection should vanish just as the coordinate subspaces 𝑋×{0} and {0}×𝑌 meet only at the origin inside 𝑋⊕𝑌; we verify this directly.

Suppose 𝑘:𝐾→𝑋∐𝑌 satisfies 𝜓∘𝑘=0. Then 𝑞1𝑘=𝜋1𝜓𝑘=0 and 𝑞2𝑘=𝜋2𝜓𝑘=0. Since 𝑠2=ker(𝑞1) there is 𝛼:𝐾→𝑌 with 𝑠2𝛼=𝑘, and since 𝑠1=ker(𝑞2) there is 𝛽:𝐾→𝑋 with 𝑠1𝛽=𝑘, so 𝑠2𝛼=𝑠1𝛽, that is, the following diagram commutes

Composing with 𝑞1: 𝑞1𝑠2𝛼=𝑞1𝑠1𝛽 gives 0=𝛽. Therefore 𝑘=𝑠1𝛽=0.

By (2.6.166), ker(𝜓)=0, so by (2.7.4), 𝜓 is monic. ∎

Proposition 2.7.12 (Finite Biproducts Exist)
Let 𝒞︀ be an Abelian category. Then 𝒞︀ has all finite biproducts (2.6.103).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

By (2.6.105) it suffices to show that binary biproducts exist. Fix 𝑋,𝑌∈𝒞︀; by (2.6.104) there is a unique comparison morphism 𝜓:𝑋∐𝑌→𝑋×𝑌, with 𝜋1,𝜋2 the projections of 𝑋×𝑌 and 𝑠1,𝑠2 the injections of 𝑋∐𝑌, and it suffices to show 𝜓 is an isomorphism.

By (2.7.11), 𝜓 is monic. Since 𝒞︀op is again Abelian (2.7.2), in which 𝑋×𝑌 (with 𝜋1,𝜋2) and 𝑋∐𝑌 (with 𝑠1,𝑠2) play the roles of the coproduct and product respectively (2.6.92), the canonical comparison morphism for 𝑋,𝑌 in 𝒞︀op is exactly 𝜓op, since both are characterised by the same defining equations 𝜋𝑖∘(−)∘𝑠𝑗=𝛿𝑖𝑗. So applying (2.7.11) to 𝒞︀op, 𝜓op is monic in 𝒞︀op, i.e. 𝜓 is epic in 𝒞︀ (2.6.33).

As 𝒞︀ is binormal it is balanced (2.6.164), so 𝜓, being both monic and epic, is an isomorphism. By (2.6.104), a biproduct 𝑋⊕𝑌 therefore exists. ∎

Proposition 2.7.13 (Mor(𝑋,𝑌) is an Abelian Group)
Let 𝒞︀ be an Abelian category and 𝑋,𝑌∈𝒞︀. Then every 𝑓∈Mor(𝑋,𝑌) has an additive inverse; combined with (2.6.121) (applicable since 𝒞︀ has all finite biproducts (2.7.12)), (Mor(𝑋,𝑌),+) is an abelian group.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino. Following [Fre64] Theorem 2.39.

The underlying idea: in a category of modules, the inverse of the unitriangular matrix (10𝑓1) is (10−𝑓1), so the additive inverse of 𝑓 can be read off from the inverse of this matrix; we therefore try to show that the same matrix 𝑀 is an isomorphism in any Abelian category.

Fix 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 and let 𝑀≔(1𝑋0𝑓1𝑌):𝑋⊕𝑌→𝑋⊕𝑌 (2.6.108). By (2.6.122), matrices now multiply in the usual way, entry-by-entry, dot-product style.

𝑀 is monic. For 𝑔=(𝑔1𝑔2):𝑍→𝑋⊕𝑌

𝑀∘𝑔=(1𝑋0𝑓1𝑌)∘(𝑔1𝑔2)=(𝑔1𝑓∘𝑔1+𝑔2)

so 𝑀∘𝑔=0 gives 𝑔1=0 and 𝑓∘𝑔1+𝑔2=0; substituting, 𝑓∘0+𝑔2=0, i.e. 𝑔2=0, so 𝑔=0. By (2.6.166), ker(𝑀)=0, so by (2.7.4), 𝑀 is monic.

𝑀 is epic. Dually, for ℎ=(ℎ1ℎ2):𝑋⊕𝑌→𝑍

ℎ∘𝑀=(ℎ1ℎ2)∘(1𝑋0𝑓1𝑌)=(ℎ1+ℎ2∘𝑓ℎ2)

so ℎ∘𝑀=0 gives ℎ2=0 and ℎ1+ℎ2∘𝑓=0, i.e. ℎ1=0, so ℎ=0. Dually, coker(𝑀)=0, so by (2.7.4), 𝑀 is epic.

𝑀 is invertible. Since 𝒞︀ is Abelian it is binormal (2.7.1), hence balanced (2.6.164), so 𝑀, being monic and epic, is an isomorphism.

Extracting the inverse. Write 𝑀−1=(𝑝𝑞𝑟𝑠). Then

(1𝑋0𝑓1𝑌)∘(𝑝𝑞𝑟𝑠)=(𝑝𝑞𝑓∘𝑝+𝑟𝑓∘𝑞+𝑠)=(1𝑋001𝑌)

gives 𝑝=1𝑋, 𝑞=0, 𝑓∘𝑝+𝑟=0 and 𝑓∘𝑞+𝑠=1𝑌. Substituting 𝑝=1𝑋 into the third equation gives 𝑓+𝑟=0: so 𝑓 has additive inverse −𝑓≔𝑟. ∎
