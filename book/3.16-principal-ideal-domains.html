3.16 Principal Ideal Domains

Definition 3.16.1 (Principal Ideal Domain)
An integral domain 𝐴 is a principal ideal domain (or PID) if every ideal 𝔞 is principal.
Proposition 3.16.2
A PID is Noetherian.

Proof. Suppose we have an ascending chain of ideals

𝔞1⊆𝔞2⊆⋯⊆𝔞𝑛⊆⋯

Clearly the union is again an ideal, which is also principal of the form (𝑎). We must have 𝑎∈𝔞𝑛 for some 𝑛, whence it terminates after 𝑛. ∎

Proposition 3.16.3 (Integers form a PID)
ℤ is a PID.

Proof. This follows from the well-ordering principle. Let 𝔞 be an ideal with minimal positive element 𝑑. We claim 𝔞=(𝑑). By the division algorithm (or apply well-ordering principle to the coset 𝑥+(𝑑)), for every 𝑥∈𝔞 there is 0≤𝑟<𝑑 and 𝑞∈ℤ such that

𝑥=𝑞𝑑+𝑟.

Clearly 𝑟∈𝔞, whence by minimality 𝑟=0 as required. ∎

Proposition 3.16.4
Let 𝑘 be a field then the polynomial ring 𝑘[𝑋] is a PID
Proof. Let 𝔞◃𝑘[𝑋] be an ideal. Choose 𝑓∈𝔞 to have minimal degree, then we claim 𝔞=(𝑓). For 𝑔∈𝔞 we have by (3.8.13) 𝑔=𝑞𝑓+𝑟 for deg(𝑟)<deg(𝑓). Clearly 𝑟∈𝔞, so by minimality 𝑟=0 and the result follows. ∎
Lemma 3.16.5 (Co-prime elements in a PID)
Let 𝐴 be a PID, then 𝑥,𝑦 are coprime if and only if they have no non-invertible common divisors.

Proof. First suppose (𝑥,𝑦)=𝐴, then 𝑎𝑥+𝑏𝑦=1 and any common divisor 𝑑 must divide 1 and therefore be invertible.

Conversely suppose (𝑥,𝑦)≠(1), since 𝐴 is a PID it must equal (𝑑) for some non-invertible 𝑑 which is then a common divisor. ∎

Proposition 3.16.6
Let 𝐴 be a principal ideal domain, 𝑀 a free 𝐴-module with rank 𝑚 and 𝑁⊂𝑀 an 𝐴-submodule. Then 𝑁 is free with rank 𝑛≤𝑚.

Proof. The rank of a finite free 𝐴-module is well-defined (3.5.15).

We proceed by induction on 𝑚. If 𝑚=0 then 𝑀=0 and so 𝑁=0 is free of rank 0. Suppose 𝑚≥1 and the result holds for rank 𝑚−1. Let 𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑚 be a basis for 𝑀 and let 𝜋:𝑀→𝐴 be the map ∑𝑖𝑐𝑖𝑥𝑖↦𝑐𝑚. Its kernel is the free submodule 𝑀′ generated by 𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑚−1, of rank 𝑚−1. Then 𝑁′≔𝑁∩𝑀′ is the kernel of 𝜋|𝑁 and a submodule of 𝑀′, hence free of rank 𝑛′≤𝑚−1 by the induction hypothesis. The image 𝜋(𝑁) is an ideal of 𝐴, hence equal to (𝑎) for some 𝑎∈𝐴.

If 𝑎=0 then 𝑁=𝑁′ and we are done. Suppose 𝑎≠0 and choose 𝑦∈𝑁 with 𝜋(𝑦)=𝑎. We claim 𝑁=𝑁′⊕𝐴𝑦 with 𝐴𝑦≅𝐴. For 𝑧∈𝑁 we have 𝜋(𝑧)=𝑐𝑎 for some 𝑐∈𝐴, whence 𝑧−𝑐𝑦∈𝑁′. Moreover 𝜋(𝑐𝑦)=𝑐𝑎 is non-zero for 𝑐≠0, as 𝐴 is an integral domain. Hence 𝑐↦𝑐𝑦 is injective, so 𝐴𝑦≅𝐴, and 𝑁′∩𝐴𝑦=0 as 𝑐𝑦∈𝑁′ forces 𝑐𝑎=0 and so 𝑐=0. Therefore 𝑁 is free with basis a basis of 𝑁′ together with 𝑦, of rank 𝑛′+1≤𝑚. ∎

Proposition 3.16.7
Let 𝐴 be a principal ideal domain and 𝑀 a finitely generated, torsion-free 𝐴-module. Then 𝑀 is a finite free 𝐴-module.

Proof. Let {𝑚1,…,𝑚𝑛} be a generating set for 𝑀, and {𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑟} an arbitrary linearly independent subset of 𝑀. Then by definition we have

𝑣𝑖=∑𝑗=1𝑛𝑎𝑖𝑗𝑚𝑗𝑎𝑖𝑗∈𝐴

Consider the elements of 𝐴𝑛 given by

𝑤𝑖≔(𝑎𝑖1,…,𝑎𝑖𝑛)

Then we can see that {𝑤1,…,𝑤𝑟} is an 𝐴-linearly independent subset of 𝐴𝑛, and therefore a 𝐾-linearly independent subset of 𝐾𝑛 for 𝐾=Frac(𝐴) (a 𝐾-linear dependence gives an 𝐴-linear dependence on clearing denominators). As 𝐾𝑛 has the standard basis of order 𝑛, it has dimension 𝑛 over 𝐾, so 𝑟≤𝑛 (3.3.126).

Therefore we may choose {𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑟} to be a maximal linearly independent subset and define 𝑀′≔⟨𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑟⟩. For each generator 𝑚𝑖, by maximality of the subset there must exist an 𝑎𝑖≠0 such that

𝑎𝑖𝑚𝑖∈𝑀′

Define 𝑎≔𝑎1…𝑎𝑛 then this satisfies 𝑎𝑀⊆𝑀′, since the 𝑚𝑖 generate 𝑀. As 𝐴 is an integral domain we have a series of injections

𝑀↪︎𝑎𝑀⊆𝑀′⟶∼𝐴𝑟

Therefore we are done by (3.16.6). ∎
