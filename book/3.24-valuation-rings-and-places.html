3.24 Valuation Rings and Places

Definition 3.24.1 (Valuation Ring)

A subring 𝐴⊂𝐾 of a field 𝐾 is a valuation ring for 𝐾 if for every 0≠𝑥∈𝐾 either 𝑥∈𝐴 or 𝑥−1∈𝐴 (or both). Such a ring is an integral domain and 𝐾 is necessarily a quotient field for 𝐴.

An integral domain 𝐴 is a valuation ring if it is a valuation ring for its quotient field.

Proposition 3.24.2 (Properties of valuation rings)

Let 𝐴 be a valuation ring and 𝐾 its quotient field then the following properties hold

  1. 𝐴 is local ring
  2. 𝑥∈𝐴∖𝐴⋆⟺𝑥∈𝔪⟺𝑥−1∉𝐴
  3. 𝐴 is integrally closed in 𝐾

Proof. We prove each in turn

  1. By (3.19.3) we need to show that 𝔪≔𝐴∖𝐴⋆ is an additive subgroup of 𝐴. Given 𝑥,𝑦∈𝔪, without loss of generality we may assume that 𝑥,𝑦 are non-zero, and 𝑥𝑦∈𝐴. Then 𝑥+𝑦=𝑦(1+𝑥𝑦). If (𝑥+𝑦)∈𝐴⋆ then 𝑦∈𝐴⋆ a contradiction. Therefore (𝑥+𝑦)∈𝔪 as required.
  2. Note 𝑥−1∉𝐴⟺𝑥∈𝐴∖𝐴⋆⟺𝑥∈𝔪.
  3. Suppose 0≠𝑥∈𝐾 is integral over 𝐴. If 𝑥∈𝐴 we are done. If 𝑥−1∈𝐴 then by (3.23.13) 𝑥∈𝐴[𝑥−1]⊆𝐴 as required.

 ∎

Lemma 3.24.3 (Restriction of a Valuation Ring to a Subfield)
Let 𝐴 be a valuation ring of a field 𝑀 and 𝐿⊂𝑀 a subfield. Then 𝐴∩𝐿 is a valuation ring of 𝐿.
Proof. For 0≠𝑥∈𝐿⊂𝑀, either 𝑥∈𝐴 or 𝑥−1∈𝐴; as 𝑥,𝑥−1∈𝐿 too, the same alternative holds for 𝐴∩𝐿. ∎
Proposition 3.24.4 (Local Homomorphism)

Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be a homomorphism of local rings. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝜙(𝔪𝐴)⊂𝔪𝐵
  2. 𝔪𝐴=𝜙−1(𝔪𝐵)
  3. 𝜙(𝔪𝐴)𝐵⊊𝐵

We say that in this case 𝜙 is a local homomorphism. When 𝜙 is simply inclusion then we say 𝐵 dominates 𝐴.

Proof. a) ⟹ b) 𝜙−1(𝔪𝐵) is a proper ideal containing 𝔪𝐴 and therefore equal by maximality

b) ⟹ a) This is obvious

a) ⟹ c) 𝜙(𝔪𝐴)𝐵⊂𝔪𝐵𝐵=𝔪𝐵⊊𝐵

c) ⟹ a) By (3.3.15) 𝜙(𝔪𝐴)𝐵⊂𝔪𝐵 and trivially 𝜙(𝔪𝐴)⊂𝜙(𝔪𝐴)𝐵. ∎

Corollary 3.24.5

Let (𝐴,𝔪) be a local ring, 𝐾 a field and 𝜙:𝐴→𝐾 a homomorphism. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝜙 is a local ring homomorphism (i.e. 𝔪⊂ker(𝜙))
  2. ker(𝜙)=𝔪
  3. 𝜙 factors through 𝜅(𝔪).
Lemma 3.24.6 (Restriction to a Valuation Ring)
Let 𝐴 be a valuation ring of a field 𝐹, 𝐿⊇𝐹 a field and 𝐵⊆𝐿 a subring containing 𝐴, with a proper ideal 𝔫⊊𝐵 such that 𝔪𝐴⊆𝔫 (equivalently 𝔪𝐴𝐵≠𝐵). Then 𝐵∩𝐹=𝐴 and 𝔫∩𝐹=𝔪𝐴. In particular this applies to any local ring 𝐵⊆𝐿 dominating 𝐴 (3.24.4), with 𝔫=𝔪𝐵.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Clearly 𝐴⊆𝐵∩𝐹. Let 𝑥∈𝐹∖𝐴; then 𝑥≠0 and (𝑥−1)−1=𝑥∉𝐴, so 𝑥−1∈𝔪𝐴⊆𝔫 by (3.24.2). If 𝑥∈𝐵 then 1=𝑥𝑥−1∈𝔫, which is absurd; so 𝑥∉𝐵 and 𝐵∩𝐹=𝐴. Finally 𝔫∩𝐹⊆𝐵∩𝐹=𝐴 is a proper ideal of 𝐴, hence contained in 𝔪𝐴; the reverse inclusion is 𝔪𝐴⊆𝔫. ∎

Lemma 3.24.7 (A Directed Union of Valuation Rings is a Valuation Ring)
Let (𝐾𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 be a directed system of fields with union 𝐾≔⋃𝑖𝐾𝑖, and for each 𝑖 let 𝑅𝑖⊂𝐾𝑖 be a valuation ring, such that 𝑅𝑙 dominates 𝑅𝑖 (3.24.4) whenever 𝐾𝑖⊆𝐾𝑙. Then 𝑅≔⋃𝑖𝑅𝑖 is a valuation ring of 𝐾, with 𝔪𝑅=⋃𝑖𝔪𝑅𝑖.

Proof. 𝑅 is a subring of 𝐾, being a directed union of subrings. For 0≠𝑥∈𝐾, 𝑥∈𝐾𝑖 for some 𝑖 (directed union of fields); as 𝑅𝑖 is a valuation ring of 𝐾𝑖, 𝑥∈𝑅𝑖 or 𝑥−1∈𝑅𝑖, hence 𝑥∈𝑅 or 𝑥−1∈𝑅: 𝑅 is a valuation ring of 𝐾.

𝔪𝑅⊆⋃𝑖𝔪𝑅𝑖: if 𝑥∈𝔪𝑅, then 𝑥∈𝑅𝑖 for some 𝑖 (as 𝑅=⋃𝑖𝑅𝑖); as 𝑥−1∉𝑅⊇𝑅𝑖, in particular 𝑥−1∉𝑅𝑖, so 𝑥∈𝔪𝑅𝑖 (3.24.2) b).

𝔪𝑅⊇⋃𝑖𝔪𝑅𝑖: let 𝑥∈𝔪𝑅𝑖, and suppose for contradiction 𝑥−1∈𝑅𝑙 for some 𝑙. By directedness choose 𝑚 with 𝑖,𝑙≤𝑚, so 𝑥∈𝑅𝑖⊆𝑅𝑚 and 𝑥−1∈𝑅𝑙⊆𝑅𝑚, that is 𝑥∈𝑅𝑚⋆. But 𝑅𝑚 dominates 𝑅𝑖, so 𝔪𝑅𝑖⊆𝔪𝑅𝑚, and 𝑥∈𝔪𝑅𝑖 gives 𝑥∈𝔪𝑅𝑚, contradicting 𝑥∈𝑅𝑚⋆. So 𝑥−1∉𝑅𝑙 for every 𝑙, that is 𝑥−1∉𝑅, giving 𝑥∈𝔪𝑅. ∎

Definition 3.24.8 (Place (Zariski-Samuel 1960 / Lang 1972))

Let 𝐾 be a field. A place of 𝐾 consists of a valuation ring (𝐴,𝔪𝐴) for 𝐾 and a local homomorphism to a field 𝐹

𝜙:𝐴→𝐹

(such that ker(𝜙)=𝔪𝐴)

Furthermore if 𝑥∈𝐾∖𝐴 then we may write 𝜙(𝑥)=∞. Note that the second part of the previous Proposition then may be reinterpreted as saying

𝜙(𝑥)=∞⟺𝜙(𝑥−1)=0∀𝑥∈𝐾

which motivates the alternative definition below.

We say it is a semi-place of 𝐾 if (𝐴,𝔪𝐴) is simply a local ring.

Lemma 3.24.9
Let 𝐴⊂𝐾 be a subring of a field and 𝔞◃𝐴 a proper ideal. Then at least one of 𝔞𝐴[𝑥] or 𝔞𝐴[𝑥−1] is a proper ideal.

Proof. Suppose neither are proper then we can write

1=∑𝑗=0𝑛𝑎𝑗𝑥𝑗1=∑𝑗=0𝑚𝑏𝑗𝑥−𝑗

for 𝑎𝑗,𝑏𝑗∈𝔞. Choose 𝑛,𝑚 to be minimal and assume wlog that 𝑚≤𝑛. Observe that 𝑎0≠1⟹𝑚>0. Multiply the second equation by 𝑥𝑛𝑎𝑛 to find

𝑥𝑛𝑎𝑛(1−𝑏0)=𝑎𝑛𝑏1𝑥𝑛−1+…𝑎𝑛𝑏𝑚𝑥𝑛−𝑚

and multiply the first by (1−𝑏0) to find

(1−𝑏0)=𝑎0(1−𝑏0)+…+𝑎𝑛(1−𝑏0)𝑥𝑛

consequently cancelling the 𝑥𝑛 term and we obtain a relation of smaller degree a contradiction. ∎

Proposition 3.24.10 (Extension to localization)

Let 𝐴 be a ring and 𝜙:𝐴→Ω a homomorphism into a field. Let 𝔭≔ker(𝜙). Then

  • 𝔭 is prime

  • There is a unique extension 𝜙̃ making the diagram commute

Furthermore ker(𝜙̃)=𝔭𝐴𝔭 i.e. 𝜙̃ is local.

Proof. Clearly 𝔭 is prime because 𝜙(𝐴) is an integral domain. We may extend 𝜙 to the ring 𝐴𝔭 in the obvious way. The extension has kernel 𝔭𝐴𝔭, the unique maximal ideal of 𝐴𝔭. ∎
Proposition 3.24.11 (Places as maximal extensions)

Let 𝐴 be a subring of a field 𝐾 and 𝜙:𝐴→Ω a homomorphism into an algebraically closed field. Then

  • For all 𝑥∈𝐾, 𝜙 may be extended to at least one of 𝐴[𝑥] and 𝐴[𝑥−1].
  • There exists a maximal extension 𝜙̃:𝐵→Ω. For any such maximal extension 𝐵 is a valuation ring and 𝜙̃ is local (i.e. ker(𝜙̃)=𝔪𝐵). Furthermore 𝔪𝐵∩𝐴=ker(𝜙).

Proof. We prove each in turn.

  • By (3.24.10) we may assume without loss of generality that 𝐴 is a local ring with unique maximal ideal 𝔪=ker(𝜙). By (3.24.9) we may also suppose that 𝔪𝐴[𝑥] is proper. Then it’s contained in a maximal ideal 𝔅◃𝐴[𝑥]. Furthermore 𝔪=𝔅∩𝐴 by maximality of 𝔪. Let 𝑘=𝐴/𝔪 and 𝐾=𝐴[𝑥]/𝔅, then there is a commutative diagram

    Then 𝐾=𝑘[𝑥―] is a field and by (…) 𝑥― is algebraic over 𝑘. Therefore 𝐾/𝑘 is algebraic and, because Ω is algebraically closed, by (3.18.61) there is an extension to 𝐾, which gives the required extension to 𝐴[𝑥].

  • It’s easy to show that the poset of extensions to subrings of 𝐾 ordered by consistency is chain complete. Therefore by Zorn’s Lemma there is a maximal extension 𝜙̃:𝐵→Ω. By the previous part for any 𝑥∈𝐾 any such maximal element 𝐵 must satisfy either 𝐵[𝑥]=𝐵 or 𝐵[𝑥−1]=𝐵, i.e. 𝐵 is a valuation ring for 𝐾. Consider 𝔅≔ker(𝜙̃) a prime ideal contained in 𝔪𝐵. Then by (3.24.10) may extend 𝜙̃ to 𝐵𝔅 and so by maximality 𝐵=𝐵𝔅. Finally (3.19.6) shows that 𝐵 is a local ring with maximal ideal 𝔅=𝔪𝐵. Clearly ker(𝜙̃)∩𝐴=ker(𝜙), so the final statement follows easily.

 ∎

Corollary 3.24.12
Let 𝐴⊂𝐾 be a subring of a field and 𝔞◃𝐴 a proper ideal. Then there exists a valuation ring (𝐵,𝔪𝐵) such that 𝐴⊂𝐵 and 𝔞⊂𝔪𝐵∩𝐴. In particular if 𝔞=𝔪𝐴 is maximal then 𝔪𝐴=𝔪𝐵∩𝐴.
Proof. By (3.3.37) there is a maximal ideal 𝔪𝐴◃𝐴 containing 𝔞. Let 𝑘=𝐴/𝔪𝐴 and Ω=𝑘―. Then the canonical homomorphism 𝜙:𝐴→Ω has kernel 𝔪𝐴. It has an extension to a valuation ring (𝐵,𝔪𝐵) by (3.24.11), such that 𝔪𝐵∩𝐴=ker(𝜙)=𝔪𝐴. ∎
Proposition 3.24.13 (Alternative Characterisation of Valuation Rings)

Let 𝐾 be a field and 𝐴⊂𝐾 a subring. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝐴 is a valuation ring for 𝐾
  2. 𝐴 is a local ring maximal under the relation “𝐵 dominates 𝐴”
  3. 𝐾=Frac(𝐴) and the principal ideals of 𝐴 are totally ordered
  4. 𝐾=Frac(𝐴) and the ideals of 𝐴 are totally ordered

Proof. Let (𝐴,𝔪𝐴)⪯(𝐵,𝔪𝐵) denote the relation 𝐵 dominates 𝐴.

a) ⟹ b) Suppose that (𝐴,𝔪𝐴)⪯(𝐵,𝔪𝐵) with 𝐵 local and 𝐴 a valuation ring. We claim that 𝐴=𝐵; suppose for a contradiction that 𝑥∈𝐵∖𝐴. Then 𝑥−1∈𝐴⟹𝑥−1∈𝐵⟹𝑥∈𝐵⋆. Therefore 𝐴⊂𝐵∖𝐵⋆=𝔪𝐵. In particular 1∈𝔪𝐵 which is a contradiction as 𝔪𝐵 is proper.

b) ⟹ a) Let (𝐴,𝔪𝐴) be a maximal local ring. By (3.24.12) there exists a valuation ring (𝐵,𝔪𝐵) such that (𝐴,𝔪𝐴)⪯(𝐵,𝔪𝐵). By maximality 𝐴=𝐵 and 𝐴 is a valuation ring.

a) ⟹ c) Evidently 𝐾=Frac(𝐴). Suppose (𝑎)⊄(𝑏), then 𝑎∉(𝑏)⟹𝑏−1𝑎∉𝐴⟹𝑎−1𝑏∈𝐴⟹𝑏∈(𝑎)⟹(𝑏)⊂(𝑎).

c) ⟹ d) Suppose 𝔞⊄𝔟 then there exists 𝑎∈𝔞∖𝔟. In particular for all 𝑏∈𝔟 we have 𝑎∉(𝑏), whence by assumption 𝑏∈(𝑎). Therefore we conclude 𝔟⊂(𝑎)⊂𝔞.

d) ⟹ b) As 𝐴 is a non-zero ring it has a maximal ideal 𝔪𝐴, which by assumption must be the unique maximal ideal. So (𝐴,𝔪𝐴) is a local ring. Suppose that (𝐴,𝔪𝐴)⪯(𝐵,𝔪𝐵) and given 𝑥∈𝐵 we have by assumption 𝑥=𝑎1𝑎2−1 for some 𝑎1,𝑎2∈𝐴. If 𝑎1∈(𝑎2) then 𝑥∈𝐴. Otherwise 𝑎2∈(𝑎1) whence 𝑥−1∈𝐴⟹𝑥−1∈𝐵⋆⟹(3.24.2)𝑥−1∉𝔪𝐵⟹(3.24.4)𝑥−1∉𝔪𝐴⟹𝑥−1∈𝐴⋆⟹𝑥∈𝐴. Consequently 𝐴=𝐵 and b) holds. ∎

Corollary 3.24.14

Let 𝐴⊂𝐾 be a subring of a field then the integral closure of 𝐴 in 𝐾 (denoted 𝐴―) satisfies

𝐴―=⋂𝐴⊂𝑉𝑉

where the intersection is taken over all valuation rings 𝑉 of 𝐾 containing 𝐴.

Alternatively the integral elements over 𝐴 are precisely the elements which are finite at all places of 𝐾, which are finite over 𝐴.

Proof. First if 𝑥∈𝐴― then by (3.23.13) we have 𝑥∈𝐴[𝑥−1]⊆𝑉[𝑥−1]. If 𝑥∉𝑉 then by hypothesis 𝑥−1∈𝑉, whence 𝑥∈𝑉 a contradiction. Therefore 𝑥∈𝑉 as required.

Conversely suppose 𝑥∉𝐴―, then 𝑥∉𝐴[𝑥−1]. That is to say (𝑥−1) is a proper ideal in 𝐴[𝑥−1]. Therefore by (3.24.12) there is a valuation ring (𝑉,𝔪𝑉) such that 𝑥−1∈𝔪𝑉 which implies 𝑥∉𝑉 by (3.24.2). ∎

Definition 3.24.15 (Valuation)

Let 𝐾 be a field and Γ an abelian ordered group. A function 𝑣:𝐾⋆→Γ is said to be a valuation if it satisfies the following properties

  1. 𝑣(𝑎𝑏)=𝑣(𝑎)+𝑣(𝑏) for all 𝑎,𝑏∈𝐾⋆
  2. 𝑣(𝑎+𝑏)≥min(𝑣(𝑎),𝑣(𝑏)) with the convention that 𝑣(0)=∞

Note 𝑣(1)=0. We say that two valuations 𝑣,𝑣′ are equivalent if there is an order-preserving isomorphism 𝜙:𝑣(𝐾⋆)→𝑣′(𝐾⋆) such that 𝜙∘𝑣=𝑣′. Clearly this is an equivalence relation.

If 𝑘⊂𝐾 and 𝑣(𝑘)=0 then we say 𝑣 is a valuation for 𝐾/𝑘.

Lemma 3.24.16 (Strict Ultrametric Inequality)

Let 𝑣:𝐾⋆→Γ be a valuation (3.24.15), extended to 𝐾 by 𝑣(0)≔∞. For 𝑥,𝑦∈𝐾 with 𝑣(𝑥)≠𝑣(𝑦),

𝑣(𝑥+𝑦)=min(𝑣(𝑥),𝑣(𝑦))

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

If 𝑥=0 or 𝑦=0 this is immediate (𝑣(0)=∞ dominates), so assume 𝑥,𝑦∈𝐾⋆, and without loss of generality 𝑣(𝑥)<𝑣(𝑦). By property 2 of (3.24.15), 𝑣(𝑥+𝑦)≥min(𝑣(𝑥),𝑣(𝑦))=𝑣(𝑥). For the reverse inequality, apply property 2 again to 𝑥=(𝑥+𝑦)+(−𝑦):

𝑣(𝑥)≥min(𝑣(𝑥+𝑦),𝑣(−𝑦))

As 𝑣(−1)+𝑣(−1)=𝑣((−1)(−1))=𝑣(1)=0, and Γ is totally ordered (so 𝑣(−1)>0, resp. 𝑣(−1)<0, would force 𝑣(−1)+𝑣(−1)>0, resp. <0), we must have 𝑣(−1)=0; so 𝑣(−𝑦)=𝑣(−1)+𝑣(𝑦)=𝑣(𝑦). As 𝑣(𝑦)>𝑣(𝑥), the displayed inequality 𝑣(𝑥)≥min(𝑣(𝑥+𝑦),𝑣(−𝑦)) forces 𝑣(𝑥)≥𝑣(𝑥+𝑦) (else the minimum would be 𝑣(−𝑦)=𝑣(𝑦)>𝑣(𝑥), a contradiction). Combined with the first inequality, 𝑣(𝑥+𝑦)=𝑣(𝑥)=min(𝑣(𝑥),𝑣(𝑦)). ∎

Proposition 3.24.17 (Correspondence between Valuations Rings and Valuations)

Let 𝐾 be a field with 𝑘⊂𝐾. Then there is a bijection

{𝑘⊂𝐴⊂𝐾|𝐴is a valuation ring}⟷{𝑣:𝐾⋆→Γ|𝑣is a valuation over𝑘}/∼𝐴→𝑣𝐴:𝐾⋆→𝐾⋆/𝐴⋆𝑣−1(Γ≥0)←𝑣:𝐾⋆→Γ

Under this map 𝑣𝐴 is just the quotient map and 𝑥―≤𝑦―⟺𝑦𝑥−1∈𝐴⋆. Furthermore

  1. 𝑣𝐴(𝑥―)≥0⟺𝑥∈𝐴.
  2. 𝑣𝐴(𝑥―)=0⟺𝑥∈𝐴⋆
  3. 𝑣𝐴(𝑥―)>0⟺𝑥∈𝔪𝐴

Proof. Given a valuation ring we may define the abelian group Γ≔𝐾⋆/𝐴⋆ under multiplication with 𝑥―≤𝑦―⟺𝑦𝑥−1∈𝐴. This is easily verified to be a total ordering. Then 𝑣𝐴:𝐾⋆→𝐾⋆/𝐴⋆ is simply the quotient map and we clearly 𝑣𝐴(𝑥―)≥0⟺𝑥∈𝐴⋆. To demonstrate the additive condition we may consider the case 𝑥,𝑦≠0 and suppose wlog that 𝑥𝑦−1∈𝐴

𝑣(𝑥+𝑦)=𝑣((𝑥𝑦−1+1)𝑦)=𝑣(𝑥𝑦−1+1)+𝑣(𝑦)≥𝑣(𝑦)≥min(𝑣(𝑥),𝑣(𝑦))

The case that one of 𝑥,𝑦=0 is trivial. Observe 𝐴=𝑣𝐴−1(Γ≥0)∪{0}. We may therefore construct a one-sided inverse; given a valuation 𝑣:𝐾′→Γ we may define 𝐴𝑣≔𝑣−1(Γ≥0)∪{0}. It’s evident from the two properties of 𝑣 that 𝐴 is an additive and multiplicative subgroup. Further by total ordering we see that 𝐴 is a valuation ring. We only need to demonstrate that the given constructions are mutually inverse, namely 𝑣∼𝑣𝐴𝑣. However this is immediate because the composite 𝑣(𝐾⋆)→𝐾⋆/𝐴𝑣⋆→𝑣𝐴𝑣(𝐾⋆) is an abelian group isomorphism, which preserves the positive segments, and therefore is an order isomorphism. ∎

Corollary 3.24.18 (Valuation Dominance gives a Ring Multiple)

Let 𝑣:𝐾⋆→Γ be a valuation with valuation ring 𝐴 (3.24.17), extended to 𝐾 by 𝑣(0)≔∞. If 𝑥,𝑦∈𝐾 with 𝑦≠0 and 𝑣(𝑥)≥𝑣(𝑦), then 𝑥=𝑎𝑦 for some 𝑎∈𝐴.

If further 𝑣(𝑥)=𝑣(𝑦) then 𝑎∈𝐴⋆.

Proof. If 𝑥=0 take 𝑎=0. Otherwise 𝑥,𝑦∈𝐾⋆ and 𝑣(𝑥𝑦−1)=𝑣(𝑥)−𝑣(𝑦)≥0, so 𝑎≔𝑥𝑦−1∈𝐴 by (3.24.17), and 𝑥=𝑎𝑦. If further 𝑣(𝑥)=𝑣(𝑦) then 𝑣(𝑎)=0, so 𝑎∈𝐴⋆ by (3.24.17). ∎
Proposition 3.24.19 (Bezout Domain)

Let 𝐴 be an integral domain. Then the following are equivalent

  1. Every finitely generated ideal is principal
  2. Every ideal generated by two elements is principal

In this case we say 𝐴 is a Bezout Domain. Further in this case every pair of elements has a greatest common divisor 𝑑 which satisfies 𝑑=𝑎𝑥+𝑏𝑦 for some 𝑥,𝑦∈𝐴.

Proof. By assumption (𝑎,𝑏)=(𝑑). Immediately we have 𝑑∣𝑎 and 𝑑∣𝑏. Similarly 𝑑=𝜆𝑎+𝜇𝑏. Suppose 𝑎=𝑥𝑒 and 𝑏=𝑦𝑒 then 𝑑=(𝜆𝑥+𝜇𝑦)𝑒 whence 𝑒∣𝑑. ∎
Lemma 3.24.20 (Valuation Ring = Local Bezout Domain)

Let 𝐴 be an integral domain. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝐴 is a valuation ring
  2. 𝐴 is a local Bezout domain.

In particular 𝐴 is a Noetherian valuation ring iff it is a local principal ideal domain.

Proof. Suppose 𝐴 is a valuation ring and 𝔞=(𝑥,𝑦). Without loss of generality 𝑥𝑦−1∈𝐴 whence 𝑥∈(𝑦) so 𝔞=(𝑦) as required.

Conversely suppose 𝐴 is a local Bezout domain and let 𝑥=𝑦𝑧∈𝐾. We may divide by the greatest common divisor so that 𝜆𝑦+𝜇𝑧=1. We claim one of 𝜆𝑦 and 𝜇𝑧 is a unit, for suppose not then by (3.19.3) we have 𝜆𝑦,𝜇𝑧∈𝔪⟹1∈𝔪 a contradiction. Therefore one of 𝑦,𝑧 is a unit which implies 𝑥∈𝐴 or 𝑥−1∈𝐴, and 𝐴 is a valuation ring. ∎
