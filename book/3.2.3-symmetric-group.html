3.2.3 Symmetric Group

Definition 3.2.34 (Symmetric Group)

Let 𝑆𝑛 denote the set of permutations (bijections) of {1,…,𝑛}.

Permutations 𝜎,𝜏∈𝑆𝑛 are called disjoint if the supports are disjoint. Note disjoint permutations commute.

Definition 3.2.35 (Cycle)

Let 𝑖1,…,𝑖𝑟∈𝐽𝑛 be an ordered 𝑟-tuple, the permutation which maps

𝑖𝑘→{𝑖𝑘+1𝑘<𝑟𝑖1𝑘=𝑟

is denoted by (𝑖1𝑖2…𝑖𝑟) and called a cycle.

A cycle with two elements (𝑖𝑗) is called a transposition. Finally an adjacent transposition is one of the form (𝑖𝑖+1).

Proposition 3.2.36

Let 𝜎∈𝑆𝑛. Then 𝜎 may be represented by

  1. a product of disjoint cycles, which is unique up to permutation of cycles.
  2. a product of transpositions, the number of which is unique modulo 2

  3. There is a well-defined group homomorphism

    𝜀:𝑆𝑛→{−1,1}

    such that

    𝜀((𝑖𝑗))=−1

    and 𝜀(1)=1.

  4. A cycle 𝜎 of length 𝑟 satisfies 𝜀(𝜎)=(−1)𝑟+1
Proposition 3.2.37 (Adjacent Transpositions Generate the Symmetric Group)
Every 𝜎∈𝑆𝑛 is a product of adjacent transpositions (𝑖𝑖+1), 1≤𝑖<𝑛.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Every permutation is a product of transpositions, so it suffices to write each transposition (𝑖𝑗) with 𝑖<𝑗 as a product of adjacent ones, which we do by induction on 𝑗−𝑖. If 𝑗−𝑖=1 there is nothing to show. If 𝑗−𝑖>1 then

(𝑖𝑗)=(𝑗−1𝑗)(𝑖𝑗−1)(𝑗−1𝑗)

since conjugating (𝑖𝑗−1) by (𝑗−1𝑗) replaces 𝑗−1 with 𝑗, and (𝑖𝑗−1) is a product of adjacent transpositions by induction. ∎
