5.3 Metric Spaces

Definition 5.3.1 (Metric Space)

Let 𝑋 be a set and 𝑑:𝑋×𝑋→ℝ≥0 a function. We say that (𝑋,𝑑) is a metric space if the following properties hold

  1. 𝑑(𝑥,𝑦)=0⟺𝑥=𝑦 for all 𝑥,𝑦∈𝑋
  2. 𝑑(𝑥,𝑦)=𝑑(𝑦,𝑥)
  3. 𝑑(𝑥,𝑧)≤𝑑(𝑥,𝑦)+𝑑(𝑦,𝑧) for all 𝑥,𝑦,𝑧∈𝑋

Given 𝑥∈𝑋 and 𝜀>0 we define the open ball at 𝑥 to be

𝐵(𝑥;𝜀)≔{𝑦∈𝑋|𝑑(𝑥,𝑦)<𝜀}
Proposition 5.3.2

Let (𝑋,𝑑) be a metric space. Then we say that a subset 𝑈⊂𝑋 is open if

∀𝑥∈𝑋∃𝜀>0s.t.𝐵(𝑥;𝜀)⊂𝑈

The open sets form a topology with open balls forming a base.

For each 𝑥∈𝑋 a local base consists of open balls of the form 𝐵(𝑥;𝜀). Further we may also consider the countable local base

ℬ︀𝑥≔{𝐵(𝑥;1𝑛)|𝑛∈ℕ}

In particular every metric space is first countable.

Proposition 5.3.3
A metric space is Hausdorff.
Proposition 5.3.4 (Convergent Sequence)

Let (𝑋,𝑑) be a metric space and (𝑥𝑛) a sequence in 𝑋 and 𝑥∈𝑋. Then the following are equivalent

  1. For every 𝜀>0 there exists 𝑁 such that 𝑛≥𝑁⟹𝑑(𝑥𝑛,𝑥)<𝜀

  2. The function

    ℕ∪{∞}→𝑋𝑛→𝑥𝑛∞→𝑥

    is continuous at ∞ with respect to the topology on ℕ∪{∞} given by open sets of the form {𝑁,𝑁+1,⋯}∪{∞} and arbitrary subsets of ℕ. Note this function is automatically continuous at every 𝑛.

In this case we say that 𝑥𝑛→𝑥 is a convergent sequence.

Proposition 5.3.5 (Continuous at a Point)

Let 𝑓:(𝑋,𝑑)→(𝑌,𝑑′) be a map of metric spaces. Then for 𝑥∈𝑋 and 𝑦≔𝑓(𝑥) the following are equivalent

  1. For all 𝜀>0 there exists 𝛿>0 such that

    𝑑(𝑦,𝑥)<𝛿⟹𝑑′(𝑓(𝑦),𝑓(𝑥))<𝜀
  2. 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 is continuous at 𝑥 in the sense of topological spaces (4.1.25)
  3. For every sequence 𝑥𝑛→𝑥 we have 𝑓(𝑥𝑛)→𝑓(𝑥)
Proposition 5.3.6 (Continuous Criteria)

Let 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 be a map of metric spaces. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑓 is continuous at every 𝑥∈𝑋 (5.3.5)
  2. 𝑓 is continuous in the topological sense (4.1.27)
  3. For every convergent sequence 𝑥𝑛→𝑥 we have 𝑓(𝑥𝑛)→𝑓(𝑥)
Definition 5.3.7 (Product Metric Space)

Let (𝑋𝑖,𝑑𝑖) be metric spaces for 𝑖=1⋯𝑛. Define the metric space on 𝑋1×⋯×𝑋𝑛 by

𝑑((𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑛),(𝑦1,⋯,𝑦𝑛))=max(𝑑1(𝑥1,𝑦1),⋯,𝑑𝑛(𝑥𝑛,𝑦𝑛))

Then the topology induced on (𝑋,𝑑) coincides with the product topology.

5.3.1 Completeness

Definition 5.3.8 (Cauchy Sequence)

Let (𝑋,𝑑) be a metric space. A sequence (𝑥𝑛) in 𝑋 is said to be cauchy if

∀𝜀∃𝑁s.t.𝑛,𝑚≥𝑁⟹𝑑(𝑥𝑛,𝑥𝑚)<𝜀
Definition 5.3.9
A metric space (𝑋,𝑑) is said to be complete if every cauchy sequence is convergent.
Proposition 5.3.10
Let (𝑥𝑛) be a Cauchy sequence in a metric space (𝑋,𝑑) with 𝑥∈𝑋 a cluster point (equivalently, subsequential limit point). Then 𝑥𝑛→𝑥.
Proposition 5.3.11
Let (𝑋,𝑑) be a complete metric space. A closed subset 𝑌⊂𝑋 is complete iff it is closed in 𝑋.
Proposition 5.3.12
Let (𝑋𝑖,𝑑𝑖) be complete metric spaces for 𝑖=1⋯𝑛. Then the product metric space is (𝑋1×⋯×𝑋𝑛,𝑑) is complete.
Corollary 5.3.13

The metric space ℝ𝑘 induced by the product metric is complete.

A subset 𝑋⊂ℝ𝑘 is complete if and only if it is closed.

Proof. This is simply (5.3.12) and (5.3.11). ∎

5.3.2 Compactness

Definition 5.3.14
We say a metric space (𝑋,𝑑) is separable if there exists a countable dense subset 𝑋0.
Proposition 5.3.15
Suppose 𝑋 is a separable metric space. Then it is second countable.

Proof. We may show that the family

ℬ︀≔{𝐵(𝑥0;1𝑛)|𝑥0∈𝑋0,𝑛∈ℕ}

is a countable base. ∎

Proposition 5.3.16 (Precompact)

Let (𝑋,𝑑) be a metric space. The following are equivalent

  1. For all 𝜀>0 there exists a finite covering 𝑋=𝑈1∪⋯∪𝑈𝑛 such that diam(𝑈𝑖)<𝜀
  2. For all 𝜀>0 there exists a finite set of points 𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑛 such that 𝑋=⋃𝑖=1𝑛𝐵(𝑥𝑖;𝜀)
  3. Every sequence has a cauchy subsequence.

In this case we say 𝑋 is precompact or totally bounded.

Proof. 𝑎)⟺𝑏) is straightforward. For 𝑐)⟹𝑏) suppose 𝑋 did not satisfy this property, then we could inductively choose a sequence 𝑥𝑛 such that

𝑥𝑛∈𝑋∖⋃𝑖=1𝑛−1𝐵(𝑥𝑖;𝜀)

Then evidently 𝑑(𝑥𝑛,𝑥𝑛+1)≥𝜀 for all 𝑛 and it can have no cauchy subsequence.

For 𝑏)⟹𝑐) Let (𝑥𝑛) be a sequence. If {𝑥𝑛} is finite then we are done. Otherwise assuming it is infinite, we construct a decreasing sequence of sets

𝐴1⊃⋯⊃𝐴𝑘⊃⋯

such that diam(𝐴𝑘)<1𝑘 and 𝐴𝑘∩{𝑥𝑛} is infinite. For suppose 𝐴1,⋯,𝐴𝑘 are constructed, then evidently 𝐴𝑘 itself satisfies 𝑏) so we may find 𝐴𝑘=⋃𝑖=1𝑚𝐵(𝑥𝑖;1𝑘+1). For at least one 𝑖 we must have 𝐵(𝑥𝑖;1𝑘+1)∩𝐴𝑘∩{𝑥𝑛} is infinite. Therefore we may define 𝐴𝑘+1≔𝐵(𝑥𝑖;1𝑘+1)∩𝐴𝑘. Finally choosing an increasing sequence 𝑛𝑘 such that 𝑥𝑛𝑘∈𝐴𝑘 yields the required cauchy subsequence. ∎

Corollary 5.3.17 (Compactness Criteria)

Let (𝑋,𝑑) be a metric space then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑋 is compact
  2. 𝑋 is countably compact
  3. 𝑋 is sequentially compact
  4. 𝑋 is complete and precompact

Proof. 𝑎),𝑐)⟹𝑏) This is (4.1.106).a)

𝑎),𝑏)⟹𝑐) This is (4.1.106).b)

𝑐)⟹𝑑) By (5.3.10) every cauchy sequence is convergent. Therefore 𝑋 is complete, and precompact by (5.3.16).

𝑑)⟹𝑎) We show that (𝑋,𝑑) is sequentially compact and second countable, and the result follows from (4.1.106).c). Sequential compactness is a consequence of (5.3.16) and (5.3.10). To show that 𝑋 is second countable we may for each 𝑛 find a finite cover

𝑋=⋃𝑖=1𝑁𝑛𝐵(𝑥𝑛𝑖;1𝑛)

We claim that

{𝐵(𝑥𝑛𝑖;1𝑛)}

is a base. For given 𝑦∈𝑋 and a nbhd 𝑈∈𝒰︀𝑦 we have 𝐵(𝑦;𝜀)⊂𝑈 for some 𝜀>0. Choose 𝑛 such that 1𝑛<𝜀2. Then for some 1≤𝑖≤𝑁𝑛, 𝑦∈𝐵(𝑥𝑛𝑖;1𝑛). We may show that 𝐵(𝑥𝑛𝑖;1𝑛)⊂𝐵(𝑦;𝜀)⊂𝑈 which shows that the given collection is a base. ∎

5.3.3 Uniform Continuity

Definition 5.3.18 (Uniformly Continuous)

Let (𝑋,𝑑) and (𝑌,𝑑′) be metric spaces and 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 a function. We say that 𝑓 is uniformly continuous if

∀𝜀>0∃𝛿>0s.t.𝑑(𝑥,𝑦)<𝛿⟹𝑑′(𝑓(𝑥),𝑓(𝑦))<𝜀
Proposition 5.3.19 (Compact & Continuous ⟹ Uniformly Continuous)
Let 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 be a continuous map of metric spaces and suppose 𝑋 is (sequentially) compact. Then 𝑓 is uniformly continuous.

Proof 1.. Fix 𝜀>0 then for every 𝑥∈𝑋 there exists 𝛿𝑥 such that 𝑓(𝐵(𝑥;𝛿𝑥))⊆𝐵(𝑓(𝑥);𝜀). Evidently 𝑋=⋃𝑥𝐵(𝑥;𝛿𝑥2) whence there is a finite subcover associated to 𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑛. Define

𝛿≔min(𝛿𝑥12,⋯,𝛿𝑥𝑛2)

Given 𝑦∈𝑋 then 𝑦∈𝐵(𝑥𝑖;𝛿𝑥𝑖2) for some 𝑖=1⋯𝑛. Furthermore suppose 𝑑(𝑦,𝑧)<𝛿 then

𝑑(𝑥𝑖,𝑧)≤𝑑(𝑥𝑖,𝑦)+𝑑(𝑦,𝑧)<𝛿𝑥𝑖

whence 𝑦,𝑧∈𝐵(𝑥𝑖;𝛿𝑥𝑖). Therefore by construction 𝑑(𝑓(𝑦),𝑓(𝑧))<𝜀, as required. ∎

Proof 2.. By (5.3.17) we know that 𝑋 is sequentially compact. Suppose that 𝑓 is not uniformly continuous, then there exists 𝜀>0 and sequences {𝑥𝑛},{𝑦𝑛} such that 𝑑(𝑥𝑛,𝑦𝑛)<1𝑛 and 𝑑(𝑓(𝑥𝑛),𝑓(𝑦𝑛))>𝜀. By assumption there is a subsequence 𝑛𝑘 such that 𝑥𝑛𝑘→𝑥 and 𝑦𝑛𝑘→𝑦. Evidently

𝑑(𝑥,𝑦)≤𝑑(𝑥,𝑥𝑛𝑘)+𝑑(𝑥𝑛𝑘,𝑦𝑛𝑘)+𝑑(𝑦𝑛𝑘,𝑦)→0

whence 𝑑(𝑥,𝑦)=0 and 𝑥=𝑦. By (…) 𝑓(𝑥𝑛𝑘)→𝑓(𝑥) and 𝑓(𝑦𝑛𝑘)→𝑓(𝑥) which is a contradiction. ∎
