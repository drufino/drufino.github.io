3.4.7 Algebra Tensor Product

Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring and revert to the case of commutative tensor product described in (3.4.4). We show that given two 𝐴-algebras 𝐵, 𝐶 the tensor product naturally forms an algebra. We first prove some preliminary results.

Lemma 3.4.32

Let 𝐵 be a commutative 𝐴-algebra. Then there exists a unique homomorphism of 𝐴-modules

𝐵⊗𝐴𝐵→𝐵

such that

𝑏⊗𝑏′→𝑏𝑏′
Proof. Multiplication is bilinear so the map exists by universal property. ∎
Lemma 3.4.33

Let 𝐵,𝐶 be commutative 𝐴-algebras and define the 𝐴-module 𝑋≔𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶. Then there is a unique homomorphism of 𝐴-modules

𝑚:𝑋⊗𝐴𝑋→𝑋

such that

(𝑏⊗𝑐)⊗(𝑏′⊗𝑐′)→(𝑏𝑏′)⊗(𝑐𝑐′)

Define 1𝑋≔(1⊗1) then it satisfies

  1. 𝑚(1𝑋⊗𝑥)=𝑚(𝑥⊗1𝑋)=𝑥
  2. 𝑚(𝑥⊗𝑦)=𝑚(𝑦⊗𝑥)
  3. 𝑚(𝑥⊗𝑚(𝑦⊗𝑧))=𝑚(𝑚(𝑥⊗𝑦)⊗𝑧)
  4. 𝑚((𝑥+𝑦)⊗𝑧)=𝑚(𝑥⊗𝑧)+𝑚(𝑦⊗𝑧)
  5. 𝑚(𝑥⊗(𝑦+𝑧))=𝑚(𝑥⊗𝑦)+𝑚(𝑥⊗𝑧)

for 𝑥,𝑦,𝑧∈𝑋.

Proof. By associativity and commutativity of the tensor product there is an 𝐴-module isomorphism

𝑋⊗𝐴𝑋≅(𝐵⊗𝐴𝐵)⊗𝐴(𝐶⊗𝐴𝐶)(𝑏⊗𝑐)⊗(𝑏′⊗𝑐′)→(𝑏⊗𝑏′)⊗(𝑐⊗𝑐′)

composing with 𝑚𝐵⊗𝑚𝐶 where 𝑚𝐵:(𝐵⊗𝐴𝐵)→𝐵 and 𝑚𝐶:(𝐶⊗𝐴𝐶)→𝐶 are the maps given in (3.4.32), yields the required map 𝑚.

The properties may be verified on tensors of the form 𝑥𝑖=(𝑏𝑖⊗𝑐𝑖) and 𝑦𝑗=(𝑏𝑗′⊗𝑐𝑗′). The results follow from linearity, since (∑𝑖𝑥𝑖)⊗(∑𝑗𝑦𝑗)=∑𝑖,𝑗(𝑥𝑖⊗𝑦𝑗). ∎

Proposition 3.4.34 (Algebra Tensor Product)

Let 𝐵,𝐶 be commutative 𝐴 algebras. Then the 𝐴-module 𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶 has a unique commutative ring structure with unit 1⊗1 and multiplication which satisfies

⋅:(𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶)×(𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶)→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶(𝑏⊗𝑐)⋅(𝑏′⊗𝑐′)≔(𝑏𝑏′)⊗(𝑐𝑐′)

and may be extended by linearity. Further there is an ring homomorphism

𝑖𝐴:𝐴→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶𝑎→(𝑎⊗1)=(1⊗𝑎)

making 𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶 into an 𝐴-algebra.

Proof. Let 𝑋≔𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶 and consider the map 𝑚:𝑋⊗𝐴𝑋→𝑋 defined in (3.4.33). Define 𝑥⋅𝑦≔𝑚(𝑥⊗𝑦) then the properties of 𝑚 ensure that this satisfies the properties of a ring. ∎
Proposition 3.4.35 (Criteria to be an algebra homomorphism)

Let 𝐵,𝐶,𝑍 be commutative 𝐴-algebras and let 𝜙:𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶→𝑍 be an 𝐴-module homomorphism. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝜙 is an 𝐴-algebra homomorphism
  2. 𝜙((𝑏⊗𝑐)⋅(𝑏′⊗𝑐′))=𝜙(𝑏⊗𝑐)⋅𝜙(𝑏′⊗𝑐′)

Similarly suppose 𝜓:𝑍→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶 is an 𝐴-module homomorphism and 𝑍 is generated as an 𝐴-module by 𝑆⊂𝑍. Then the following equivalent

  1. 𝜓 is an 𝐴-algebra homomorphism
  2. 𝜓(𝑠𝑠′)=𝜓(𝑠)𝜓(𝑠′)
Proof. In each case 𝑎)⟹𝑏) is obvious. For the converse we simply need to show that 𝜙 and 𝜓 are in general multiplicative. This follows by linearity and because every element of the tensor product is a linear combination of elementary tensors. ∎
Proposition 3.4.36 (Functoriality)

Let 𝜙:𝐵→𝐵′ and 𝜓:𝐶→𝐶′ be 𝐴-algebra homomorphisms. The 𝐴-module homomorphism

𝜙⊗𝜓:𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶→𝐵′⊗𝐴𝐶′𝑏⊗𝑐→𝜙(𝑏)⊗𝜙(𝑐)

is an 𝐴-algebra homomorphism.

Proof. The 𝐴-module homomorphism exists by (3.4.4). It is in 𝐴-algebra homomorphism by (3.4.35). ∎
Proposition 3.4.37 (Tensor Product is Coproduct)

Let 𝐵,𝐶 be commutative 𝐴-algebras then there are 𝐴-algebra homomorphisms

𝑖𝐵:𝐵→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶𝑏→𝑏⊗1𝑖𝐶:𝐶→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶𝑐→1⊗𝑐

which are natural in 𝐵 and 𝐶 respectively. In particular 𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶 is both a 𝐶-algebra and a 𝐵-algebra.

Furthermore for 𝑍 an 𝐴-algebra there is a bijection

AlgHom𝐴(𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶,𝑍)≅AlgHom𝐴(𝐵,𝑍)×AlgHom𝐴(𝐶,𝑍)𝜓→(𝜓∘𝑖𝐵,𝜓∘𝑖𝐶)

which is natural in 𝑍.

Proof. The existence of 𝑖𝐵 and 𝑖𝐶 is easily demonstrated using universal property of tensor product. Observe that in general

(𝜓∘𝑖𝐵)(𝑏)=𝜓(𝑏⊗1𝐶)(𝜓∘𝑖𝐶)(𝑐)=𝜓(1𝐵⊗𝑐)

Furthermore the map is clearly well-defined. It’s injective because if 𝜓 and 𝜓′ have the same image then 𝜓(𝑏⊗1)=𝜓′(𝑏⊗1) and 𝜓(1⊗𝑐)=𝜓′(1⊗𝑐) whence 𝜓(𝑏⊗𝑐)=𝜓′(𝑏⊗𝑐). By linearity they are everywhere equal.

To show that the map is a bijection we construct a two-sided inverse. For given 𝐴-algebra homomorphisms 𝑓:𝐵→𝑍 and 𝑔:𝐶→𝑍 then (𝑏,𝑐)→(𝑓(𝑏)𝑔(𝑐)) is 𝐴-bilinear and so corresponds to an 𝐴-module homomorphism

𝜓𝑓,𝑔:𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶→𝑍𝑏⊗𝑐→𝑓(𝑏)𝑔(𝑐)

and by (3.4.35) this is an algebra homomorphism. Clearly 𝜓∘𝑖𝐵=𝑓 and 𝜓∘𝑖𝐶=𝑔. Conversely let 𝜓:𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶→𝑍 be an algebra homomorphism and we define 𝑓(𝑏)≔𝜓(𝑏⊗1𝐶) and 𝑔(𝑐)≔𝜓(1𝐶⊗𝑐). Then 𝑓(𝑏)𝑔(𝑐)=𝜓(𝑏⊗𝑐) which shows that 𝜓𝑓,𝑔 agrees with 𝜓 on elementary tensors, and therefore is identically equal. This completes the demonstration that 𝜓𝑓,𝑔 is a two-sided inverse to the given map.

Naturality in 𝑍 is straightforward. ∎

Proposition 3.4.38 (Extension of Scalars)

Let 𝐵,𝐶 be commutative 𝐴-algebras. Then define the 𝐶-algebra

𝐵(𝐶)≔𝐶⊗𝐴𝐵

There is a natural isomorphism for any 𝐶-algebra 𝑍

AlgHom𝐶(𝐵(𝐶),𝑍)≅AlgHom𝐴(𝐵,𝑍)𝜓→𝜓∘𝑖𝐵

Proof. We consider the commutative diagram obtained from (3.4.37)

An 𝐴-algebra homomorphism 𝜙:𝐵(𝐶)→𝑍 is a 𝐶-algebra homomorphism precisely when 𝜙∘𝑖𝐶=𝑖𝐶𝑍 so that the bottom arrow is well-defined and bijective as required. ∎

Proposition 3.4.39 (Transitivity of Extension of Scalars)

Let 𝐵,𝐶 be commutative 𝐴-algebras and 𝐷 a commutative 𝐶-algebra. Then there is an isomorphism of 𝐷-algebras

𝐷⊗𝐶(𝐶⊗𝐴𝐵)≅𝐷⊗𝐴𝐵𝑑⊗(𝑐⊗𝑏)→(𝑑𝑐)⊗𝑏

Proof. Let 𝑍 be a 𝐷-algebra then there is a natural isomorphism of functors

AlgHom𝐷(𝐷⊗𝐶(𝐶⊗𝐴𝐵),𝑍)≅AlgHom𝐶(𝐶⊗𝐴𝐵,𝑍)≅AlgHom𝐴(𝐵,𝑍)≅AlgHom𝐷(𝐷⊗𝐴𝐵,𝑍)

Consider the case 𝑍=𝐷⊗𝐴𝐵 and the identity map yields the required isomorphism (by the Yoneda Lemma). ∎

Proposition 3.4.41 (Structural Morphisms are Injective)

Let 𝐵,𝐶 be commutative 𝐴-algebras such that the structural morphisms are injective and 𝐴 is a direct factor of 𝐵 and 𝐶 (e.g. if 𝐴=𝑘 is a field). Then the canonical maps (3.4.37)

𝐵→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶𝐶→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶

are injective.

Proof. By (3.4.22) the canonical map

𝐵⊗𝐴𝐴→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶

is injective. By (3.4.40) we have a canonical isomorphism 𝐵≅𝐵⊗𝐴𝐴 and the composite is simply the canonical map 𝐵→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶. ∎

Proposition 3.4.42 (Extension of Ideals under Tensor Product)

Let 𝐵,𝐶 be commutative 𝐴-algebras and 𝔟◃𝐵 and 𝔠◃𝐶 ideals. Then 𝔟𝑒 (resp. 𝔠𝑒) is the image of the 𝐴-module 𝔟⊗𝐴𝐶 (resp. 𝐵⊗𝐴𝔠) in 𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶. Furthermore there is a canonical 𝐴-algebra isomorphism

(𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶)/(𝔟𝑒+𝔠𝑒)≅𝐵/𝔟⊗𝐴𝐶/𝔠
Proof. Let 𝜙:𝐵→𝐵⊗𝐴𝐶 be the structural morphism then for 𝑏∈𝔟 we have 𝑏⊗𝑐=(𝑐⊗1)(𝑏⊗1)=(1⊗𝑐)𝜙(𝑏) whence 𝜙(𝔟)⊆𝔟⊗𝐴𝐶⊆𝔟𝑒. Evidently 𝔟⊗𝐴𝐶 is an ideal and therefore we see 𝔟⊗𝐴𝐶=𝔟𝑒 as required. The isomorphism follows from (3.4.29). ∎
