3.8.1 Multilinear Maps and Determinants

Throughout this section 𝐴 is a commutative ring.

The purpose of this section is to define the determinant, rather than develop any further theory. The approach is a little non-standard as we take an abstract module-theoretic approach as in [Bou98a], but avoid use of the tensor algebra. The connection is that there are natural isomorphisms (the exterior power Λ𝑛(𝑀) represents the functor 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀;−))

𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀;𝑁)→∼Hom(Λ𝑛(𝑀),𝑁)

and

Λ𝑛(𝑀)∨⟶∼Λ𝑛(𝑀∨)

when 𝑀 is finite free. Therefore the exterior product we define really corresponds to the exterior product on (the exterior algebra of) 𝑀∨.

Definition 3.8.1 (Bilinear form)

Let 𝑀,𝑁 be 𝐴-modules then 𝜓:𝑀×𝑁→𝐴 is a bilinear form if it is 𝐴-multilinear.

Denote the set of such bilinear pairings by Bilin𝐴(𝑀,𝑁). It is naturally an 𝐴-module.

Proposition 3.8.2

Let 𝑀,𝑁 be 𝐴-modules then there is a natural bijection

Hom𝐴(𝑀,Hom𝐴(𝑁,𝐴))↔Bilin𝐴(𝑀,𝑁)↔Hom𝐴(𝑁,Hom𝐴(𝑀,𝐴))𝜓𝐿←𝜓𝜓→𝜓𝑅

where

𝜓𝐿(𝑚)(𝑛)=𝜓(𝑚,𝑛)=𝜓𝑅(𝑛)(𝑚)
Definition 3.8.3 (Alternating map)

An 𝐴-multilinear map 𝑓:𝑀𝑛→𝑁 is alternating if

𝑓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)=0

whenever 𝑥𝑖=𝑥𝑖+1 for some 𝑖=1…,𝑛−1.

Denote by 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀,𝑁) the set of such alternating maps, and 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀)≔𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀,𝐴) the set of alternating forms. These are clearly 𝐴-modules.

Proposition 3.8.4 (Functorial Properties)

Let 𝑀 be an 𝐴-module and 𝐿𝑎𝑘(𝑀) the set of 𝑘-alternating forms. Then it is a contravariant functor in 𝑀, that is if 𝑔:𝑀→𝑁 then there is a well-defined map

𝐿𝑎𝑘(𝑔):𝐿𝑎𝑘(𝑁)→𝐿𝑎𝑘(𝑀)𝜓→𝜓∘𝑔(𝑘)

such that 𝐿𝑎𝑘(𝑔∘ℎ)=𝐿𝑎𝑘(ℎ)∘𝐿𝑎𝑘(𝑔)

Lemma 3.8.5

Let 𝑀 be an 𝐴-module and 𝜓∈𝐿𝑎𝑝−1(𝑀;𝐴). Then

𝜓′(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝)≔∑𝑗=1𝑝(−1)𝑗+1𝜓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑗̂,…,𝑥𝑝)𝑥𝑗

is an element of 𝐿𝑎𝑝(𝑀;𝑀).

Proof. It is clear that 𝜓′ is multilinear, so we only need to show it is alternating. Suppose that 𝑥𝑖=𝑥𝑖+1. then the only non-zero terms are where one of 𝑥𝑖 and 𝑥𝑖+1 are omitted, hence

𝜓′(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝)=(−1)𝑖𝜓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑖̂,…,𝑥𝑝)𝑥𝑖+(−1)𝑖+1𝜓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑖+1̂,…,𝑥𝑝)𝑥𝑖+1=0

as required. ∎

Corollary 3.8.6 (Exterior Product of Alternating Forms)

Let 𝑀 be an 𝐴-module, 𝜃∈𝐿𝑎1(𝑀), 𝜓∈𝐿𝑎𝑛−1(𝑀) then the function

𝜃∧𝜓:𝑀𝑛→𝐴(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)→∑𝑗=1𝑛(−1)𝑗+1𝜓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑗̂,…,𝑥𝑛)𝜃(𝑥𝑗)

is an alternating form. We denote this by 𝜃∧𝜓 and refer to it as either the wedge product or exterior product, and determines a bilinear map

∧:𝐿𝑎1(𝑀)×𝐿𝑎𝑛−1(𝑀)→𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀)
Lemma 3.8.7

Let 𝑓:𝑀𝑛→𝑁 be an alternating map then

𝑓(𝑥𝜎(1),…,𝑥𝜎(𝑛))=𝜀(𝜎)𝑓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)

for any permutation 𝜎∈𝑆𝑛.

Furthermore if any of the 𝑥𝑖 are equal then 𝑓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)=0

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Every permutation is a product of adjacent transpositions (3.2.37) and 𝜀 is multiplicative, so for the first part it is enough to treat 𝜎=(𝑖𝑖+1). Fixing all arguments other than the 𝑖-th and (𝑖+1)-th, this follows directly from the definition because

0=𝑓(𝑥+𝑦,𝑥+𝑦)=𝑓(𝑥,𝑥)+𝑓(𝑦,𝑥)+𝑓(𝑥,𝑦)+𝑓(𝑦,𝑦)=𝑓(𝑥,𝑦)+𝑓(𝑦,𝑥)

For the second part suppose 𝑥𝑖=𝑥𝑗 with 𝑖<𝑗. Applying the first part to a permutation which moves the 𝑗-th argument to position 𝑖+1 we obtain 𝑓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)=±𝑓(𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑛) with 𝑦𝑖=𝑥𝑖 and 𝑦𝑖+1=𝑥𝑗=𝑥𝑖. Then 𝑓(𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑛)=0 by definition, so 𝑓(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)=0. ∎

Lemma 3.8.8

Let 𝜓:𝑀𝑝→𝑁 be a multilinear map. Suppose 𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛∈𝑀 and 𝑤1,…,𝑤𝑝∈𝑀 such that

𝑤𝑖=∑𝑗=1𝑛𝑎𝑖𝑗𝑣𝑗∀𝑖=1…𝑝and some𝑎𝑖𝑗∈𝐴

Then

𝜓(𝑤1,…,𝑤𝑝)=∑𝜎𝑎1𝜎(1)…𝑎𝑝𝜎(𝑝)𝜓(𝑣𝜎(1),…,𝑣𝜎(𝑝))

where 𝜎 runs over all functions [1,𝑝]→[1,𝑛].

Lemma 3.8.9

Let 𝜓:𝑀𝑛→𝑁 be an alternating map. Suppose 𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛∈𝑀 and 𝑤1,…,𝑤𝑛∈𝑀 such that

𝑤𝑖=∑𝑗=1𝑛𝑎𝑖𝑗𝑣𝑗∀𝑖=1…𝑛and some𝑎𝑖𝑗∈𝐴

then

𝜓(𝑤1,…,𝑤𝑛)=∑𝜎∈𝑆𝑛𝜀(𝜎)𝑎1𝜎(1)…𝑎𝑛𝜎(𝑛)𝜓(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)

Proof. By (3.8.8)

𝜓(𝑤1,…,𝑤𝑛)=∑𝜎𝑎1𝜎(1)…𝑎𝑛𝜎(𝑛)𝜓(𝑣𝜎(1),…,𝑣𝜎(𝑛))

where 𝜎 ranges over all maps from {1,…,𝑛} to itself. If 𝜎 is not a permutation, then it must not be injective and by (3.8.7) the corresponding term is zero. Therefore we may restrict to the case 𝜎∈𝑆𝑛. By the first part of (3.8.7) the result follows. ∎

Proposition 3.8.10
Let 𝑀 be a finite free 𝐴-module of rank 𝑛. Then the space of alternating forms 𝐿𝑎𝑝(𝑀;𝑁)=0 for any integer 𝑝>𝑛.
Proof. We may use (3.8.8) and observe that at least one basis element must occur at least twice in the expansion, and so is zero by (3.8.7). ∎
Proposition 3.8.11 (Existence and Uniqueness of Determinant Corresponding to a Basis)

Let 𝑀 be a finite free 𝐴-module with basis {𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛}. Then there is a unique alternating form Δ𝑣∈𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀) such that Δ𝑣(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)=1.

Furthermore 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀) is a free module of rank 1 with basis Δ𝑣.

More precisely every Δ∈𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀) satisfies Δ=Δ(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)Δ𝑣. Further every Δ also satisifes Leibniz’ formula

Δ(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)=∑𝜎∈𝑆𝑛𝜀(𝜎)𝑣𝜎(1)∨(𝑥1)…𝑣𝜎(𝑛)∨(𝑥𝑛)Δ(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)

Proof. Let {𝑣1∨,…,𝑣𝑛∨} be the canonical dual basis of 𝑀∨. Then we may use the wedge product (3.8.6) to construct Δ𝑣 inductively

Δ𝑣≔𝑣1∨∧…∧𝑣𝑛∨

(using “right associativity” to ensure this expression is meaningful as the left operand must always be a linear functional). It is then clear that Δ𝑣(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)=1. We may apply (3.8.9) to obtain Leibniz’ formula for both Δ𝑣 and Δ, which shows that Δ=Δ(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)Δ𝑣. ∎

Corollary 3.8.12 (Existence and Uniqueness of Determinant)

For every 𝑛≥1 there is a unique 𝐷𝑛∈𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝐴𝑛) such that 𝐷𝑛(𝑒1,…,𝑒𝑛)=1, given by the Leibniz Formula

𝐷𝑛(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)=∑𝜎∈𝑆𝑛𝜀(𝜎)𝑣1𝜎(1)…𝑣𝑛𝜎(𝑛).

Further 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝐴𝑛) is a free 𝐴-module of rank 1 generated by 𝐷𝑛. Explicitly every Δ∈𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝐴𝑛) satisfies

Δ=Δ(𝑒1,…,𝑒𝑛)𝐷𝑛

Further 𝐷𝑛 satisfies the Laplace Expansion Formula that is for all 𝑖,𝑗=1…𝑛 we have the relationship

𝐷𝑛(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)=∑𝑗=1𝑛(−1)𝑖+𝑗𝑣𝑗𝑖𝐷𝑛−1(𝜋𝑖(𝑣1),…,𝜋𝑖(𝑣𝑗)̂,…,𝜋𝑖(𝑣𝑛))

where 𝜋𝑖:𝐴𝑛→𝐴𝑛−1 drops the 𝑖-th element.

Proof. The first two statements follow from (3.8.11) in the case 𝑀=𝐴𝑛.

The function (−1)𝑖+1𝐷𝑛−1(𝜋𝑖(𝑣1),…,𝜋𝑖(𝑣𝑛−1)) is evidently multilinear and alternating, that is an element of 𝐿𝑛−1(𝐴𝑛). Forming the wedge product with 𝑒𝑖∨ we obtain an element Δ of 𝐿𝑛(𝐴𝑛), which has the form given by the Laplace Expansion. Furthermore

Δ(𝑒1,…,𝑒𝑛)=(−1)𝑖+1𝑒𝑖∨(𝑒𝑖)(−1)𝑖+1𝐷𝑛−1(𝜋𝑖(𝑒𝑛),…,𝜋𝑖(𝑒𝑖)̂,…,𝜋𝑖(𝑒𝑛))=1.

whence by (3.8.11) Δ=𝐷𝑛 as required. ∎

Proposition 3.8.13 (Rank is Unique)
Let 𝑀 be a finite free 𝐴-module. Then the rank is unique.
Proof. This follows from (3.8.10) and (3.8.11). Explicitly if 𝑀 has bases of order 𝑝 and 𝑞 with 𝑝<𝑞, then we deduce 𝐿𝑎𝑞(𝑀) is both zero and non-zero a contradiction. ∎
Definition 3.8.14 (Determinant of a Module)

Let 𝑀 be a finite free 𝐴-module of rank 𝑛, then we say a generator for 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀) is a determinant and Δ𝑣 is the determinant corresponding to the basis 𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛.

The determinant for 𝐴𝑛 corresponding to the standard basis 𝑒1,…,𝑒𝑛 is called the standard determinant for 𝐴𝑛, and denoted by 𝐷𝑛.

Corollary 3.8.15 (Determinant of an endomorphism)

Let 𝑀 be a finite free 𝐴-module of rank 𝑛 and 𝑓∈End𝐴(𝑀) an endomorphism. Then the corresponding linear map

𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑓):𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀)→𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀)

satisfies

𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑓)(𝜓)=det(𝑓)𝜓

for a unique det(𝑓)∈𝐴, which we call the determinant of 𝑓. We have the following properties

det(𝑓∘𝑔)=det(𝑓)det(𝑔)det(1𝑀)=1𝐴det(𝑓)=Δ𝑣(𝑓(𝑣1),…,𝑓(𝑣𝑛))

for Δ𝑣 any generator for 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀) corresponding to a basis 𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛.

Proof. Let Δ be a generator then 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑓)(Δ)=Δ𝑣(𝑓(𝑣1),…,𝑓(𝑣𝑛))Δ by (3.8.11). Clearly det(𝑓)≔Δ𝑣(𝑓(𝑣1),…,𝑓(𝑣𝑛)) then satisfies the equation for all such 𝜓=𝑎Δ. It’s unique because 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀) is a free module, and the properties follow from uniqueness. ∎

We may use this to define the determinant of a matrix

Definition 3.8.16 (Determinant of a Matrix)
Let 𝐸∈Mat𝑛×𝑛(𝐴) then we define the determinant of 𝐸 to be simply det(𝐸̂).
Proposition 3.8.17 (Expressions for Matrix Determinant)

Let 𝐸∈Mat𝑛×𝑛(𝐴) and denote by 𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛 the column vectors of 𝐸. Then

det(𝐸)=𝐷𝑛(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛)

where 𝐷𝑛 is the standard determinant. Further there is the Leibniz Formula

det(𝐸)=∑𝜎∈𝑆𝑛𝜀(𝜎)∏𝑖=1𝑛𝐸𝑖𝜎(𝑖)

and Laplace Expansion Formula

det(𝐸)=∑𝑗=1𝑛(−1)𝑖+𝑗𝐸𝑖𝑗det(𝐸(𝑖𝑗))

where 𝐸(𝑖𝑗) is the (𝑛−1)×(𝑛−1) matrix with the 𝑖-th row and 𝑗-th column dropped.

Proof. Let 𝑒1,…,𝑒𝑛 be the standard basis of 𝐴𝑛 then by definition we have 𝐸̂(𝑒𝑖)=𝑣𝑖. Recall by (3.8.12) that 𝐷𝑛 is a generator of 𝐿𝑎𝑛(𝑀) and so by (3.8.15) we have det(𝐸̂)=𝐷𝑛(𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛).

The Leibniz Formula and Laplace Expansion Formula also follows directly from (3.8.12). ∎

Corollary 3.8.18 (Properties of Matrix Determinant)

The determinant satisfies a number of properties

  1. det(𝐸𝐹)=det(𝐸)det(𝐹)=det(𝐹)det(𝐸)
  2. det(𝐼𝑛)=1
  3. det(𝑃𝐸𝑃−1)=det(𝐸)
  4. det(𝐸𝑡)=det(𝐸)

Proof. a)-c) follow from (3.3.115) and (3.8.15). Explicitly

det(𝐸𝐹)=𝐷(𝐸𝐹̂)=𝐷(𝐸̂∘𝐹̂)=𝐷(𝐸̂)𝐷(𝐹̂)=det(𝐸)det(𝐹)

and

det(𝐼𝑛)=𝐷(𝐼𝑛̂)=𝐷(𝟏𝐴𝑛)=1𝐴

d) can be shown by using Leibniz formula and observing that 𝜎→𝜎−1 simply permutes the elements of 𝑆𝑛. ∎

Corollary 3.8.19 (Determinants Commute with Ring Homomorphisms)

Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be a homomorphism of commutative rings and 𝐸∈Mat𝑛×𝑛(𝐴). Then

det(𝜙(𝐸))=𝜙(det(𝐸))

where 𝜙 is applied to each entry of 𝐸.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

As 𝜙 preserves sums and products, the Leibniz Formula (3.8.17) gives

𝜙(det(𝐸))=∑𝜎∈𝑆𝑛𝜀(𝜎)∏𝑖=1𝑛𝜙(𝐸𝑖𝜎(𝑖))=det(𝜙(𝐸)).

 ∎

Proposition 3.8.20 (Determinant of an Endomorphism Stabilising a Direct Summand)

Let 𝑀 be a finite free 𝐴-module and 𝑓∈End𝐴(𝑀). Suppose 𝑀=𝑁⊕𝐶 for finite free submodules 𝑁,𝐶 with 𝑓(𝑁)⊆𝑁, and let 𝜋𝐶:𝑀→𝐶 be the projection along 𝑁. Then

det(𝑓)=det(𝑓|𝑁)det(𝜋𝐶∘𝑓|𝐶).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Write 𝑓𝑁≔𝑓|𝑁∈End𝐴(𝑁) and 𝑓𝐶≔𝜋𝐶∘𝑓|𝐶∈End𝐴(𝐶). Let 𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑝 be a basis of 𝑁 and 𝑣𝑝+1,…,𝑣𝑝+𝑞 a basis of 𝐶, so that 𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑝+𝑞 is a basis of 𝑀, and let Δ, Δ𝑁, Δ𝐶 be the corresponding determinants of 𝑀, 𝑁 and 𝐶 (3.8.14).

Claim. For 𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝∈𝑁 and 𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑞∈𝑀

Δ(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝,𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑞)=Δ𝑁(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝)Δ𝐶(𝜋𝐶𝑦1,…,𝜋𝐶𝑦𝑞).

Write 𝑦𝑗=𝑛𝑗+𝜋𝐶𝑦𝑗 with 𝑛𝑗∈𝑁 and expand by multilinearity. In every term in which some 𝑛𝑗 occurs, the 𝑝+1 arguments 𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝,𝑛𝑗 lie in 𝑁. Fixing the remaining arguments, this term is the value of an alternating form of degree 𝑝+1 on 𝑁 ((3.8.7)), which vanishes by (3.8.10) as 𝑁 has rank 𝑝. So only Δ(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝,𝜋𝐶𝑦1,…,𝜋𝐶𝑦𝑞) remains. For fixed 𝑥 this is an alternating 𝑞-form on 𝐶, so by (3.8.11) it equals Δ(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝,𝑣𝑝+1,…,𝑣𝑝+𝑞)Δ𝐶(𝜋𝐶𝑦1,…,𝜋𝐶𝑦𝑞). Finally 𝑥↦Δ(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑝,𝑣𝑝+1,…,𝑣𝑝+𝑞) is an alternating 𝑝-form on 𝑁 with value 1 at (𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑝), so equals Δ𝑁 by (3.8.11).

Now apply the claim with 𝑥𝑖=𝑓(𝑣𝑖)∈𝑁 (𝑖≤𝑝) and 𝑦𝑗=𝑓(𝑣𝑝+𝑗). By (3.8.15),

det(𝑓)=Δ(𝑓(𝑣1),…,𝑓(𝑣𝑝+𝑞))=Δ𝑁(𝑓𝑁(𝑣1),…,𝑓𝑁(𝑣𝑝))Δ𝐶(𝑓𝐶(𝑣𝑝+1),…,𝑓𝐶(𝑣𝑝+𝑞))=det(𝑓𝑁)det(𝑓𝐶).

 ∎

Corollary 3.8.21 (Determinant of a Block Triangular Matrix)

Let 𝑃∈Mat𝑝×𝑝(𝐴) and 𝑅∈Mat𝑞×𝑞(𝐴). Then

det(𝑃𝑄0𝑅)=det(𝑃)det(𝑅)=det(𝑃0𝑆𝑅)
