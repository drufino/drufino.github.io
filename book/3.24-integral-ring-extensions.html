3.24 Integral Ring Extensions

Definition 3.24.1 (Integral Element)

Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be a ring map and 𝛼∈𝐵. Then we say 𝛼 is algebraic over 𝐴 if 𝑚(𝛼)=0 for some polynomial 𝑚(𝑋)∈𝐴[𝑋].

Furthermore we say that 𝛼 is integral over 𝐴 if 𝑚(𝑋) may be chosen to be monic.

Note often we assume 𝐴⊆𝐵 and 𝜙 is the identity.

Definition 3.24.2 (Ring Extensions)

Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be a ring map (so that 𝐵 is an 𝐴-algebra). Then we say 𝜙 is

  • finite if 𝐵 is finite as an 𝐴-module
  • finite-type if 𝐵 is finitely generated as an 𝐴-algebra
  • integral if every element of 𝐵 is integral over 𝐴

When 𝐴⊆𝐵 and 𝜙 is the identity then we say that 𝐵 is respectively finite over 𝐴, finite-type over 𝐴 or integral over 𝐴.

We note the trivial implication

finite⟹finite type

For example 𝑘[𝑋] is a ring of finite type over 𝑘, but certainly not finite. The following criterion for integrality is fundamental.

Proposition 3.24.3

Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be a ring map and 𝑏∈𝐵. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑏 is integral over 𝐴
  2. 𝜙(𝐴)[𝑏] is a finite 𝐴-module
  3. 𝜙(𝐴)[𝑏] is contained in a subring 𝐶 of 𝐵 which is a finite 𝐴-module
  4. There exists a 𝜙(𝐴)[𝑏]-module 𝑀 which is faithful and finite as an 𝐴-module
Proof. Note that the subring 𝐶 in c) is a faithful 𝐴-module, so the only non-trivial step is d) ⟹ a). This is the usual “determinant trick”. We apply (3.18.4) by considering 𝜓𝑏∈End𝐴(𝑀) to be multiplication by 𝑏. Then we have some monic polynomial 𝑃(𝑋)∈𝐴[𝑋] such that 𝑃(𝜓𝑏)=0, whence 𝑃𝜙(𝑏)𝑚=0 for all 𝑚∈𝑀. Since 𝑀 is faithful, then we have 𝑃(𝑏)=0 as required. ∎
Proposition 3.24.4 (Finite ⟺ finite-type and integral)

Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be a ring map and 𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑛∈𝐵 integral over 𝐴. Then the ring homomorphism 𝜙:𝐴→𝜙(𝐴)[𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑛] is finite and integral.

In particular if 𝜙 is integral and of finite type if and only if it is finite.

Proof. We assume without loss of generality that 𝐴⊆𝐵 and 𝜙 is the identity map. Consider a tower

𝐴⊂𝐴[𝑏1]⊂…⊂𝐴[𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑛]=𝐵

We proceed inductively on 𝑛. Namely we assume that 𝐴[𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑖] is a finite 𝐴-module. Then a-fortiori 𝐴[𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑖+1] is integral over 𝐴[𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑖]. Therefore by the previous Proposition it is a finite 𝐴[𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑖]-module and therefore a finite 𝐴-module (by (3.15.5)).

For any 𝑏∈𝐴[𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑛] we have 𝐴[𝑏]⊂𝐴[𝑏1,…,𝑏𝑛] so by (3.24.3) we have 𝑏 is integral over 𝐴.

It then follows that 𝐵 integral and finite type ⟹ 𝐵 is finite (as an 𝐴-module). Conversely if 𝐵 is finite then it is clearly finitely-generated. Further for any 𝑏∈𝐵 then 𝐴[𝑏] is contained in the ring 𝐵 which is finite as an 𝐴-module, and therefore is 𝑏 is integral by (3.24.3). ∎

Proposition 3.24.5 (Transitivity property)

Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 and 𝜓:𝐵→𝐶 be ring maps.

If 𝑐∈𝐶 is integral over 𝐵, then it is integral over 𝐴 (with respect to the ring map 𝜓∘𝜙:𝐴→𝐶)

In particular if 𝜙 integral and 𝜓 integral (e.g. surjective) ⟹ 𝜓∘𝜙 integral.

Proof. Suppose 𝑐∈𝐶 is integral over 𝐵. Let 𝑏0,…,𝑏𝑛−1 be the coefficients of the integral relation then clearly 𝑐 is integral over 𝐵′≔𝐴[𝑏0,…,𝑏𝑛−1]. By (3.24.3) 𝐵′ is a finite 𝐴-module and 𝐵′[𝑐] is a finite 𝐵′-module. Therefore 𝐵′[𝑐] is a finite 𝐴-module and by (3.24.3) we have 𝑐 is integral over 𝐴. ∎
Definition 3.24.6 (Integrally Closed)

Let 𝐴⊂𝐵 be a subring, then we say that 𝐴 is integrally closed in 𝐵 if

𝑏∈𝐵integral over𝐴⟹𝑏∈𝐴.

We say that an integral domain 𝐴 is integrally closed if it is integrally closed in its quotient field.

Proposition 3.24.7 (Integral Closure)

Let 𝐴 be a subring of a ring 𝐵. Then the set of elements of 𝐵 integral over 𝐴 (the integral closure) is a subring of 𝐵. Denote this by 𝐴―𝐵 or just 𝐴― when the overring 𝐵 is unambiguous.

Further 𝐴―𝐵 is integrally closed in 𝐵.

Proof. Let 𝛼,𝛽∈𝐵 be integral over 𝐴. Then by (3.24.4) the subring 𝐴[𝛼,𝛽] is a finite 𝐴-module containing 𝛼±𝛽 and 𝛼𝛽. Therefore by (3.24.3) they are also integral over 𝐴.

Clearly 𝐴― is integral over 𝐴 so by (3.24.5) it is integrally closed. ∎

Lemma 3.24.8

Let 𝐴 be a subring of a field 𝐾 and 𝑆⊂𝐴 a multiplicatively closed subset. If 𝑥∈𝐾 is integral over 𝑆−1𝐴 then 𝑠𝑥 is integral over 𝐴 for some 𝑠∈𝑆.

Conversely if 𝑦∈𝐾 is integral over 𝐴 then 𝑦𝑠 is integral over 𝑆−1𝐴 for every 𝑠∈𝑆.

Proof. Suppose 𝑥∈𝐾 is integral over 𝑆−1𝐴 then we have by definition

𝑥𝑛+∑𝑗=0𝑛−1𝑎𝑗𝑠𝑗𝑥𝑗=0

for 𝑎𝑗∈𝐴 and 𝑠𝑗∈𝑆. Let 𝑠≔𝑠0…𝑠𝑛−1 and multiply by 𝑠𝑛 to find

𝑦𝑛+∑𝑗=0𝑛−1𝑠𝑛−𝑗−1𝑠𝑠𝑗𝑎𝑗𝑦𝑗=0

where 𝑦=𝑠𝑥. This shows that 𝑠𝑥 is integral over 𝐴

Similarly if 𝑦∈𝐾 is integral over 𝐴 then

𝑦𝑛+∑𝑗=0𝑛−1𝑎𝑗𝑦𝑗=0

for some 𝑎𝑗∈𝐴. Then let 𝑥=𝑦𝑠 we have

𝑥𝑛+∑𝑗=0𝑛−1𝑎𝑗𝑠𝑛−𝑗𝑥𝑗=0

whence 𝑥 is integral over 𝑆−1𝐴. ∎

Lemma 3.24.9

Let 𝐴⊂𝐾 be a subring of a field. Then 𝑆−1𝐴―𝐾=𝑆−1𝐴―𝐾.

In particular if 𝐴 is integrally closed in 𝐾 then so is 𝑆−1𝐴.

Proof. This follows directly from (3.24.8). ∎
Proposition 3.24.10 (Normality is Local)

Let 𝐴 be an integral domain. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝐴 is integrally closed
  2. 𝐴𝔭 is integrally closed for every prime ideal 𝔭
  3. 𝐴𝔪 is integrally closed for every maximal ideal 𝔪

Proof. a) ⟹ b) This is simply (3.24.9).

b) ⟹ c) is immediate.

c) ⟹ a) Suppose that 𝑥∈𝐾 is integral over 𝐴 and consider the ideal 𝔞≔{𝑎∈𝐴|𝑎𝑥∈𝐴}. Then by definition it is a non-zero ideal, and we wish to show that 𝔞=𝐴. Suppose not, then by (…) it is contained in some maximal ideal 𝔪. A-fortiori 𝑥 is integral over 𝐴𝔪 whence by assumption 𝑥∈𝐴𝔪. This means there is some 𝑠∉𝔪 such that 𝑠𝑥∈𝐴, that is 𝑠∈𝔞 a contradiction. ∎

Proposition 3.24.11 (Integral closure of an ideal)

Let 𝐴⊂𝐵 be a subring and 𝔞◃𝐴 an ideal. Then for 𝑏∈𝐵 TFAE

  1. 𝑏 integral over 𝔞

𝑏𝑛+𝑎𝑛−1𝑏𝑛−1+…𝑎0=0𝑎𝑖∈𝔞

By (3.24.7) 𝐴― is a subring, and by assumption 𝑏∈𝐴―. Therefore 𝑏𝑘∈𝐴―, and the integral relation shows that 𝑏𝑛∈𝔞𝐴― as required.

Lemma 3.24.13 (Integral Criterion II)
Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be a ring map. Suppose 𝑥∈𝐵 is invertible, then 𝑥 is integral over 𝐴 if and only if 𝑥∈𝜙(𝐴)[𝑥−1]

Proof. Suppose 𝑥∈𝜙(𝐴)[𝑥−1] then

𝑥=𝜙(𝑎0)+𝜙(𝑎1)𝑥−1+…+𝜙(𝑎𝑛)𝑥−𝑛

Multiply by 𝑥𝑛 to deduce an integral equation. Conversely suppose 𝑥∈𝐵 is integral over 𝐴 then by definition

𝑥𝑛+𝜙(𝑎𝑛−1)𝑥𝑛−1+…+𝜙(𝑎0)=0

Multiply by 𝑥−(𝑛−1) to deduce 𝑥∈𝜙(𝐴)[𝑥−1] ∎

Proposition 3.24.14 (Integral extension preserves field property)
Let 𝜙:𝐴↪︎𝐵 be an injective, integral ring map. Then 𝐵 is a field if and only if 𝐴 is a field.

Proof. As 𝜙 is injective, it induces an isomorphism between 𝐴 and 𝜙(𝐴). So we may assume without loss of generality that 𝐴⊆𝐵 and 𝜙 is the identity.

Suppose 𝐵 is a field and 𝑥∈𝐴. Then 𝑥−1∈𝐵 is integral over 𝐴 by hypothesis, so by the previous Lemma 𝑥−1∈𝐴[𝑥]⊆𝐴. Therefore 𝐴 is a field.

Conversely suppose 𝐴 is a field and 0≠𝑥∈𝐵. Then by hypothesis 𝑥 is integral over 𝐴, that is

𝑥𝑛+𝑎𝑛−1𝑥𝑛−1+…+𝑎1𝑥+𝑎0=0

Choose the degree 𝑛 to be minimal. We claim 𝑎0≠0, for if 𝑎0=0 we may cancel 𝑥 to obtain an integral relation of smaller degree. Therefore

−𝑥(𝑥𝑛−1+𝑎𝑛−1𝑥𝑛−2+…+𝑎1)𝑎0−1=1

and in particular 𝑥 is invertible. ∎

Proposition 3.24.16 (Maximal ideals under integral extension)

Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be an integral ring map. Suppose 𝔮 lies above 𝔭. Then

𝔭is maximal⟺𝔮is maximal
Proof. The map 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 induces an injective map 𝐴/𝔭↪︎𝐵/𝔮 of integral domains by (3.3.56). By (3.24.15) this map is also integral. Note 𝐴/𝔭 (resp. 𝐵/𝔮) is a field if and only if 𝔭 (resp. 𝔮) is a maximal ideal by (3.3.59). Then we may apply (3.24.14) to show the equivalence. ∎

Proposition 3.24.18 (Coefficients of minimal polynomial)

Let 𝐴⊆𝐵 be integral domains, 𝐴 is integrally closed, and define 𝐾=Frac(𝐴) and 𝐿=Frac(𝐵). For 𝑏∈𝐵 integral over 𝔞◃𝐴 we have the non-leading coefficients of 𝑚𝑏(𝑋) are integral over 𝔞 and therefore lie in 𝔞.

Note if 𝑏 is only assumed to be integral over 𝐴 then the coefficients of 𝑚𝑏(𝑋) lie in 𝐴.

Both Norm𝐿/𝐾(𝑏) and Tr𝐿/𝐾(𝑏) lie in 𝐴.

Proof. Let 𝑀/𝐾 be a normal closure for 𝐿/𝐾 (…). By (3.24.11) the integral closure of 𝔞 in 𝑀 is simply 𝔞. Then the minimal polynomial 𝑚𝑏(𝑋) splits completely in 𝑀 and by (3.19.66) all the roots 𝑏𝑖 are conjugate by Aut(𝑀/𝐾). In particular it’s clear that 𝑏𝑖 are integral over 𝔞, and so lie in 𝔞. The coefficients of 𝑚𝑏(𝑋) are polynomials in the 𝑏𝑖, and so by the observation above are also lie in 𝔞 (and are integral over 𝔞).

We may consider the case 𝔞=𝐴 to deduce that 𝑚𝑏(𝑋) is a monic polynomial with coefficients in 𝐴. By (3.19.128) then the coefficients of Pc𝐿/𝐾(𝑏;𝑋) lie in 𝐴, and so by (3.19.123) so do the norm and trace. ∎

Proposition 3.24.19 (Going Down)
Let 𝐴⊆𝐵 be integral ring extension such that 𝐴 is an integrally closed domain and 𝐵 is an integral domain. Then it has the Going Down property.

Proof. Let 𝔭⊊𝔭′ be prime ideals of 𝐴 and 𝔮′ a prime ideal lying over 𝔭′. We wish to find a prime ideal 𝔮⊆𝔮′ lying over 𝔭 (clearly inclusion must be strict).

Consider the inclusion of rings 𝐴⊆𝐵𝔮′. Then by (3.23.5) 𝔭 lies under a prime of 𝐵𝔮′ if and only if 𝔭=𝔭𝑒𝑐=𝔭𝐵𝔮′∩𝐴. If this is the case then it is of the form 𝔮𝐵𝔮′ for some prime ideal 𝔮⊆𝔮′ (3.6.42) of 𝐵. It’s clear that 𝔮 lies over 𝔭.

Note in general that 𝔭⊆𝔭𝑒𝑐, so we only need to demonstrate the reverse inclusion. Choose 𝑥∈𝔭𝐵𝔮′∩𝐴. By (3.6.18) 𝔭𝐵𝔮′=𝑆−1(𝔭𝐵) where 𝑆=𝐵∖𝔮′.

Then 𝑥=𝑦𝑠 for 𝑦∈𝔭𝐵 and 𝑠∈𝐵∖𝔮′. By (3.24.11) we have 𝑦 is integral over 𝔭 whence by (3.24.18) the minimal polynomial 𝑚𝑦,𝐾(𝑋) is equal to

𝑋𝑟+𝑢1𝑋𝑟−1+…+𝑢𝑟𝑢𝑖∈𝔭

However 𝑠=𝑦𝑥−1 and 𝑥∈𝐴⟹𝑥−1∈𝐾. So we can derive the minimal polynomial 𝑚𝑠,𝐾(𝑋)

𝑋𝑟+𝑣1𝑋𝑟−1+…+𝑣𝑟𝑣𝑖≔𝑢𝑖𝑥𝑖

As 𝑠 is assumed to be integral over 𝐴 the coefficients must all lie in 𝐴, by (…). Consequently 𝑣𝑖∈𝐴 and 𝑣𝑖𝑥𝑖∈𝔭 for all 𝑖. If 𝑥∉𝔭 then we have 𝑣𝑖∈𝔭 for all 𝑖, and 𝑠 is integral over 𝔭. By the minimal polynomial we see that 𝑠∈𝐵𝔭⊆𝐵𝔭′⊆𝔮′, which contradicts the choice of 𝑠. Therefore 𝑥∈𝔭 as required. ∎

Proposition 3.24.20

Lemma 3.24.21
Let 𝜙:𝐴→𝐵 be a finite ring homomorphism. Suppose 𝐵=(𝑓1,…,𝑓𝑛) and the composite map 𝜙𝑖:𝐴→𝐵𝑓𝑖 is finite for all 𝑖=1…𝑛, then so is 𝜙.
Proof. There are finitely many elements {𝑏𝑖𝑗𝑓𝑖𝑛𝑖𝑗}𝑗 which generate 𝐵𝑓𝑖 as an 𝐴-module. Let 𝐵′ be the 𝐴-submodule of 𝐵 generated by all the 𝑏𝑖𝑗. Then 𝐵𝑓𝑖′=𝐵𝑓𝑖 as 𝐵-modules. By (3.6.47) we have 𝐵′=𝐵 and therefore 𝐵 is finitely generated as an 𝐴-module. ∎
