6.3.1 Relation to the Literature

These treatments fall into two methodological groups:

  • Weil’s répartition/adele method, in the tradition initiated by Chevalley [Che51]: used by Chevalley himself, by Stichtenoth [Sti09], by Lang [Lan82] (all three over a general constant field), and, at its computational core, even by Serre [Ser59] – whose own bibliographic note attributes the proof to Weil, though he additionally erects the formalism of sheaf cohomology around it, identifying 𝐻1(𝑋,𝒪︀𝑋) with the classes of repartitions. Lang’s book, unlike the others in this group, has a different overall aim: a bridge to the classical complex-analytic theory of abelian and theta functions, which the algebraic function-field theory here does not pursue.
  • the classical algebraic-geometric (scheme-free, ringed-space) approach shared by Hartshorne [Har13] and Bump [Bum98], the latter specialised to curves with a dedicated treatment of differentials, residues and ramification.

I have interpolated between these, guided by four principles.

Dedekind domains over bare valuation theory. I use Dedekind domain theory for the key results on function fields and the Weil form of Riemann-Roch, rather than the purely valuation-theoretic development of [Che51] and [Sti09]. In the same spirit, finite extensions of 𝑘((𝑡)) (3.33.5) are analysed directly via the integral closure in a Dedekind domain and a direct power-series computation, rather than via the 𝑃-adic completion machinery both of those references build on; this also yields the result in every characteristic, where [Che51]‘s analogous Puiseux-expansion treatment (Ch. II, §6) is restricted to the tame case, as noted in the remark opening the Extensions of Power Series Rings section. We also use the theory of affine algebras to simplify the proof of some further technical results.

A general constant field, reduced to the algebraically closed case only once. Riemann-Roch and the Residue Theorem are proved over a general constant field, matching Riemann-Roch (though not the Residue Theorem, which is stated only over an algebraically closed field) in [Sti09], but going beyond [Ser59], [Har13], [Bum98] and [Lan82], whose treatments of Riemann-Roch (and, for [Bum98], the Residue Theorem too) all assume an algebraically closed constant field throughout. The general-field Residue Theorem ((6.3.57)) is not proved directly: it is deduced from the algebraically closed case ((6.3.34)) by base extension to 𝑘― and a Galois-descent argument summing conjugate residues, rather than by redoing the local theory over a general field from scratch.

Concrete constructions over general machinery, wherever one suffices. Like [Che51], [Sti09] and [Lan82], I work with Chevalley and Weil’s répartition/adele-style methods directly, and connect this to cohomology only after the fact, rather than building up the general machinery of coherent sheaf cohomology as [Ser59] does around the same underlying répartition computation. Likewise, the Residue Theorem is proved without recourse to local completions, unlike the concrete, differential-level residue theorems of [Ser59] and [Sti09], which both depend on the 𝑃-adic completion; for the rational function field this is done via a short, self-contained elementary proof by partial fractions, rather than as a special case of the general machinery. The Trace Formula for Residues ((6.3.42)) likewise avoids completions, via the same Laurent-series finite-extension lemma above, rather than by appeal to the Complete Discrete Valuation Rings machinery developed alongside it (the Extensions of Power Series Rings and Complete Discrete Valuation Rings sections). And residues are defined directly from the uniformiser derivation without ever constructing the module of Kähler differentials Ω𝐾/𝑘, in contrast to [Ser59] and [Sti09], which both identify their (Weil-differential-based) residues with genuine differentials in this sense.

Characteristic 𝑝 handled uniformly, via universal ℤ-polynomial identities. Invariance of the residue of a differential under change of uniformiser – notoriously tricky in characteristic 𝑝, where the naive derivative-of-a-power argument breaks down whenever 𝑝 divides the relevant exponent – is settled once, universally, over ℤ[𝑎1,𝑎1−1,𝑎2,𝑎3,…] and specialised ((6.3.26)), the same device used by [Ser59] and [Sti09], rather than the Cartier-operator route of [Bum98] unlike [Ser59] and [Sti09], this is done over a general constant field rather than an algebraically closed one. The same device is reused for the Trace Formula for Residues, which holds without any tameness hypothesis ((6.3.40)) by the identical method [Ser59] uses (there attributed to Hasse) for the analogous local identity Res𝑡(𝑓𝑑𝑢)=Res𝑢(Tr(𝑓)𝑑𝑢) underlying its own residue formula; it is exactly this universal-identity device that lets the wildly ramified case go through in both results.

Two further points of departure concern scope and connections to the classical picture, rather than proof technique: the dictionary between the function field and the regular algebraic curve – established even over a non-algebraically closed field, generalising the algebraically-closed-field statement of [Har13] – is proved later, in (6.2.127) of the Algebraic Geometry chapter’s treatment of regular algebraic curves; and, in the classical case of a compact Riemann surface ((6.3.19)), Weil differentials are shown to coincide canonically with classical meromorphic 1-forms, reconnecting the algebraic formalism with the analytic theory it generalises.
