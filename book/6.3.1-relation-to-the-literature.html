6.3.1 Relation to the Literature

These treatments fall into two methodological groups:

  • Weil’s répartition/adele method, in the tradition initiated by Chevalley [Che51]: used by Chevalley himself, by Stichtenoth [Sti09], by Lang [Lan82] (all three over a general constant field), and, at its computational core, even by Serre [Ser59] – whose own bibliographic note attributes the proof to Weil, though he additionally erects the formalism of sheaf cohomology around it, identifying 𝐻1(𝑋,𝒪︀𝑋) with the classes of repartitions. Lang’s book, unlike the others in this group, has a different overall aim: a bridge to the classical complex-analytic theory of abelian and theta functions, which the algebraic function-field theory here does not pursue.
  • the classical algebraic-geometric (scheme-free, ringed-space) approach shared by Hartshorne [Har13] and Bump [Bum98], the latter specialised to curves with a dedicated treatment of differentials, residues and ramification.

I have interpolated between these, guided by four principles.

Dedekind domains over bare valuation theory. I use Dedekind domain theory for the key results on function fields and the Weil form of Riemann-Roch, rather than the purely valuation-theoretic development of [Che51] and [Sti09]. In the same spirit, finite extensions of 𝑘((𝑡)) (3.32.35) are analysed directly via the integral closure in a Dedekind domain and a direct power-series computation, rather than via the 𝑃-adic completion machinery both of those references build on; this also yields the result in every characteristic, where [Che51]‘s analogous Puiseux-expansion treatment (Ch. II, §6) is restricted to the tame case, as noted in the remark opening the Extensions of Power Series Rings subsection. We also use the theory of affine algebras to simplify the proof of some further technical results.

A perfect constant field, reduced to the algebraically closed case only once. Riemann-Roch is proved over a general constant field, matching [Sti09], but going beyond [Ser59], [Har13], [Bum98] and [Lan82], whose treatments of Riemann-Roch all assume an algebraically closed constant field throughout. The Residue Theorem, on which the identification of the genus and the index of speciality with rational differentials rests, is likewise stated in the literature only over an algebraically closed field ([Ser59] (Ch. II), [Lan82] (Ch. I, §5), [Sti09] and [Bum98]). Our proof in that case (6.3.34) follows [Ser59] (Ch. II, nos. 12–13) and [Lan82] (Ch. I, §§4–5): partial fractions on the rational function field, the trace formula for residues to reduce the general case to it, and a local trace formula for power series fields proved by a universal identity over ℤ. The local theory rests on the structure of finite extensions of 𝑘((𝑡)) from the Extensions of Power Series Rings subsection (3.32.35), (3.32.36) and the Weierstrass division lemma (3.32.31), as [Lan82] (Ch. I, §4, Prop. 4.3) does. We need the Residue Theorem over a perfect field (6.3.53), and deduce it from the algebraically closed case by relating the residue symbol over 𝑘 to its base change to 𝑘―: the residue at a place 𝑃 is the trace of the residue at a conjugate place of 𝐾𝑘― above it (6.3.52), so the sum of the residues at the places of 𝐾 is the sum of the residues at all the places of 𝐾𝑘― (6.3.51).

The perfect case can also be assembled using only results of [Sti09]. There the residue is defined only at places of degree one (Def. 4.2.10), and the Residue Theorem (Cor. 4.3.3) is stated only over an algebraically closed field, where every place has degree one. But Thm. 4.3.2 identifies differentials with Weil differentials over any perfect constant field 𝐾, and for a place 𝑃 of degree 𝑓, with the 𝑓 places 𝑃―1,…,𝑃―𝑓 of the constant field extension 𝐹𝐾― above it (Cor. 3.6.5), Rem. 4.3.7(d) gives the formula (4.38)

𝜔𝑃(𝑢)=∑𝑖=1𝑓res𝑃―𝑖(𝑢𝜔),

that is, the local component of a differential at 𝑃 is the sum of the residues at the conjugate rational places. This is exactly our residue symbol at 𝑃 (6.3.51). The vanishing of the sum is then Prop. 1.7.2, ∑𝑃𝜔𝑃(1)=𝜔(1)=0, valid over any constant field since a Weil differential vanishes on the principal adele 1. So over a perfect field the Residue Theorem is literally ∑𝑃𝜔𝑃(1)=0 once residues are read as local components; its algebraically closed statement is the special case where every place is rational. The compatibility of local components with constant field extension used there is Prop. 4.3.6, (4.33), which rests on the cotrace formula (4.21) – the cotrace of a Weil differential 𝜔 of 𝐹 in a finite separable extension 𝐹′ being the Weil differential 𝜔′ of 𝐹′ characterised by 𝜔𝑃(Tr𝐹′/𝐹(𝑦))=∑𝑃′∣𝑃𝜔𝑃′′(𝑦) for 𝑦∈𝐹′ – and the construction of the Weil differential 𝛿(𝑥) as a cotrace; our approach replaces it by the direct comparison of Laurent expansions (6.3.50).

Concrete constructions over general machinery, wherever one suffices. Like [Che51], [Sti09] and [Lan82], I work with Chevalley and Weil’s répartition/adele-style methods directly, and connect this to cohomology only after the fact, rather than building up the general machinery of coherent sheaf cohomology as [Ser59] does around the same underlying répartition computation. Residues are defined directly from the Laurent expansion at a place, via the uniformiser derivation, without ever constructing the module of Kähler differentials Ω𝐾/𝑘, in contrast to [Ser59] and [Sti09], which both identify their (Weil-differential-based) residues with genuine differentials in this sense; at a non-rational place the residue is the trace of the residue in 𝑘(𝑃)((𝑋)), rather than a local component of a Weil differential defined through cotraces as in [Sti09]. As in [Lan82], the local analysis uses only explicit Laurent series fields and the structure of their finite extensions (3.32.35), rather than the abstract 𝑃-adic completion on which [Ser59] and [Sti09] depend, and the rational function field is handled by a short, self-contained proof by partial fractions rather than as a special case of the general machinery. The Trace Formula for Residues (6.3.42) likewise rests on this Laurent-series finite-extension lemma, and not on the general Complete Discrete Valuation Rings section developed alongside it, of which the lemma is the special case 𝒪︀𝐾≅𝑘[[𝑡]].

Characteristic 𝑝 handled uniformly, via universal ℤ-polynomial identities. Invariance of the residue of a differential under change of uniformiser – notoriously tricky in characteristic 𝑝, where the naive derivative-of-a-power argument breaks down whenever 𝑝 divides the relevant exponent – is settled once, universally, over ℤ[𝑎1,𝑎1−1,𝑎2,𝑎3,…] and specialised (6.3.26), the same device used by [Ser59] and [Sti09], rather than the Cartier-operator route of [Bum98] unlike [Ser59] and [Sti09], this is done over a general constant field rather than an algebraically closed one. The same device is reused for the Trace Formula for Residues, which holds without any tameness hypothesis (6.3.40) by the identical method [Ser59] uses (there attributed to Hasse) for the analogous local identity Res𝑡(𝑓𝑑𝑢)=Res𝑢(Tr(𝑓)𝑑𝑢) underlying its own residue formula; it is exactly this universal-identity device that lets the wildly ramified case go through in both results.

Two further points of departure concern scope and connections to the classical picture, rather than proof technique: the dictionary between the function field and the regular algebraic curve – established even over a non-algebraically closed field, generalising the algebraically-closed-field statement of [Har13] – is established earlier, in (6.2.127) of the Algebraic Geometry chapter’s treatment of regular algebraic curves; and, in the classical case of a compact Riemann surface (6.3.19), Weil differentials are shown to coincide canonically with classical meromorphic 1-forms, reconnecting the algebraic formalism with the analytic theory it generalises.
