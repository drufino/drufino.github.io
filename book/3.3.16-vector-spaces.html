3.3.16 Vector Spaces

Definition 3.3.123 (Vector Space)

A vector space 𝑉 over 𝑘 is simply a 𝑘-module.

A 𝑘-submodule is referred to as a subspace

A 𝑘-module homomorphism is referred to as a linear map

A vector space is finite-dimensional if it is finite as a 𝑘-module.

The main result on vector spaces is that bases exist and all have the same cardinality. Recall that ⟨⋅⟩ is a closure operator. We show that (𝑉,⟨⋅⟩) determines a matroid so that we may appeal to results in the Matroids section. First we need to show that the notions of independence coincide

Proposition 3.3.124 (Equivalent definitions of linear independence)

Let 𝑉 be a vector space and 𝑆⊂𝑉. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑆 is linearly independent
  2. No proper subset 𝑆′⊂𝑆 satisfies ⟨𝑆′⟩=⟨𝑆⟩
  3. Matroid Independence 𝑥∈𝑆⟹𝑥∉⟨𝑆∖{𝑥}⟩

Further 𝑆 is independent if and only if every finite subset of 𝑆 is.

Proof. b) ⟺ c) This is b).

a) ⟹ c). If 𝑥∈⟨𝑆∖{𝑥}⟩ then it’s clear that 𝑆 is not linearly independent.

c) ⟹ a). Suppose we have a linear relationship

0=∑𝑖=1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑣𝑖𝑣𝑖∈𝑆

By renumbering assume that 𝜆1≠0, then rearrange to show 𝑣1∈⟨𝑆∖{𝑣1}⟩, contradicting the hypothesis. ∎

We show that (𝑉,⟨⋅⟩) satisfies the Exchange Property and therefore constitutes a matroid.

Proposition 3.3.125 (Exchange Property)

Let 𝑉 be a vector space and 𝑆⊂𝑉. Then

𝑦∈⟨𝑆∪{𝑥}⟩∖⟨𝑆⟩⟹𝑥∈⟨𝑆∪{𝑦}⟩

Proof. Suppose 𝑦 is as given, then

𝑦=𝜆𝑥+∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝑠𝑖𝑠𝑖∈𝑆

The case 𝜆=0 is obvious. Otherwise we may rearrange to find

𝑥=𝜆−1𝑦−∑𝑖𝜆−1𝜆𝑖𝑠𝑖

whence 𝑥∈⟨𝑆∪{𝑦}⟩. ∎

Therefore we have the following

Proposition 3.3.126 (Vector Spaces are Free)

Every vector space has a basis, and in the finite-dimensional case every basis is finite of the same size. We denote this by dim𝑘𝑉.

More generally every linearly independent set is contained in a basis (so has order at most dim𝑘𝑉) and every spanning set contains a basis (so has order at least dim𝑘𝑉)

Proof. Follows from (2.3.7) and (2.3.12). The final statement follows from (2.3.13). ∎
Proposition 3.3.127 (Basis Criteria)

Let 𝑉 be a vector space with 𝑛=dim𝑘𝑉 and ℬ︀⊆𝑉. Then TFAE

  1. ℬ︀ is a basis
  2. ℬ︀ is linearly independent and #︎ℬ︀≥dim𝑘𝑉
  3. ℬ︀ is spanning and #︎ℬ︀≤dim𝑘𝑉
Proof. The equivalence of a), b) and c) follows from (2.3.14). ∎
Proposition 3.3.128
A vector space 𝑉={0} if and only if dim𝑘𝑉=0
Proposition 3.3.129 (Image of a basis)

Let 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 be a linear map

  1. If 𝑆 is linearly-independent and 𝜑 is injective, then 𝜑(𝑆) is linearly-independent
  2. If Γ is spanning then (𝜑 is surjective ⟺ 𝜑(Γ) is spanning)
  3. If ℬ︀ is a basis then (𝜑 is an isomorphism ⟺𝜑(ℬ︀) is a basis and 𝜑 injective on ℬ︀)

Proof.

  1. Suppose ∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝜑(𝑠𝑖)=0⟹𝜑(∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝑠𝑖)=0. As 𝜑 is injective this implies ∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝑠𝑖=0⟹𝜆𝑖=0.
  2. If 𝜑 is surjective then for 𝑤∈𝑊 we have 𝜑(𝑣)=𝑤 for some 𝑣∈𝑉. By hypothesis 𝑣=∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝛾𝑖 and 𝑤=∑𝑖𝜑(𝜆𝑖). Conversely given 𝑤∈𝑊 by hypothesis 𝑤=∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝜑(𝛾𝑖)=𝜑(∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝛾𝑖) and 𝜑 is surjective as required.
  3. Suppose 𝜑 is isomorphism, then it’s surely injective on ℬ︀ and by a),b) 𝜑(ℬ︀) is a basis. Conversely if 𝜑(ℬ︀)≕ℬ︀′ is a basis then by b) 𝜑 is surjective. Suppose 𝜑(𝑣)=0. Then by hypothesis 𝑣=∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝑣𝑖 for 𝑣𝑖∈ℬ︀ and 0=𝜑(𝑣)=∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝜑(𝑣𝑖). By hypothesis 𝜑(𝑣𝑖) are distinct elements of the basis ℬ︀′ and therefore 𝜆𝑖=0 and 𝑣=0. Therefore 𝜑 is injective and hence bijective.

 ∎

Corollary 3.3.130 (Dimension is an invariant)

Dimension is preserved under isomorphism. More generally for 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 we have

𝜑injective⟹dim𝑘𝑉≤dim𝑘𝑊𝜑surjective⟹dim𝑘𝑉≥dim𝑘𝑊
Proposition 3.3.131

Let 𝑊⊆𝑉 be finite-dimensional vector spaces then the dimension of the quotient module satisfies

dim𝑘𝑉/𝑊=dim𝑘𝑉−dim𝑘𝑊

Proof. Let {𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑚} be a basis of 𝑊, then there exists a basis {𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑚,𝑣𝑚+1,…,𝑣𝑛} of 𝑉 containing the first by (3.3.126). We claim that

{[𝑣𝑚+1],…,[𝑣𝑛]}

is a basis for 𝑉/𝑊, and the result follows. For given [𝑣]∈𝑉/𝑊 then

𝑣=∑𝑖=1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑣𝑖

since the basis is spanning. We have

𝑣−∑𝑖=𝑚+1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑣𝑖∈𝑊

therefore

[𝑣]=[∑𝑖=𝑚+1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑣𝑖]=∑𝑖=𝑚+1𝑛𝜆𝑖[𝑣𝑖]

and the given set is spanning. Similarly suppose

∑𝑖=𝑚+1𝑛𝜆𝑖[𝑣𝑖]=0

then by definition ∑𝑖=𝑚+1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑣𝑖∈𝑊. Therefore

∑𝑖=𝑚+1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑣𝑖=∑𝑖=1𝑚𝜆𝑖𝑣𝑖

and since 𝑣𝑖 are linearly independent we must have 𝜆𝑖=0. ∎

Proposition 3.3.132 (Injective Criteria)

Let 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 be a linear map then

𝜑injective⟺ker(𝜑)={0}⟺dim𝑘ker(𝜑)=0
Proof. Note for any linear map 𝜑 we have 𝜑(0)=0. Therefore 𝜑 injective clearly shows ker(𝜑)={0}. Conversely suppose ker(𝜑)=0 and 𝜑(𝑣)=𝜑(𝑤). Then 𝜑(𝑣−𝑤)=0⟹𝑣−𝑤=0⟹𝑣=𝑤 as required. ∎
Definition 3.3.133 (Rank)

Let 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 be a linear map then define

rank𝑘(𝜑)≔dim𝑘(Im(𝜑))
Proposition 3.3.134 (Surjective Criteria)

Let 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 be a linear map with 𝑊 finite-dimensional then

𝜑surjective⟺rank𝑘(𝜑)=dim𝑘𝑊
Proof. This follows directly from (2.3.15). ∎
Proposition 3.3.135 (Isomorphism Theorem / Rank-Nullity)

Let 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 be a linear map then this induces an isomorphism

𝑉/ker(𝜑)⟶∼Im(𝜑)

in particular when 𝑉 is finite-dimensional

dim𝑘𝑉=dim𝑘ker(𝜑)+rank𝑘(𝜑)
Corollary 3.3.136 (Isomorphism Criteria)

Let 𝑉,𝑊 vector spaces with 𝑊 finite-dimensional. A linear map 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 is an isomorphism if and only if any two of the following are satisfied

  1. dim𝑘ker(𝜑)=0⟺𝜑 injective
  2. dim𝑘𝑉=dim𝑘𝑊
  3. rank𝑘(𝜑)=dim𝑘𝑊⟺𝜑 surjective
Proof. The rank-nullity equation ensures that if any two hold the third is automatically satisfied. In this case 𝜑 is isomorphism as required. ∎
Corollary 3.3.137 (Endomorphism Isomorphism Criteria)

Let 𝑉 be a finite-dimensional vector space and 𝜑:𝑉→𝑉 then TFAE

  1. 𝜑 is injective
  2. 𝜑 is surjective
  3. 𝜑 is an isomorphism
Proof. For the equivalence of a), b) and c) we may use the previous result with 𝑊=𝑉 and note dim𝑘𝑊=dim𝑘𝑉 is automatically satisfied. ∎
Proposition 3.3.138 (Internal Direct Sum)

Let 𝑈1,𝑈2 be two subspaces of 𝑉 then TFAE

  1. 𝑈1∩𝑈2={0} and 𝑉=𝑈1+𝑈2
  2. Every 𝑣∈𝑉 may be written uniquely as 𝑢1+𝑢2 for 𝑢𝑖∈𝑈𝑖.

and we say 𝑉=𝑈1⊕𝑈2 is an internal direct sum and 𝑈2 is a supplementary subspace for 𝑈1.

Proposition 3.3.139 (Subspaces are direct factors)

Every subspace 𝑈 has a supplementary subspace 𝑈′ such that

𝑉=𝑈⊕𝑈′
Proof. Let ℬ︀1 be a basis for 𝑈 and extend to a basis ℬ︀ and define ℬ︀2≔ℬ︀∖ℬ︀1. Then it’s easy to show that 𝑈′=⟨ℬ︀2⟩ is a supplementary subspace. ∎
Proposition 3.3.140 (Dimension formula for direct sums)

Suppose 𝑉=𝑈1⊕𝑈2, ℬ︀1 is a basis for 𝑈1 and ℬ︀2 is a basis for 𝑈2. Then ℬ︀1∩ℬ︀2=∅ and ℬ︀1∪ℬ︀2 is a basis for 𝑉. In particular

dim𝑘𝑉=dim𝑘𝑈1+dim𝑘𝑈2
3.3.16.1 Dual Space
Definition 3.3.141 (Dual Space)

Let 𝑉 be a 𝑘-vector space and define the dual space to be

𝑉∨≔Hom𝑘(𝑉,𝑘)

This is an abelian group and even a 𝑘-vector space under the obvious operations. The construction 𝑉→𝑉∨ determines a contravariant functor

(−)∨:𝐕𝐞𝐜𝐭𝑘→𝐕𝐞𝐜𝐭𝑘
Definition 3.3.142 (Annihilator)

Let 𝑉 be a vector space and 𝑈⊆𝑉 a subspace. Define the annihilator of 𝑈 by

𝑈∘={𝜃∈𝑉∨|𝜃(𝑢)=0∀𝑢∈𝑈}

This is a linear subspace of 𝑉∨.

Proposition 3.3.143 (Dimension formula for annihilators)

There is a canonical isomorphism by restriction

𝑉∨/𝑈∘→𝑈∨

In particular when 𝑉 is a finite-dimensional vector space then

dim𝑘𝑉=dim𝑘𝑈+dim𝑘𝑈∘
Proof. Let 𝑊 be a supplementary subspace and consider the morphism 𝑉=𝑈⊕𝑊→𝜋𝑈𝑈. Then (𝜃∘𝜋𝑈)|𝑈=𝜃 so the restriction map is surjective. Clearly the kernel is 𝑈∘. The dimension formula follows from (3.3.135) and (3.3.109). ∎
Corollary 3.3.144 (Dual rank = rank)

Let 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 be a linear map and 𝜑∨:𝑊∨→𝑉∨ then

ker(𝜑∨)=Im(𝜑)∘Im(𝜑∨)⊆ker(𝜑)∘

In the finite-dimensional case Im(𝜑∨)=ker(𝜑)∘ and

dim𝑘ker(𝜑∨)=dim𝑘𝑊−rank𝑘(𝜑)rank𝑘(𝜑∨)=rank𝑘(𝜑)

Proof. Note ker(𝜑∨)=Im(𝜑)∘ and Im(𝜑∨)⊆ker(𝜑)∘ by the definitions.

Consider the finite-dimensional case. By (3.3.143)

dim𝑘ker(𝜑∨)=dim𝑘Im(𝜑)∘=dim𝑘𝑊−rank𝑘(𝜑)

By rank-nullity applied to 𝜑∨ and dim𝑘𝑊=dim𝑘𝑊∨ we deduce

rank𝑘(𝜑∨)=rank𝑘(𝜑).

By (3.3.143) and rank-nullity applied to 𝜑

dim𝑘ker(𝜑)∘=dim𝑘𝑉−dim𝑘ker(𝜑)=rank𝑘(𝜑).

Finally by (2.3.15) Im(𝜑∨)=ker(𝜑)∘. ∎

From this it follows that taking duals reflects and preserve isomorphisms

Corollary 3.3.145 ((−)∨ reflects isomorphisms)

Let 𝜑:𝑉→𝑊 be a linear map of finite-dimensional spaces then

  1. 𝜑 is injective if and only if 𝜑∨ is surjective
  2. 𝜑 is surjective if and only if 𝜑∨ is injective
  3. 𝜑 is iso if and only if 𝜑∨ is iso

Proof. Note by (3.3.144) we have rank𝑘(𝜑)=rank𝑘(𝜑∨) and by (3.3.109) dim𝑘𝑉∨=dim𝑘𝑉.

𝜑 is surjective ⟺rank𝑘(𝜑)=dim𝑘𝑊=rank𝑘(𝜑∨)⟺(3.3.135)dim𝑘ker(𝜑∨)=0⟺ker(𝜑∨)={0}

𝜑 is injective ⟺dim𝑘ker(𝜑)=0⟺(3.3.135)rank𝑘(𝜑)=dim𝑘𝑉⟺dim𝑘𝑉∨=rank𝑘(𝜑∨)⟺𝜑∨ is surjective.

The last point may be deduced from the first two, or the fact that (−)∨ is full and faithful (3.3.113) and category-theoretic result (2.6.42). ∎

3.3.16.2 Bilinear Pairings
Definition 3.3.146 (Bilinear maps)

Let 𝑉,𝑊 be vector spaces a bilinear map 𝜓 is a map

𝜓:𝑉×𝑊→𝑘

which is 𝑘-linear in each variable. We denote the set of bilinear maps as

Bilin𝑘(𝑉,𝑊)
Proposition 3.3.147 (Matrix Representation)

Let 𝑉,𝑊 be finite-dimensional vector spaces with bases {𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛} and {𝑤1,…,𝑤𝑚} then there is a canonical isomorphism

Bilin𝑘(𝑉,𝑊)⟶∼Mat𝑛,𝑚(𝑘)𝜓→(𝜓(𝑣𝑖,𝑤𝑗))𝑖𝑗

In particular a bilinear map 𝜓 is determined uniquely by the values 𝜓(𝑣𝑖,𝑤𝑗).

Proposition 3.3.148 (Dual maps)

Let 𝑉 and 𝑊 be vector spaces, then there is a natural bijection

Mor𝑘(𝑉,𝑊∨)↔Bilin𝑘(𝑉,𝑊)↔Mor𝑘(𝑊,𝑉∨)𝜓𝐿←𝜓𝜓→𝜓𝑅

where

𝜓𝐿(𝑣)(𝑤)=𝜓(𝑣,𝑤)=𝜓𝑅(𝑤)(𝑣)

When 𝑉,𝑊 are finite-dimensional then 𝜓𝐿 is an isomorphism if and only if 𝜓𝑅 is an isomorphism. In this case we say 𝜓 is a perfect pairing. More generally

rank𝑘(𝜓𝐿)=rank𝑘(𝜓𝑅)
Proof. The bijections stated are obvious. One may show that 𝜓𝐿=𝜓𝑅∨∘𝜂𝑉 where 𝜂𝑉:𝑉→𝑉∨∨ is the dual isomorphism. Therefore 𝜓𝐿 is an isomorphism if and only if 𝜓𝑅∨ is an isomorphism, and by (3.3.145) if and only if 𝜓𝑅 is an isomorphism. Since 𝜂𝑉 is surjective we have rank𝑘(𝜓𝐿)=rank𝑘(𝜓𝑅∨)=rank𝑘(𝜓𝑅), by (3.3.144). ∎
Proposition 3.3.149

Let 𝜓:𝑉×𝑉→𝑘 be a bilinear map. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝜓 is a perfect pairing
  2. 𝜓𝐿 is an isomorphism
  3. 𝜓𝑅 is an isomorphism
  4. 𝜓𝐿 is injective
  5. 𝜓𝑅 is injective
  6. 𝜓𝐿 is surjective
  7. 𝜓𝑅 is surjective.
Proof. We may use the fact dim𝑘𝑉=dim𝑘𝑉∨ and (3.3.136) to show b) ⟺ d) ⟺ f) and c) ⟺ e) ⟺ g). We’ve already shown that a) ⟺ b) ⟺ c). ∎
Definition 3.3.150 (Orthogonal Complement)

Let 𝜓:𝑉×𝑊→𝑘 be a perfect pairing of finite-dimensional vector spaces. Suppose 𝑈⊂𝑉 is a subspace then define the orthogonal complement

𝑈⟂≔{𝑤∈𝑊|𝜓(𝑣,𝑤)=0∀𝑣∈𝑈}
Proposition 3.3.151

Let 𝜓:𝑉×𝑊→𝑘 be a perfect pairing of finite-dimensional vector spaces and 𝑈⊂𝑉 a subspace. Then

dim𝑘𝑈+dim𝑘𝑈⟂=dim𝑘𝑉

Indeed 𝜓𝑅 induces an isomorphism 𝑈⟂→𝑈∘.

Proof. We claim that 𝜓𝑅(𝑈⟂)=𝑈∘. For if 𝑤∈𝑈⟂ then 𝜓𝑅(𝑤)(𝑣)=𝜓(𝑤,𝑣)=0 for all 𝑣∈𝑈, and so 𝜓𝑅(𝑤)∈𝑈∘. Conversely given 𝜃∈𝑈∘, as 𝜓𝑅 is surjective, there is 𝑤∈𝑊 such that 𝜓𝑅(𝑤)=𝜃. By definition 𝑤∈𝑈⟂ as required.

As 𝜓𝑅 is injective then dim𝑘𝑈⟂=(3.3.130)dim𝑘𝑈∘=(3.3.143)dim𝑘𝑉/𝑈=dim𝑘𝑉−dim𝑘𝑈. ∎

Remark 3.3.152

In the case 𝑉=𝑊, then it’s not necessarily true that 𝑈∩𝑈⟂={0}, and so 𝑈⟂ is not necessarily a complementary subspace.

The classic example is the perfect pairing on ℝ𝑛 induced by 𝑣𝐷𝑣𝑇 for a real diagonal matrix 𝐷. Then it’s true in general if and only if 𝐷 is positive-definite.

Proposition 3.3.153 (Quotients are dual to subspaces)

Let 𝜓:𝑉×𝑊→𝑘 be a perfect pairing of finite-dimensional vector spaces. Suppose 𝑈⊂𝑉 is a subspace, then there is a canonical perfect pairing

𝜓′:𝑉/𝑈×𝑈⟂→𝑘

given by

𝜓′(𝑣+𝑈,𝑤)=𝜓(𝑣,𝑤)

Proof. The given map is well defined, for suppose 𝑣1+𝑈=𝑣2+𝑈 then 𝑣1−𝑣2∈𝑈⟹𝜓(𝑣1−𝑣2,𝑤)=0∀𝑤∈𝑈⟂⟹𝜓(𝑣1,𝑤)=𝜓(𝑣2,𝑤) as required. It’s clearly 𝑘-bilinear.

It’s clear that 𝜓𝑅′ is injective, because 𝜓𝑅′(𝑤)=0𝑉/𝑈⟹𝜓𝑅(𝑤)=0𝑉⟹𝑤=0.

By the previous Proposition dim𝑘𝑈⟂=dim𝑘𝑉/𝑈. Therefore by (3.3.136) 𝜓𝑅′ is an isomorphism and 𝜓′ is perfect. ∎
