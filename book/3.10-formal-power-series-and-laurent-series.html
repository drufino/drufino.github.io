3.10 Formal Power Series and Laurent Series

Definition 3.10.1 (Formal Power Series)

Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring. Define the formal power series ring 𝐴[[𝑋]] to be the set of sequences (𝑐𝑛)𝑛≥0 in 𝐴, with componentwise addition and convolution product, written as formal sums

𝑓(𝑋)≔∑𝑛=0∞𝑐𝑛𝑋𝑛

so 𝐴[𝑋]⊂𝐴[[𝑋]] is the subring of finitely-supported sequences. If 𝐴 is an integral domain, so is 𝐴[[𝑋]], with 𝑣(𝑓𝑔)=𝑣(𝑓)+𝑣(𝑔) for 𝑣(𝑓)≔min{𝑛|𝑐𝑛≠0} (the coefficient of 𝑋𝑣(𝑓)+𝑣(𝑔) in 𝑓𝑔 is 𝑐𝑣(𝑓)𝑑𝑣(𝑔)≠0, every other contributing pair vanishing).

Proposition 3.10.2 (Units of a Formal Power Series Ring)
Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring and 𝑢∈𝐴[[𝑋]], with constant coefficient 𝑢0 (the coefficient of 𝑋0). If 𝑢0∈𝐴⋆ then 𝑢 is a unit of 𝐴[[𝑋]]. When 𝐴=𝑘 is a field, the converse holds too, so 𝑢∈𝑘[[𝑋]]⋆⟺𝑢0≠0.

Proof. Suppose 𝑢0∈𝐴⋆. Set 𝑤≔1−𝑢0−1𝑢, so 𝑤 has constant coefficient 0; then 𝑢0−1∑𝑖≥0𝑤𝑖 is a well-defined element of 𝐴[[𝑋]] (at each order 𝑛, only finitely many terms 𝑤𝑖 contribute, as 𝑣(𝑤)≥1), and 𝑢⋅𝑢0−1∑𝑖≥0𝑤𝑖=(1−𝑤)∑𝑖≥0𝑤𝑖=1 (telescoping), an explicit inverse of 𝑢.

For the converse when 𝐴=𝑘 is a field: if 𝑢 is a unit with inverse 𝑣, then 𝑢0𝑣0=(𝑢𝑣)0=1, so 𝑢0≠0; conversely 𝑢0≠0 in the field 𝑘 gives 𝑢0∈𝑘⋆, so the first paragraph applies. ∎

Lemma 3.10.3 (𝑒-th Roots of Units in a Formal Power Series Ring)
Let 𝑘 be a field, 𝑒≥1 an integer invertible in 𝑘 (i.e. char(𝑘)∤𝑒), and 𝑢∈𝑘[[𝑋]]⋆. If 𝑢(0) has an 𝑒-th root in 𝑘 (automatic if 𝑘 is algebraically closed), then 𝑢 has an 𝑒-th root in 𝑘[[𝑋]]⋆.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

Let 𝜆∈𝑘 with 𝜆𝑒=𝑢(0); replacing 𝑢 by 𝜆−𝑒𝑢 (whose 𝑒-th root, if found, gives one for 𝑢 after multiplying by 𝜆), we may assume 𝑢(0)=1. Construct 𝑦=1+∑𝑛≥1𝑦𝑛𝑋𝑛 recursively: set 𝑦(0)≔1, and given 𝑦(𝑛−1)≔1+∑𝑗=1𝑛−1𝑦𝑗𝑋𝑗 with (𝑦(𝑛−1))𝑒≡𝑢(mod𝑋𝑛) (trivial for 𝑛=1, as 𝑢(0)=1), write (𝑦(𝑛−1))𝑒=𝑢+𝑐𝑛𝑋𝑛+𝑂(𝑋𝑛+1) for some 𝑐𝑛∈𝑘; by the binomial theorem,

(𝑦(𝑛−1)+𝑦𝑛𝑋𝑛)𝑒≡(𝑦(𝑛−1))𝑒+𝑒(𝑦(𝑛−1))𝑒−1𝑦𝑛𝑋𝑛(mod𝑋𝑛+1)

(every other term has order ≥2𝑛≥𝑛+1), and (𝑦(𝑛−1))𝑒−1≡1(mod𝑋) (as 𝑦(𝑛−1)(0)=1), so the coefficient of 𝑋𝑛 on the right is 𝑐𝑛+𝑒𝑦𝑛. As 𝑒 is invertible in 𝑘, setting 𝑦𝑛≔−𝑐𝑛/𝑒 gives 𝑦(𝑛)≔𝑦(𝑛−1)+𝑦𝑛𝑋𝑛 with (𝑦(𝑛))𝑒≡𝑢(mod𝑋𝑛+1), continuing the induction. The resulting 𝑦≔1+∑𝑛≥1𝑦𝑛𝑋𝑛∈𝑘[[𝑋]] satisfies 𝑦𝑒=𝑢 (agreeing with 𝑢 to every order), and is a unit as 𝑦(0)=1≠0 (3.10.2). ∎

Definition 3.10.4 (Formal Laurent Series)
Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring. Define the formal Laurent series ring 𝐴((𝑋)) to be the set of sequences (𝑐𝑛)𝑛∈ℤ in 𝐴 bounded below, with the same convolution product; then 𝐴[[𝑋]]⊂𝐴((𝑋)) is the subring with 𝑐𝑛=0 for 𝑛<0. Write 𝑣(ℎ)≔min{𝑛|𝑐𝑛≠0} for 0≠ℎ=∑𝑛𝑐𝑛𝑋𝑛∈𝐴((𝑋)), and 𝑣(0)≔∞.
Proposition 3.10.5 (Summable Families in 𝐴((𝑋)))

Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring and (ℎ𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 a family in 𝐴((𝑋)) such that, for every 𝑁∈ℤ, all but finitely many 𝑖∈𝐼 satisfy 𝑣(ℎ𝑖)≥𝑁. Then

∑𝑖∈𝐼ℎ𝑖∈𝐴((𝑋))

is well-defined: for every 𝑚∈ℤ, only finitely many 𝑖∈𝐼 have nonzero coefficient of 𝑋𝑚 in ℎ𝑖, and the resulting sequence of coefficients (of ∑𝑖ℎ𝑖) is bounded below. Moreover, derivatives may be computed termwise,

(∑𝑖∈𝐼ℎ𝑖)′=∑𝑖∈𝐼ℎ𝑖′

and multiplication distributes over the sum: for any 𝑔∈𝐴((𝑋)), (𝑔ℎ𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 also meets the hypothesis, and

𝑔⋅∑𝑖∈𝐼ℎ𝑖=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔ℎ𝑖

Proof. Bounded below. Taking 𝑁≔0, all but finitely many 𝑖 have 𝑣(ℎ𝑖)≥0, that is ℎ𝑖∈𝐴[[𝑋]]; the remaining finitely many ℎ𝑖 are each individually bounded below (as elements of 𝐴((𝑋))), so ∑𝑖ℎ𝑖 is bounded below by the minimum of 0 and these finitely many bounds.

Local finiteness. Fix 𝑚∈ℤ and take 𝑁≔𝑚+1: all but finitely many 𝑖 have 𝑣(ℎ𝑖)≥𝑚+1>𝑚, that is coefficient of 𝑋𝑚 in ℎ𝑖 equal to 0; so only the finitely many remaining 𝑖 can contribute a nonzero coefficient of 𝑋𝑚, giving a well-defined finite sum ∑𝑖(ℎ𝑖)𝑚∈𝐴 for the coefficient of 𝑋𝑚 in ∑𝑖ℎ𝑖.

Termwise derivatives. Since 𝑣(ℎ𝑖′)≥𝑣(ℎ𝑖)−1 (differentiation shifts a coefficient at index 𝑛 to index 𝑛−1, scaled by 𝑛; it strictly cannot lower the least nonzero index by more than one), (ℎ𝑖′)𝑖∈𝐼 also meets the hypothesis (for every 𝑁, all but finitely many 𝑖 have 𝑣(ℎ𝑖)≥𝑁+1, hence 𝑣(ℎ𝑖′)≥𝑁), so ∑𝑖ℎ𝑖′ is itself well-defined by the case just proved. Fix 𝑚; by local finiteness applied to degree 𝑚+1, a finite set 𝐼0⊂𝐼 contains every 𝑖 with nonzero coefficient of 𝑋𝑚+1 in ℎ𝑖 – in particular every 𝑖 that can contribute to the coefficient of 𝑋𝑚 in either (∑𝑖ℎ𝑖)′ or ∑𝑖ℎ𝑖′, both computed from the coefficient of 𝑋𝑚+1 in the ℎ𝑖’s. Restricted to the finite set 𝐼0, differentiation and summation commute (both reduce to finite operations on coefficients), so the two coefficients of 𝑋𝑚 agree; as 𝑚 was arbitrary, (∑𝑖ℎ𝑖)′=∑𝑖ℎ𝑖′.

Distributivity. If 𝑔=0 both sides are 0; otherwise 𝑣(𝑔ℎ𝑖)≥𝑣(𝑔)+𝑣(ℎ𝑖) for every 𝑖 (every coefficient of 𝑔ℎ𝑖 below index 𝑣(𝑔)+𝑣(ℎ𝑖) vanishes, as any pair contributing to it has an index below 𝑣(𝑔) or below 𝑣(ℎ𝑖), where 𝑔, resp. ℎ𝑖, already vanishes). So, given 𝑁, taking 𝑁′≔𝑁−𝑣(𝑔) in the hypothesis on (ℎ𝑖), all but finitely many 𝑖 have 𝑣(ℎ𝑖)≥𝑁′, hence 𝑣(𝑔ℎ𝑖)≥𝑣(𝑔)+𝑁′=𝑁: the family (𝑔ℎ𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 meets the hypothesis too. For the equality, fix 𝑚; by local finiteness (applied to (ℎ𝑖), at every degree 𝑞 with 𝑔𝑚−𝑞≠0 and 𝑚−𝑞 within 𝑔’s finitely-many-relevant range, itself finite as convolution (𝑔⋅𝐻)𝑚 is already a finite sum for any 𝐻∈𝐴((𝑋)), (3.10.1)) only finitely many pairs (𝑖,𝑞) have 𝑔𝑚−𝑞(ℎ𝑖)𝑞≠0; both (𝑔⋅∑𝑖ℎ𝑖)𝑚=∑𝑞𝑔𝑚−𝑞(∑𝑖ℎ𝑖)𝑞=∑𝑞𝑔𝑚−𝑞∑𝑖(ℎ𝑖)𝑞 and (∑𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑖)𝑚=∑𝑖(𝑔ℎ𝑖)𝑚=∑𝑖∑𝑞𝑔𝑚−𝑞(ℎ𝑖)𝑞 reduce to the same finite double sum over these pairs (𝑖,𝑞), so agree; as 𝑚 was arbitrary, 𝑔⋅∑𝑖ℎ𝑖=∑𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑖. ∎

Proposition 3.10.6 (𝑘((𝑋)) is a Field of Fractions for 𝑘[[𝑋]])
Let 𝑘 be a field. Then 𝑘((𝑋)) is a field, and every element is 𝑎/𝑏 for 𝑎,𝑏∈𝑘[[𝑋]], 𝑏≠0 – that is, 𝑘((𝑋)) is a field of fractions for the subring 𝑘[[𝑋]]⊂𝑘((𝑋)). Moreover, the order function 𝑣:𝑘((𝑋))⋆→ℤ is a group homomorphism under multiplication: 𝑣(𝑓𝑔)=𝑣(𝑓)+𝑣(𝑔), and 𝑣(𝑓−1)=−𝑣(𝑓).

Proof. Every 𝑐≠0 in 𝑘((𝑋)) is 𝑐=𝑋𝑛0𝑢 for 𝑛0 its least nonzero index and 𝑢≔𝑋−𝑛0𝑐∈𝑘[[𝑋]] with nonzero constant coefficient; by (3.10.2), 𝑢 is a unit of 𝑘[[𝑋]], giving an explicit inverse 𝑋−𝑛0𝑢−1 of 𝑐 – so 𝑘((𝑋)) is a field. This same decomposition writes 𝑐 as 𝑢/𝑋−𝑛0 if 𝑛0<0, or as 𝑢𝑋𝑛0∈𝑘[[𝑋]] itself if 𝑛0≥0 – in either case a ratio of two elements of 𝑘[[𝑋]].

For 0≠𝑓,𝑔∈𝑘((𝑋)), write 𝑓=𝑋𝑛0𝑢, 𝑔=𝑋𝑚0𝑤 as above (𝑢,𝑤∈𝑘[[𝑋]]⋆); then 𝑓𝑔=𝑋𝑛0+𝑚0𝑢𝑤 with 𝑢𝑤∈𝑘[[𝑋]]⋆ again (nonzero constant coefficient, the product of those of 𝑢 and 𝑤), so 𝑣(𝑓𝑔)=𝑛0+𝑚0=𝑣(𝑓)+𝑣(𝑔). Applying this to 𝑓,𝑓−1: 0=𝑣(1)=𝑣(𝑓𝑓−1)=𝑣(𝑓)+𝑣(𝑓−1), giving 𝑣(𝑓−1)=−𝑣(𝑓). ∎

Proposition 3.10.7 (Formal Power Series Ring is a DVR)
Let 𝑘 be a field. Then 𝑘[[𝑋]] is a discrete valuation ring with fraction field 𝑘((𝑋)) (3.10.6), maximal ideal (𝑋), and valuation the order function 𝑣 (3.10.1), (3.10.4).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet 5, reviewed by David Rufino.

𝑘[[𝑋]] is a domain ((3.10.1), 𝑘 a domain) and not a field, as 𝑋≠0 has no inverse (𝑣(𝑋𝑔)=1+𝑣(𝑔)≠0 for every 𝑔∈𝑘[[𝑋]]). Given 0≠𝑎∈𝑘[[𝑋]] with 𝑛0≔𝑣(𝑎)≥0, write 𝑎=𝑋𝑛0𝑢 for 𝑢≔𝑋−𝑛0𝑎∈𝑘[[𝑋]] (well-defined as 𝑛0≥0), which has nonzero constant coefficient, so 𝑢∈𝑘[[𝑋]]⋆ (3.10.2): this is exactly the uniformised-element criterion (3.31.2)e), with 𝜋≔𝑋. ∎

Definition 3.10.8 (Formal Derivative)
Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring. For 𝑓=∑𝑛𝑐𝑛𝑋𝑛∈𝐴((𝑋)), define 𝑓′≔∑𝑛𝑛𝑐𝑛𝑋𝑛−1∈𝐴((𝑋)).
Proposition 3.10.9 (Product Rule)
Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring and 𝑓,𝑔∈𝐴((𝑋)). Then (𝑓𝑔)′=𝑓′𝑔+𝑓𝑔′.

Proof. Write 𝑓=∑𝑖𝑎𝑖𝑋𝑖, 𝑔=∑𝑗𝑏𝑗𝑋𝑗. The coefficient of 𝑋𝑛−1 in (𝑓𝑔)′ is 𝑛 times the coefficient of 𝑋𝑛 in 𝑓𝑔, that is 𝑛∑𝑖+𝑗=𝑛𝑎𝑖𝑏𝑗. The coefficient of 𝑋𝑛−1 in 𝑓′𝑔 is ∑𝑖+𝑗=𝑛(𝑖𝑎𝑖)𝑏𝑗 (as 𝑓′ has coefficient 𝑖𝑎𝑖 at degree 𝑖−1), and in 𝑓𝑔′ is ∑𝑖+𝑗=𝑛𝑎𝑖(𝑗𝑏𝑗). Summing,

∑𝑖+𝑗=𝑛𝑖𝑎𝑖𝑏𝑗+∑𝑖+𝑗=𝑛𝑎𝑖𝑗𝑏𝑗=∑𝑖+𝑗=𝑛(𝑖+𝑗)𝑎𝑖𝑏𝑗=𝑛∑𝑖+𝑗=𝑛𝑎𝑖𝑏𝑗

matching the coefficient of 𝑋𝑛−1 in (𝑓𝑔)′. As 𝑛 was arbitrary, (𝑓𝑔)′=𝑓′𝑔+𝑓𝑔′. ∎

Lemma 3.10.10 (Power Rule)
Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring and 𝜓∈𝐴((𝑋)). Then (𝜓𝑛)′=𝑛𝜓𝑛−1𝜓′ for every 𝑛≥0. If moreover 𝜓 is a unit of 𝐴((𝑋)), this also holds for every 𝑛<0.

Proof. 𝑛≥0. Induction on 𝑛: the case 𝑛=0 is 0=0 (𝜓0=1 is constant). Given (𝜓𝑛)′=𝑛𝜓𝑛−1𝜓′, the product rule (3.10.9) gives

(𝜓𝑛+1)′=(𝜓⋅𝜓𝑛)′=𝜓′𝜓𝑛+𝜓(𝜓𝑛)′=𝜓′𝜓𝑛+𝑛𝜓𝑛𝜓′=(𝑛+1)𝜓𝑛𝜓′

𝑛<0, 𝜓 a unit. Differentiating 𝜓𝜓−1=1 with the product rule, 0=𝜓′𝜓−1+𝜓(𝜓−1)′, so (𝜓−1)′=−𝜓−2𝜓′ – the case 𝑛=−1. For 𝑛≤−2, write 𝑛=−𝑟, 𝑟≥2: applying the 𝑛≥0 case just shown to the element 𝜓−1∈𝐴((𝑋)) (no hypothesis on its order was used there),

(𝜓−𝑟)′=((𝜓−1)𝑟)′=𝑟(𝜓−1)𝑟−1(𝜓−1)′=𝑟(𝜓−1)𝑟−1(−𝜓−2𝜓′)=−𝑟𝜓−𝑟−1𝜓′=𝑛𝜓𝑛−1𝜓′

 ∎

Proposition 3.10.11 (Substitution into a Formal Power Series)

Let 𝐴 be a commutative ring, 𝑓=∑𝑛≥0𝑐𝑛𝑋𝑛∈𝐴[[𝑋]], and 𝜓∈𝑋𝐴[[𝑋]] (that is, 𝜓(0)=0). Then

(𝑓∘𝜓)(𝑋)≔∑𝑛≥0𝑐𝑛𝜓(𝑋)𝑛

is a well-defined element of 𝐴[[𝑋]].

Proof. 𝜓∈𝑋𝐴[[𝑋]] means 𝜓𝑛∈𝑋𝑛𝐴[[𝑋]] for every 𝑛≥0 (a product of 𝑛 elements of 𝑋𝐴[[𝑋]]), that is 𝑣(𝜓𝑛)≥𝑛. So for every 𝑁, all 𝑛≥𝑁 satisfy 𝑣(𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛)≥𝑛≥𝑁: the family (𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛)𝑛≥0 meets (3.10.5)‘s hypothesis (only 𝑛<𝑁, finitely many, can fail it), giving ∑𝑛𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛∈𝐴[[𝑋]] (bounded below by 0, as every term already is). ∎
Proposition 3.10.12 (Chain Rule, Power Series)
In the setting of (3.10.11), (𝑓∘𝜓)′=(𝑓′∘𝜓)⋅𝜓′.

Proof. By the power rule (3.10.10), 𝑓∘𝜓=∑𝑛𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛 is a sum of the summable family (𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛)𝑛≥0 ((3.10.11)‘s proof), each term of which satisfies (𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛)′=𝑐𝑛(𝜓𝑛)′ (linearity of (−)′, immediate from (3.10.8)’s termwise formula, as 𝑐𝑛∈𝐴 is a constant). So, by termwise differentiation (3.10.5),

(𝑓∘𝜓)′=∑𝑛≥0(𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛)′=∑𝑛≥0𝑐𝑛(𝜓𝑛)′=∑𝑛≥0𝑛𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛−1𝜓′

The family (𝑛𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛−1)𝑛≥0 meets (3.10.5)‘s hypothesis by the same order bound as (𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛)𝑛≥0 ((3.10.11)’s proof, shifted down one degree), so distributivity (same reference) gives

∑𝑛≥0𝑛𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛−1𝜓′=(∑𝑛≥0𝑛𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛−1)𝜓′

Reindexing 𝑚=𝑛−1, ∑𝑛≥0𝑛𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛−1=∑𝑚≥0(𝑚+1)𝑐𝑚+1𝜓𝑚=𝑓′∘𝜓, giving (𝑓∘𝜓)′=(𝑓′∘𝜓)⋅𝜓′. ∎

Proposition 3.10.13 (Substitution into a Formal Laurent Series)

Let 𝑘 be a field, 𝑓=∑𝑛≥𝑛0𝑐𝑛𝑋𝑛∈𝑘((𝑋)), and 𝜓=𝑋𝑣∈𝑋𝑘[[𝑋]] for a unit 𝑣∈𝑘[[𝑋]]⋆ (that is, 𝜓 has invertible linear term, (3.10.2)). Then

(𝑓∘𝜓)(𝑋)≔∑𝑛≥𝑛0𝑐𝑛𝜓(𝑋)𝑛

is a well-defined element of 𝑘((𝑋)) (with 𝜓𝑛, 𝑛<0, the power of 𝜓 in the field 𝑘((𝑋)), (3.10.6)).

Proof. As 𝑣 is a group homomorphism under multiplication (3.10.6), 𝑣(𝜓𝑛)=𝑛⋅𝑣(𝜓)=𝑛 for every 𝑛∈ℤ, as 𝑣(𝜓)=1. Given 𝑁, every 𝑛≥max(𝑁,𝑛0) then satisfies 𝑣(𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛)≥𝑛≥𝑁: the family (𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛)𝑛≥𝑛0 meets (3.10.5)‘s hypothesis (only 𝑛0≤𝑛<𝑁, finitely many, can fail it), giving ∑𝑛𝑐𝑛𝜓𝑛∈𝑘((𝑋)), bounded below by 𝑛0 (as every term already is). ∎
Proposition 3.10.14 (Chain Rule, Laurent Series)
In the setting of (3.10.13), (𝑓∘𝜓)′=(𝑓′∘𝜓)⋅𝜓′.

Proof. Lemma 3.10.15 (Substitution is Multiplicative)

Lemma 3.10.16 (Substitution is Additive)
