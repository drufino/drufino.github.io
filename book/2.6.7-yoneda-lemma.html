2.6.7 Yoneda Lemma

The motivation for the following result becomes more clear in the next section. Roughly speaking properties of an object, 𝑋, are encoded in the functor Mor(𝑋,−).

Proposition 2.6.58 (Yoneda Lemma)

Suppose 𝑋,𝑋′∈𝒞︀ then there is a bijection between morphisms and natural transformation of functors

Mor(𝑋′,𝑋)⟶∼Nat(Mor(𝑋,−),Mor(𝑋′,−))𝑓⟶Mor(𝑓,−):(𝜙→𝜙∘𝑓)𝛼𝑋(1𝑋)⟵𝛼

Observe that under this correspondence

  • 𝑓=Mor(𝑓,𝑋)(1𝑋)
  • Mor(𝑓∘𝑔,−)=Mor(𝑔,−)∘Mor(𝑓,−)
  • 𝑓 is an isomorphism ⟺Mor(𝑓,−) is a natural isomorphism.

In the latter case Mor(𝑓,−)−1=Mor(𝑓−1,−)

In many cases the set of morphisms Mor(𝑋,𝑋′) has an additional structure (typically an abelian group or module). We encode this with the following definition

Definition 2.6.59 (Enriched Hom Functor)

Let (𝒮︀,𝑈) be a concrete category (2.6.9) for which 𝑈 reflects isomorphisms. We say a bifunctor

Hom:𝒞︀op×𝒞︀→𝒮︀

is an enriched hom functor if we have

  • 𝑈(Hom(𝑋,𝑌))=Mor(𝑋,𝑌)
  • 𝑈(Hom(𝑓,𝑌))=Mor(𝑓,𝑌)
  • 𝑈(Hom(𝑋,𝑔))=Mor(𝑋,𝑔)

and bijections

Write the inverse of 𝑓→Hom(𝑓,−) as 𝒴︀. Then for 𝛼∈Nat(Hom(𝑋,−),Hom(𝑋′,−))

  1. 𝛼 is a natural isomorphism ⟺𝑈𝛼 is a natural isomorphism ⟺𝒴︀(𝛼) is an isomorphism.
  2. 𝒴︀(𝛼)=𝑈(𝛼𝑋)(1𝑋)

Note the right hand arrow is always a bijection by Yoneda’s Lemma (2.6.58) and the diagram always commutes by assumption.

Proof. Suppose 𝛼 is a natural isomorphism then 𝑈𝛼−1∘𝑈𝛼=𝑈(1Hom(𝑋,−))=1Mor(𝑋,−) and similarly 𝑈𝛼∘𝑈𝛼−1=1Mor(𝑋′,−), so 𝑈𝛼 is a natural isomorphism. ∎

Note this trivially includes the usual case 𝒮︀=𝐒𝐞𝐭.
