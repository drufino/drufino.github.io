5.4 Normed Vector Spaces

Definition 5.4.1 (Valued Field)

Let 𝐾 be a field. A valuation on 𝐾 is a function

|⋅|:𝐾→ℝ≥0

such that for all 𝑥,𝑦∈𝐾 the following relations are satisfied

  1. |𝑥|=0⟺𝑥=0
  2. |𝑥𝑦|=|𝑥||𝑦|
  3. |𝑥+𝑦|≤|𝑥|+|𝑦|

We say that this valuation is non-archimedean if it satisfies the ultrametric inequality

|𝑥+𝑦|≤max(|𝑥|,|𝑦|)

for all 𝑥,𝑦∈𝐾. Otherwise it is archimedean.

We say the pair (𝐾,|⋅|) is a valued field.

Example 5.4.2
Any subfield of ℂ (and therefore ℝ) is an archimedean valued field, using the complex absolute value |⋅| (5.2.2).
Definition 5.4.3 (Normed Vector Space)

Let (𝐾,|⋅|) be a valued field and 𝑋 a 𝐾-vector space. A norm ‖⋅‖ is a function

‖⋅‖:𝑋→ℝ≥0

satisfying the following properties

  1. ‖𝑥‖=0⟺𝑥=0
  2. ‖𝜆𝑥‖=|𝜆|‖𝑥‖
  3. ‖𝑥+𝑦‖≤‖𝑥‖+‖𝑦‖

We say the pair (𝑋,‖⋅‖) is a normed vector space over the valued field (𝐾,|⋅|).

A normed vector space which is complete as a metric space is called a Banach space.

A Banach space 𝑋 which is also an associative algebra satisfying

‖𝑥⋅𝑦‖≤‖𝑥‖‖𝑦‖

is a Banach Algebra. Note a valued field is naturally a Banach Algebra.

Example 5.4.4

A valued field (e.g. ℂ, ℝ, ℚ) is a normed vector space over itself.

ℂ is a normed vector space over ℝ.

For any valued field 𝐾 the vector space 𝐾𝑛 is a normed vector space with the following definition of norm

‖𝑣‖≔𝑣12+⋯+𝑣𝑛2

(the triangle inequality requires proof).

Proposition 5.4.5

Let (𝑋,‖⋅‖) a normed vector space. Then it is naturally a metric space with induced metric

𝑑(𝑥,𝑦)≔‖𝑥−𝑦‖

and in particular a Hausdorff topological space with base the open balls

𝐵(𝑥;𝜀)≔{𝑦∈𝑋|‖𝑦−𝑥‖<𝜀}

Furthermore the open balls 𝐵(𝑥;𝜀) and 𝐵(𝑥;1𝑛) form a local base at 𝑥.

The same statement applies to valued fields and in particular ℝ.

5.4.1 Continuous Functions

Proposition 5.4.6 (Continuous maps of Normed Vector Spaces)

Let 𝑆⊂𝑋 be a subset of a normed vector space and 𝑓:𝑆→𝑌 be a map to a normed vector space 𝑌 and 𝑥∈𝑆. Then the following are equivalent

  1. For all 𝜀>0 there exists 𝛿>0 such that for all 𝑥′∈𝑆, ‖𝑥−𝑥′‖<𝛿⟹‖𝑓(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑥′)‖<𝜀
  2. 𝑓 is continuous at 𝑥 with respect to the subspace topology on 𝑆

In this case we say the map 𝑓 is continuous at 𝑥.

Example 5.4.7
For 𝑋 a normed vector space then ‖⋅‖:𝑋→ℝ is evidently continuous.

5.4.2 Product Space

Definition 5.4.8 (Product Norm)

Let 𝑋1,⋯,𝑋𝑛 be normed vector spaces over the same valued field 𝐾. Then we may define the product norm on the vector space 𝑋1×⋯×𝑋𝑛 by

‖(𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑛)‖≔max(‖𝑥1‖,⋯,‖𝑥𝑛‖)
Proposition 5.4.9
Let 𝑋1,⋯,𝑋𝑛 be normed vector spaces over the same valued field 𝐾. Then the metric (resp. topology) induced by product norm is the same as product metric (resp. product topology).

Proof. Consider a point 𝑥=(𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑛) then

𝐵(𝑥;𝜀)={(𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑛)|‖𝑥𝑖‖≤𝜀}=𝐵(𝑥1;𝜀)×⋯×𝐵(𝑥𝑛;𝜀)

Therefore the open balls are open in the product topology, and being a base for the norm topology, this is a subset of the product topology (…). To show that the product topology is a subset of the norm topology it is sufficient to show that open sets of the form

𝑈≔𝑈1×⋯×𝑈𝑛

are open in the product norm topology. For given 𝑦∈𝑈 then by definition 𝐵(𝑦𝑖;𝜀𝑖)⊂𝑈𝑖. Consider 𝜀≔min(𝜀1,⋯,𝜀𝑛) and 𝑧∈𝐵(𝑦;𝜀). Then

‖𝑧−𝑦‖<𝜀⟹max𝑖(‖𝑧𝑖−𝑦𝑖‖)<𝜀≤𝜀𝑖⟹𝑧𝑖∈𝐵(𝑦𝑖;𝜀𝑖)⟹𝑧∈𝑈

in other words 𝐵(𝑦;𝜀)⊂𝑈 as required. ∎

Proposition 5.4.10 (Arithmetic Operations are Continuous)

Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space over a valued field 𝐾. The following maps are continuous

  1. addition +:𝑋×𝑋→𝑋
  2. scalar multiplication ⋅:𝐾×𝑋→𝑋
  3. inverse scalar (−)−1:𝐾∖{0}→𝐾

Similarly if 𝑋 is a Banach algebra then the multiplication operator

⋅:𝑋×𝑋→𝐾

is also continuous.

Proof. By the triangle inequality

‖(𝑥1,𝑥2)−(𝑦1,𝑦2)‖<𝜀⟹‖𝑥1−𝑦1‖<𝜀∧‖𝑥2−𝑦2‖<𝜀⟹‖(𝑥1+𝑥2)−(𝑦1+𝑦2)‖<2𝜀

Therefore we may apply the criterion given by (5.4.6), and using the product norm.

For scalar multiplication observe

‖(𝜆1,𝑥1)−(𝜆2,𝑥2)‖<𝛿⟹|𝜆1−𝜆2|<𝛿∧‖𝑥1−𝑥2‖<𝛿⟹‖𝜆1𝑥1−𝜆2𝑥2‖≤|𝜆1|‖𝑥1−𝑥2‖+‖𝑥2‖|𝜆1−𝜆2|⟹‖𝜆1𝑥1−𝜆2𝑥2‖≤|𝜆1|‖𝑥1−𝑥2‖+(‖𝑥1‖+𝛿)|𝜆1−𝜆2|⟹‖𝜆1𝑥1−𝜆2𝑥2‖≤𝛿2+(‖𝑥1‖+𝛿)𝛿

Therefore choose 𝛿<min(1,𝜀‖𝑥1‖+1) to find ‖𝜆1𝑥1−𝜆2𝑥2‖<3𝜀. ∎

5.4.3 Convergent Sequences

Proposition 5.4.11 (Convergent Sequence)

Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space and (𝑎𝑛) a sequence in 𝑋. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑎𝑛→𝛼 in the topological sense (4.1.49)

  2. For all 𝜀>0 there exists 𝑁(𝜀)∈ℕ+ such that

    𝑛≥𝑁(𝜀)⟹‖𝑎𝑛−𝛼‖<𝜀

  3. The function

    ℕ∪{∞}→𝑋𝑛→𝑎𝑛∞→𝛼

    is continuous with respect to the topology on ℕ∪{∞} given by open sets of the form {𝑁,𝑁+1,⋯}∪{∞} and arbitrary subsets of ℕ.

In this case we say the sequence (𝑎𝑛) is convergent. If this doesn’t hold it is divergent.

Proposition 5.4.12 (Uniqueness of Limits)
Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space. Then it is Hausdorff and limits are unique.
Proposition 5.4.13 (Continuous Image of Convergent Sequence)

Let 𝑋,𝑌 be normed vector spaces, 𝑆⊂𝑋 and 𝑓:𝑆→𝑌 a map. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑓 is continuous in the topological sense
  2. For every convergent sequence 𝑎𝑛→𝛼 we have 𝑓(𝑎𝑛)→𝑓(𝛼).
Proof. Recall that 𝑋 is first-countable and so the equivalence follows from (4.1.50) and (4.1.52). ∎
Proposition 5.4.14 (Arithmetic of Convergent Sequences in an NVS)

Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space and (𝑎𝑛) and (𝑏𝑛) be convergent sequences in 𝑋. Then

lim𝑛→∞(𝑎𝑛+𝑏𝑛)=lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛+lim𝑛→∞𝑏𝑛lim𝑛→∞𝜆𝑎𝑛=𝜆lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛

In particular 𝑎𝑛→𝛼⟺𝑎𝑛−𝛼→0.

Proposition 5.4.15 (Arithmetic of Convergent Sequences in a valued field)

Let 𝐾 be a valued field and (𝑎𝑛) and (𝑏𝑛) be convergent sequences. Then

lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛𝑏𝑛=(lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛)(lim𝑛→∞𝑏𝑛)

Further if both 𝑏𝑛 and lim𝑛→∞𝑏𝑛 is non-zero then

lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛𝑏𝑛=lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛lim𝑛→∞𝑏𝑛
Proposition 5.4.16 (Arithmetic of Convergent Sequences in an ordered field)
Let 𝐾 be an ordered field and (𝑏𝑛) a sequence of non-negative elements. Then 𝑏𝑛→∞⟺𝑏𝑛−1→0.
Proposition 5.4.17

Let 𝐾 be a valued field and 𝑥∈𝐾 such that |𝑥|<1. Then

lim𝑛→∞𝑥𝑛=0
Proof. We may reduce to the case that 𝑥∈ℝ and 0<𝑥<1. Let 𝑦=1−𝑥𝑥>0, then it is enough to show that (1+𝑦)𝑛→∞ by (5.4.16). However from (5.1.30) (1+𝑦)𝑛≥1+𝑛𝑦, and the result follows from Archimedean property (…). ∎
Definition 5.4.18 (Cauchy Sequence)

Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space and (𝑎𝑛)𝑛∈ℕ a sequence in 𝑋. We say (𝑎𝑛) is a cauchy sequence if for all 𝜀>0 there exists 𝑁(𝜀) such that

𝑛,𝑚≥𝑁(𝜀)⟹‖𝑎𝑛−𝑎𝑚‖<𝜀

Every convergent sequence is cauchy. If the converse holds then we say 𝑋 is complete.

5.4.4 Finite-Dimensional Normed Spaces

Definition 5.4.19 (Equivalent Norms)

Let 𝑋 be a vector space over a valued field 𝐾. Consider two norms ‖⋅‖1 and ‖⋅‖2. We say that they are equivalent if there exists constants 𝑐1,𝑐2>0 such that

𝑐1‖𝑥‖1≤‖𝑥‖2≤𝑐2‖𝑥‖1

We may prove easily that this is an equivalence relation on norms.

Proposition 5.4.20

Let 𝑋 be a vector space over a valued field 𝐾. Suppose that ‖⋅‖1 and ‖⋅‖2 are equivalent norms for 𝑋. Then the following derived quantities are the same

  • Induced topology
  • Convergent sequences
  • Cauchy sequences
Proposition 5.4.21 (Supremum Norm)

Let 𝑋 be a finite-dimensional vector space with basis (𝑒1,⋯,𝑒𝑛). Then the supremum norm defined by

‖∑𝑖=1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑒𝑖‖∞≔max(|𝜆1|,⋯,|𝜆𝑛|)

is well-defined (it is the product norm on 𝑋≅𝐾×⋯×𝐾). If 𝐾 is complete then so is 𝑋.

Proposition 5.4.22 (Equivalence of Norms)
Let 𝑋 be a non-zero finite-dimensional vector space over a complete valued field 𝐾. Then every norm is equivalent to the supremum norm (and therefore complete).

Proof. Fix a basis (𝑒1,⋯,𝑒𝑛). Then for any norm ‖⋅‖ we have by the triangle inequality

‖∑𝑖=1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑒𝑖‖≤∑𝑖=1𝑛|𝜆𝑖|‖𝑒𝑖‖≤(∑𝑖=1𝑛‖𝑒𝑖‖)‖∑𝑖=1𝑛𝜆𝑖𝑒𝑖‖∞

The reverse inequality we prove by induction. The case 𝑛=1 we may take 𝑐1=‖𝑒1‖. Suppose 𝑛>1 and there is no such constant 𝐶. Then there are vectors 𝑣𝑘∈𝑋 such that

‖𝑣𝑘‖<1𝑘‖𝑣𝑘‖∞

Suppose

𝑣𝑘=∑𝑖=1𝑛𝜆𝑘𝑖𝑒𝑖

Consider the sets of integers

𝑆𝑖≔{𝑘∈ℕ|‖𝑣𝑘‖∞=|𝜆𝑘𝑖|}𝑖=1⋯𝑛

Then at least one of 𝑆1,⋯,𝑆𝑛 is infinite, since the union is ℕ. Without loss of generality we assume it is 𝑆𝑛.

Therefore by passing to a subsequence, and scaling, we may assume that ‖𝑣𝑘‖∞=𝜆𝑘𝑛=1 and ‖𝑣𝑘‖<1𝑘. Define the vector 𝑤𝑘≔𝑣𝑘−𝑒𝑛. Then by definition 𝑤𝑘→𝑒𝑛 with respect to ‖⋅‖.

Let 𝑊 be the (𝑛−1)-dimensional subspace spanned by {𝑒1,⋯,𝑒𝑛−1}. We prove also that 𝑤𝑘→𝑤∈𝑊 with respect to ‖⋅‖, which contradicts uniqueness of limits. Evidently 𝑤𝑘 is a cauchy sequence with respect to ‖⋅‖ restricted to 𝑊. By induction ‖⋅‖∞ is equivalent to ‖⋅‖ on 𝑊, and therefore 𝑤𝑘 is cauchy with respect to ‖⋅‖∞. As 𝑊 is complete (5.4.21) we see that 𝑤𝑘→𝑤 with respect to ‖⋅‖∞, and therefore with respect to ‖⋅‖. ∎

Corollary 5.4.23

Let 𝑋 be a finite dimensional normed vector space with basis {𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑛}. The projection map

𝜋𝑖:𝑋→⟨𝑥𝑖⟩→∼𝐾

given by 𝜋𝑖(𝑥)=𝑥𝑖∨(𝑥)𝑥𝑖 is a continuous, open map.

5.4.5 Convergent Series

Definition 5.4.24 (Series)

Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space and (𝑎𝑛) a sequence in 𝑋. The formal expression

∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛

is called the series associated to this sequence. For such a sequence define the partial sum sequence as follows

𝑠𝑛≔∑𝑘=1𝑛𝑎𝑘

If the partial sum sequence converges then we say that the series converges and write

∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛≔lim𝑛→∞𝑠𝑛

If the series

∑𝑛=1∞‖𝑎𝑛‖

converges then we say the original series converges absolutely.

The choice of terminology is justified in the case that 𝑋 is complete.

Proposition 5.4.25 (Absolute Convergence ⟹ Convergence)
Let 𝑋 be a complete normed vector space and ∑𝑛=0∞𝑎𝑛 a series which converges absolutely. Then the series converges in the usual sense.
Proof. The partial sums of the series ∑𝑛=1∞‖𝑎𝑛‖ are cauchy by (5.4.18). By the triangle inequality we may demonstrate that the partial sums of the series ∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛 are cauchy. Then by completeness of 𝑋 the partial sums must also converge. ∎
Proposition 5.4.26

Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space and ∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛 and ∑𝑛=1∞𝑏𝑛 two convergent sequences. Then the following series are convergent with limits

∑𝑛=1∞(𝑎𝑛+𝑏𝑛)=∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛+∑𝑛=1∞𝑏𝑛∑𝑛=1∞𝜆𝑎𝑛=𝜆∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛

where 𝜆∈𝐾.

Proposition 5.4.27
Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space and ∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛 a convergent series. Then ‖𝑎𝑛‖→0.
Proof. Let 𝛼≔∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛. Then by definition there exists 𝑁 such that 𝑛≥𝑁⟹‖∑𝑘=1𝑛𝑎𝑘−𝛼‖<𝜀. As this holds for 𝑛+1 we may use the triangle inequality to find that 𝑛≥𝑁+1⟹‖𝑎𝑛‖<2𝜀. As 𝜀 was arbitrary we find ‖𝑎𝑛‖→0 as required. ∎
Proposition 5.4.28 (Comparison Test)

Let 𝑋 be a complete normed vector space. Suppose (𝑎𝑛) is a sequence in 𝑋 and (𝑐𝑛) is a sequence in ℝ such that

  1. The series ∑𝑛=0∞𝑐𝑛 converges
  2. ‖𝑎𝑛‖≤𝑐𝑛 for 𝑛≥𝑁0

then the series ∑𝑛=0∞𝑎𝑛 converges absolutely.

Proof. By assumption 𝑏) (𝑐𝑛) is non-negative, and by (5.4.29) the sequence of partial sums ∑𝑘=0𝑛𝑐𝑘 is bounded. therefore so is the sequence ∑𝑘=0𝑛‖𝑎𝑘‖, and by the same result the series ∑𝑛=0∞‖𝑎𝑛‖ converges, in other words ∑𝑛=0∞𝑎𝑛 converges absolutely. ∎
Proposition 5.4.29 (Series of Positive Reals)

Let (𝑎𝑛) be a sequence of non-negative real numbers. Then the following are equivalent

  1. The sequence of partial sums 𝐴𝑛≔∑𝑘=0𝑛𝑎𝑘 is bounded
  2. The series ∑𝑛=0∞𝑎𝑛 converges (absolutely)
Proof. By (5.1.23) we always have 𝐴𝑛→sup𝐴𝑛∈ℝ♯, and the limit is finite iff 𝐴𝑛 is bounded. ∎
Proposition 5.4.30 (Geometric Series)

Suppose 0≤𝑥<1 is a real number, then

∑𝑛=0∞𝑥𝑛=11−𝑥

and in particular the partial sums are bounded by 11−𝑥.

Proof. It’s straightforward to show by induction that the partial sums are given explicitly by

∑𝑛=0𝑁𝑥𝑛=1−𝑥𝑁1−𝑥.

Then the result follows from (5.4.17). ∎

Proposition 5.4.31 (Root Test)

Let 𝑋 be a normed vector space. Given a series ∑𝑛=0∞𝑎𝑛, define 𝛼≔lim sup𝑛‖𝑎𝑛‖𝑛 Then

  1. if 𝛼<1 then the series converges absolutely
  2. if 𝛼>1 then the series diverges

Proof.

  1. Define 𝛼≔lim sup𝑛‖𝑎𝑛‖𝑛 and 𝛽≔1+𝛼2<1. Then by definition there is an integer 𝑁 such that

    𝑛≥𝑁⟹‖𝑎𝑛‖𝑛≤𝛼+1−𝛼2=𝛽⟹‖𝑎𝑛‖𝑛≤𝛽𝑛

    In particular for 𝑛≥𝑁

    ∑𝑘=0𝑛‖𝑎𝑘‖≤∑𝑛=0𝑁−1‖𝑎𝑘‖+∑𝑘=𝑁𝑛‖𝑎𝑘‖≤∑𝑘=0𝑁−1‖𝑎𝑘‖+∑𝑘=𝑁𝑛𝛽𝑘≤∑𝑘=0𝑁−1‖𝑎𝑘‖+∑𝑘=0∞𝛽𝑘

    where the last step follows from by (5.4.30). We conclude from (5.4.29) that the series converges absolutely.

  2. By (5.1.35) there exists a subsequence 𝑎𝑛𝑘 such that ‖𝑎𝑛𝑘‖𝑛𝑘→𝛼>1. Therefore we may find a subsequence such that ‖𝑎𝑛𝑘‖𝑛𝑘≥1+𝛼2>1⟹‖𝑎𝑛𝑘‖>1. This shows that ‖𝑎𝑛𝑘‖↛0, whence ‖𝑎𝑛‖↛0 by (…), and the series diverges by (5.4.27).

 ∎

Proposition 5.4.32 (Ratio Test)

Let 𝐾 be a valued field and consider a series ∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛. If

lim sup𝑛|𝑎𝑛+1𝑎𝑛|<1

then the series is convergent.

Proof. Let 𝛼≔lim sup|𝑎𝑛+1𝑎𝑛| and 𝛽=1+𝛼2. Then by assumption 𝛽<1, and there exists 𝑁 such that 𝑛≥𝑁⟹|𝑎𝑛+1𝑎𝑛|≤𝛽⟹|𝑎𝑁+𝑘|≤|𝑎𝑁|𝛽𝑘. We then see by Comparison Test (5.4.28) that the series is convergent. ∎
Proposition 5.4.33 (Cauchy Product of Series)

Let 𝑋 be a complete valued field and ∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛, ∑𝑛=1∞𝑏𝑛, ∑𝑛=1∞𝑐𝑛 series such that

  1. one of ∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛 and ∑𝑛=1∞𝑏𝑛 are absolutely convergent
  2. 𝑐𝑛≔∑𝑘=0𝑛𝑎𝑘𝑏𝑛−𝑘=∑𝑘=0𝑛𝑎𝑛−𝑘𝑏𝑘

then ∑𝑛=0∞𝑐𝑛 is absolutely convergent and

∑𝑛=0∞𝑐𝑛=(∑𝑛=1∞𝑎𝑛)(∑𝑛=1∞𝑏𝑛)

5.4.6 Function Spaces

Definition 5.4.34 (Uniform Convergence)

Let 𝑆 be a metric space and 𝑌 a normed vector space. Suppose {𝑓𝑛:𝑆→𝑌} is a sequence of functions and 𝑓:𝑆→𝑌 is a function. We say that 𝑓𝑛→𝑓 converges uniformly if

∀𝜀>0∃𝑁s.t.𝑛≥𝑁⟹‖𝑓𝑛(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑥)‖<𝜀∀𝑥∈𝑆

Note this implies, but is strictly stronger than, pointwise convergence that is 𝑓𝑛(𝑥)→𝑓(𝑥) for all 𝑥∈𝑆, as 𝑁 may be chosen independently of 𝑥.

Proposition 5.4.35 (Uniform Limit of Continuous Functions is Continuous)
Let 𝑆 be a metric space and 𝑌 a normed vector space. Suppose that {𝑓𝑛:𝑆→𝑌} is a sequence of continuous functions converging uniformly to a limit 𝑓:𝑆→𝑌. Then 𝑓 is continuous.

Proof. Fix 𝜀>0. There exists 𝑁 such that

𝑛≥𝑁⟹‖𝑓𝑛(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑥)‖<𝜀∀𝑥∈𝑆

Fix 𝑥∈𝑆 then there exists 𝛿>0 such that

‖𝑦−𝑥‖<𝛿⟹‖𝑓𝑛(𝑦)−𝑓𝑛(𝑥)‖<𝜀

Therefore

‖𝑦−𝑥‖<𝛿⟹‖𝑓(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑦)‖≤‖𝑓(𝑥)−𝑓𝑛(𝑥)‖+‖𝑓𝑛(𝑥)−𝑓𝑛(𝑦)‖+‖𝑓(𝑦)−𝑓𝑛(𝑦)‖<3𝜀

Consequently 𝑓 is also continuous. ∎

Definition 5.4.36 (Bounded Function Space)

Let 𝑆 be a metric space and 𝑌 a normed vector space. Define the vector space of bounded functions by

ℬ︀(𝑆,𝑌)≔{𝑓:𝑆→𝑌|∃𝐶>0s.t.‖𝑓(𝑥)‖≤𝐶for all𝑥∈𝑆}

This is a normed vector space under the sup norm given by

‖𝑓‖∞≔sup𝑥∈𝑆‖𝑓(𝑥)‖

Evidently convergence in this norm is equivalent to uniform convergence.

Proposition 5.4.37 (Bounded Functions are Complete)
Suppose 𝑌 is a Banach Space, then ℬ︀(𝑆,𝑌) is also a Banach Space.

Proof. Let {𝑓𝑛} be a cauchy sequence of maps 𝑆→𝑌 with respect to the sup norm. Then for every 𝜀>0 there exists 𝑁 such that

𝑛,𝑚≥𝑁⟹‖𝑓𝑛−𝑓𝑚‖<𝜀

In particular for every 𝑥∈𝑆 we have {𝑓𝑛(𝑥)} is cauchy. As 𝑌 is complete it is a convergent sequence, for which we denote the limit by 𝑓(𝑥). For every 𝑥∈𝑆 we may also choose some 𝑚≥𝑁 such that

‖𝑓𝑚(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑥)‖<𝜀

then

‖𝑓(𝑥)−𝑓𝑛(𝑥)‖≤‖𝑓(𝑥)−𝑓𝑚(𝑥)‖+‖𝑓𝑚(𝑥)−𝑓𝑛(𝑥)‖<2𝜀

As this inequality is independent of 𝑚 we find that ‖𝑓−𝑓𝑛‖→0 as required. We may also show easily that it is bounded. ∎

5.4.7 Continuous Linear Maps

Proposition 5.4.38 (Continuous Linear Maps)

Let 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 be a linear map of normed vector spaces. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑓 is continuous

  2. There exists a constant 𝐶>0 such that

    ‖𝑓(𝑥)‖≤𝐶‖𝑥‖

  3. 𝑓 is bounded on the unit sphere

    sup{‖𝑓(𝑥)‖𝑌|‖𝑥‖𝑋=1}<∞

When 𝑋 is finite-dimensional then every linear map is continuous.

Proof. Suppose 𝑏) holds then 𝑓 is even uniformly continuous because

‖𝑥−𝑦‖<𝜀𝐶⟹‖𝑓(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑦)‖<𝜀

Conversely given 𝑎) there exists 𝛿 such that

‖𝑥‖≤𝛿⟹‖𝑓(𝑥)‖<1

Therefore

‖𝑓(𝛿𝑥‖𝑥‖)‖<1⟹‖𝑓(𝑥)‖<1𝛿‖𝑥‖

and we may take 𝐶=1𝛿. Given 𝑏) evidently ‖𝑓‖≤𝐶<∞, and conversely ‖𝑓(𝑥)‖≤‖𝑓‖‖𝑥‖. ∎

Proposition 5.4.39

Let 𝑋,𝑌 be normed vector spaces. Denote by 𝐿(𝑋,𝑌) the set of continuous linear maps. It is a normed vector space, with norm given by

‖𝜃‖≔sup{‖𝜃(𝑥)‖𝑌|‖𝑥‖𝑋=1}

Furthermore if 𝑌 is complete, so is 𝐿(𝑋,𝑌).

Proof. Evidently ‖𝜆𝜃‖=|𝜆|‖𝜃‖ and ‖𝜃1+𝜃2‖≤‖𝜃1‖+‖𝜃2‖<∞. Let {𝜃𝑛} be a cauchy sequence in 𝐿(𝑋,𝑌). Given 𝑥∈𝑋 then

‖𝜃𝑛(𝑥)−𝜃𝑚(𝑥)‖≤‖𝜃𝑛−𝜃𝑚‖‖𝑥‖

whence {𝜃𝑛(𝑥)} is a cauchy sequence, and therefore converges to 𝜃(𝑥). By uniqueness of limits we see that 𝜃 is linear. Furthermore by the reverse triangle inequality (…)

|‖𝜃𝑛‖−‖𝜃𝑚‖|≤‖𝜃𝑛−𝜃𝑚‖

in otherwords ‖𝜃𝑛‖ is a cauchy sequence and converges to some limit 𝐶. Suppose that ‖𝑥‖=1 then

‖𝜃(𝑥)‖≤‖𝜃(𝑥)−𝜃𝑛(𝑥)‖+‖𝜃𝑛(𝑥)‖≤‖𝜃(𝑥)−𝜃𝑛(𝑥)‖+‖𝜃𝑛‖

Choose 𝑁1 such that 𝑛≥𝑁1⟹‖𝜃𝑛‖≤𝐶+𝜀 and 𝑁2 such that 𝑛≥𝑁2⟹‖𝜃(𝑥)−𝜃𝑛(𝑥)‖<𝜀. As 𝜀 was arbitrary we see that ‖𝜃‖≤𝐶 and in particular is finite. ∎

Proposition 5.4.41 (Continuous Multilinear Maps)

Let 𝜓:𝑋1×⋯×𝑋𝑝→𝑍 be a multilinear map of normed vector spaces (using the product norm). Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝜓 is continuous

  2. There exists a constant 𝐶>0 such that

    ‖𝜓(𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑝)‖≤𝐶‖𝑥1‖⋯‖𝑥𝑝‖

  3. The following quantity is finite

    sup{‖𝜓(𝑥1,⋯,𝑥𝑝)‖|‖𝑥1‖=⋯=‖𝑥𝑝‖=1}<∞

In this case 𝐿(𝑋1,⋯,𝑋𝑝;𝑍) is a normed vector space with norm given by expression in 𝑐). Furthermore if 𝑍 is complete so is 𝐿(𝑋1,⋯,𝑋𝑝;𝑍).

Finally if 𝑋1,⋯𝑋𝑝 are finite-dimensional then every multilinear map is continuous.

Proof. a) ⟹ b) Suppose there is no such constant 𝐶, then there exists (𝑥𝑛1,⋯,𝑥𝑛𝑝)∈𝑋1×⋯×𝑋𝑝 such that
