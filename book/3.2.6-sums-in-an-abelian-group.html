3.2.6 Sums in an Abelian Group

Proposition 3.2.47 (Finite Sums in an Abelian Group)

Let 𝐺 be an abelian group. Define 𝜓0≔0∈𝐺 (the value on the one-point set 𝐺0) and, for 𝑛≥1, maps 𝜓𝑛:𝐺𝑛→𝐺 recursively by

𝜓𝑛(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)=𝑥1+𝜓𝑛−1(𝑥2,…,𝑥𝑛)

so that in particular 𝜓1=1𝐺. Then each 𝜓𝑛 is invariant under permutations, that is 𝜓𝑛(𝑥𝜎(1),…,𝑥𝜎(𝑛))=𝜓𝑛(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛) for every 𝜎∈𝑆𝑛. Moreover:

  1. 𝜓𝑚+𝑟(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑚,𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑟)=𝜓𝑚(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑚)+𝜓𝑟(𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑟) for 𝑚,𝑟≥0
  2. 𝜓𝑛(0,…,0)=0 and 𝜓𝑛+1(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛,0)=𝜓𝑛(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛) for 𝑛≥0
  3. 𝜓𝑛(𝑥1+𝑦1,…,𝑥𝑛+𝑦𝑛)=𝜓𝑛(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)+𝜓𝑛(𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑛) for 𝑛≥0
  4. if 𝜑:𝐺→𝐻 is a group homomorphism to an abelian group 𝐻 then 𝜓𝑛(𝜑(𝑥1),…,𝜑(𝑥𝑛))=𝜑(𝜓𝑛(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛))
  5. 𝜓𝑛(−𝑥1,…,−𝑥𝑛)=−𝜓𝑛(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

We show that each 𝜓𝑛 is invariant under permutations by induction on 𝑛; for 𝑛≤1 there is nothing to show. Since 𝑆𝑛 is generated by the adjacent transpositions (3.2.37) it suffices to check invariance under (𝑖𝑖+1) for 1≤𝑖<𝑛, and let 𝑛≥2 with 𝜓𝑛−1 invariant.

If 𝑖≥2 the transposition only exchanges two entries of (𝑥2,…,𝑥𝑛), so 𝜓𝑛 is unchanged since 𝜓𝑛−1 is invariant. If 𝑖=1 then

𝜓𝑛(𝑥2,𝑥1,𝑥3,…,𝑥𝑛)=𝑥2+(𝑥1+𝜓𝑛−2(𝑥3,…,𝑥𝑛))by the recursion=𝑥1+(𝑥2+𝜓𝑛−2(𝑥3,…,𝑥𝑛))by associativity and commutativity in𝐺=𝜓𝑛(𝑥1,𝑥2,𝑥3,…,𝑥𝑛)by the recursion

For a) we use induction on 𝑚. For 𝑚=0 both sides are 𝜓𝑟(𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑟), as 𝜓0=0. For 𝑚≥1, by the recursion, the induction hypothesis and associativity,

𝜓𝑚+𝑟(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑚,𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑟)=𝑥1+(𝜓𝑚−1(𝑥2,…,𝑥𝑚)+𝜓𝑟(𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑟))=𝜓𝑚(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑚)+𝜓𝑟(𝑦1,…,𝑦𝑟)

Next, 𝜓𝑛(0,…,0)=0+𝜓𝑛−1(0,…,0)=0 by induction on 𝑛, and by a) with 𝑟=1 (so 𝜓1(0)=0) 𝜓𝑛+1(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛,0)=𝜓𝑛(𝑥1,…,𝑥𝑛)+0, which proves b). The remaining properties are proved by induction on 𝑛, the case 𝑛=0 being clear (𝜓0=0, and 𝜑(0)=0 for d)). For c), writing 𝑥′=(𝑥2,…,𝑥𝑛) and 𝑦′=(𝑦2,…,𝑦𝑛),

𝜓𝑛(𝑥+𝑦)=(𝑥1+𝑦1)+𝜓𝑛−1(𝑥′+𝑦′)=(𝑥1+𝜓𝑛−1(𝑥′))+(𝑦1+𝜓𝑛−1(𝑦′))=𝜓𝑛(𝑥)+𝜓𝑛(𝑦)

using the induction hypothesis and commutativity. For d), 𝜓𝑛(𝜑(𝑥))=𝜑(𝑥1)+𝜓𝑛−1(𝜑(𝑥′))=𝜑(𝑥1)+𝜑(𝜓𝑛−1(𝑥′))=𝜑(𝜓𝑛(𝑥)) since 𝜑 is additive. Finally e) is d) for the homomorphism 𝑥↦−𝑥 of 𝐺. ∎

Definition 3.2.48 (Sum of a Finite Family)

Let 𝐺 be an abelian group, written additively. For a family (𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 in 𝐺 indexed by a finite set 𝐼 with 𝑛≥0 elements, define

∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖≔𝜓𝑛(𝑔𝑒(1),…,𝑔𝑒(𝑛))

for any enumeration of 𝐼, that is a bijection 𝑒:{1,…,𝑛}→𝐼, where 𝜓𝑛 is as in (3.2.47). In particular the sum over the empty set is 𝜓0=0. If 𝑔𝑖=0 for all 𝑖∈𝐼 then ∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖=0.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

If 𝑒,𝑒′:{1,…,𝑛}→𝐼 are two enumerations of 𝐼 then 𝜎≔𝑒−1∘𝑒′∈𝑆𝑛 and 𝑔𝑒′(𝑘)=𝑔𝑒(𝜎(𝑘)), so the sum is independent of the enumeration since 𝜓𝑛 is invariant under permutations by (3.2.47). If 𝑔𝑖=0 for all 𝑖∈𝐼 then every entry of 𝜓𝑛(𝑔𝑒(1),…,𝑔𝑒(𝑛)) is 0, so the sum is 0 since 𝜓𝑛(0,…,0)=0 by the same proposition. ∎

Proposition 3.2.49 (Sum over a Disjoint Union)

Let 𝐺 be an abelian group and (𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼∪𝐽 a family in 𝐺 indexed by the union of two disjoint finite sets 𝐼 and 𝐽. Then

∑𝑖∈𝐼∪𝐽𝑔𝑖=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖+∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Let |𝐼|=𝑚≥0 and |𝐽|=𝑟≥0, and let 𝑒𝐼:{1,…,𝑚}→𝐼 and 𝑒𝐽:{1,…,𝑟}→𝐽 be enumerations. Since 𝐼 and 𝐽 are disjoint, 𝑒:{1,…,𝑚+𝑟}→𝐼∪𝐽 with 𝑒(𝑘)=𝑒𝐼(𝑘) for 𝑘≤𝑚 and 𝑒(𝑘)=𝑒𝐽(𝑘−𝑚) for 𝑘>𝑚 is an enumeration of 𝐼∪𝐽, and by (3.2.47) (the identity for 𝜓𝑚+𝑟) and independence of the enumeration (3.2.48) ∑𝑖∈𝐼∪𝐽𝑔𝑖=𝜓𝑚+𝑟(𝑔𝑒(1),…,𝑔𝑒(𝑚+𝑟))=𝜓𝑚(𝑔𝑒𝐼(1),…,𝑔𝑒𝐼(𝑚))+𝜓𝑟(𝑔𝑒𝐽(1),…,𝑔𝑒𝐽(𝑟))=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖+∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖. ∎

Definition 3.2.50 (Sum of an Almost Zero Family)

Let 𝐺 be an abelian group, written additively. Let (𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 be a family in 𝐺 indexed by an arbitrary set 𝐼 for which all but finitely many 𝑔𝑖 are zero, that is whose support supp(𝑔)≔{𝑖∈𝐼|𝑔𝑖≠0} is finite. Define

∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖≔∑𝑖∈supp(𝑔)𝑔𝑖

using the sum over the finite set supp(𝑔) from (3.2.48). Then ∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖=∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖 for every finite subset 𝐽⊆𝐼 containing supp(𝑔). In particular, if 𝐼 is finite this is the sum of the finite family (𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 from (3.2.48) (take 𝐽=𝐼).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Let 𝐽⊆𝐼 be finite with supp(𝑔)⊆𝐽. Then 𝐽 is the disjoint union of supp(𝑔) and 𝐽∖supp(𝑔), and 𝑔𝑖=0 for every 𝑖∈𝐽∖supp(𝑔), so by (3.2.49) and (3.2.48) (a sum of zero terms is 0)

∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖=∑𝑖∈supp(𝑔)𝑔𝑖+∑𝑖∈𝐽∖supp(𝑔)𝑔𝑖=∑𝑖∈supp(𝑔)𝑔𝑖

which is the defining value. If 𝐼 is finite we may take 𝐽=𝐼. ∎

Proposition 3.2.51 (Sums of Almost Zero Families)

Let 𝐺 be an abelian group and (𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼, (ℎ𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 families in 𝐺 indexed by the same set 𝐼, with all but finitely many terms zero. Then (𝑔𝑖+ℎ𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 and (−𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 are again families with all but finitely many terms zero, and

  1. ∑𝑖∈𝐼(𝑔𝑖+ℎ𝑖)=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖+∑𝑖∈𝐼ℎ𝑖
  2. ∑𝑖∈𝐼(−𝑔𝑖)=−∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖, and consequently ∑𝑖∈𝐼(𝑔𝑖−ℎ𝑖)=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖−∑𝑖∈𝐼ℎ𝑖
  3. if 𝜑:𝐺→𝐻 is a group homomorphism to an abelian group 𝐻 (for instance multiplication by an element 𝑟 of a ring 𝑅 on the additive group 𝐺=𝑅) then (𝜑(𝑔𝑖))𝑖∈𝐼 again has all but finitely many terms zero, and ∑𝑖∈𝐼𝜑(𝑔𝑖)=𝜑(∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖).

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Let 𝐽⊆𝐼 be a finite subset containing every 𝑖 with 𝑔𝑖≠0 or ℎ𝑖≠0. For 𝑖∉𝐽 we have 𝑔𝑖+ℎ𝑖=0, −𝑔𝑖=0 and 𝜑(𝑔𝑖)=𝜑(0)=0, so all three families have all but finitely many terms zero, and 𝐽 is a valid choice of finite subset in the definition of all of the sums involved. Therefore

∑𝑖∈𝐼(𝑔𝑖+ℎ𝑖)=∑𝑖∈𝐽(𝑔𝑖+ℎ𝑖)=∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖+∑𝑖∈𝐽ℎ𝑖=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖+∑𝑖∈𝐼ℎ𝑖∑𝑖∈𝐼(−𝑔𝑖)=∑𝑖∈𝐽(−𝑔𝑖)=−∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖=−∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖∑𝑖∈𝐼𝜑(𝑔𝑖)=∑𝑖∈𝐽𝜑(𝑔𝑖)=𝜑(∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖)=𝜑(∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖)

using that finite sums are additive and commute with negation and with group homomorphisms (3.2.47). Finally 𝑔𝑖−ℎ𝑖=𝑔𝑖+(−ℎ𝑖), so the last claim of b) follows from a) and the first part of b). ∎

Proposition 3.2.52 (Sums over a Partition)

Let 𝐺 be an abelian group and (𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 a family in 𝐺 with all but finitely many terms zero. If 𝐼=⨆𝜆∈Λ𝐼𝜆 is a partition of 𝐼 into disjoint subsets then each (𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼𝜆 and the family (∑𝑖∈𝐼𝜆𝑔𝑖)𝜆∈Λ again have all but finitely many terms zero, and

∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖=∑𝜆∈Λ∑𝑖∈𝐼𝜆𝑔𝑖

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

Let 𝐽⊆𝐼 be finite containing every 𝑖 with 𝑔𝑖≠0, and let Λ𝐽≔{𝜆∈Λ|𝐼𝜆∩𝐽≠∅}, which is finite. For 𝜆∈Λ𝐽 the set 𝐽∩𝐼𝜆 is a valid choice for the subfamily, so ∑𝑖∈𝐼𝜆𝑔𝑖=∑𝑖∈𝐽∩𝐼𝜆𝑔𝑖, while for 𝜆∉Λ𝐽 all terms of the subfamily are zero and ∑𝑖∈𝐼𝜆𝑔𝑖=0. So (∑𝑖∈𝐼𝜆𝑔𝑖)𝜆∈Λ is almost zero with Λ𝐽 a valid choice, and since 𝐽 is the disjoint union of the finitely many 𝐽∩𝐼𝜆, 𝜆∈Λ𝐽, induction on |Λ𝐽| using (3.2.49) gives

∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖=∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖=∑𝜆∈Λ𝐽∑𝑖∈𝐽∩𝐼𝜆𝑔𝑖=∑𝜆∈Λ∑𝑖∈𝐼𝜆𝑔𝑖

 ∎

Lemma 3.2.53 (Reindexing and Iterated Sums)

Let 𝐺 be an abelian group.

  1. If (𝑔𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼 has all but finitely many terms zero and 𝜎:𝐼′→𝐼 is a bijection, then (𝑔𝜎(𝑖′))𝑖′∈𝐼′ has all but finitely many terms zero and ∑𝑖′∈𝐼′𝑔𝜎(𝑖′)=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖.

  2. If (𝑔(𝑖,𝑘))(𝑖,𝑘)∈𝐼×𝐾 has all but finitely many terms zero then so do each row (𝑔(𝑖,𝑘))𝑖∈𝐼 and (∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔(𝑖,𝑘))𝑘∈𝐾, and

    ∑(𝑖,𝑘)∈𝐼×𝐾𝑔(𝑖,𝑘)=∑𝑘∈𝐾∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔(𝑖,𝑘)

    and likewise with the roles of 𝑖 and 𝑘 exchanged.

Proof. Drafted by Claude Sonnet, reviewed by David Rufino.

a) Let 𝐽⊆𝐼 be finite containing every 𝑖 with 𝑔𝑖≠0. Then 𝜎−1(𝐽) is finite and contains every 𝑖′ with 𝑔𝜎(𝑖′)≠0, so the reindexed family has all but finitely many terms zero, and

∑𝑖′∈𝐼′𝑔𝜎(𝑖′)=∑𝑖′∈𝜎−1(𝐽)𝑔𝜎(𝑖′)=∑𝑖∈𝐽𝑔𝑖=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔𝑖

since 𝜎 restricts to a bijection 𝜎−1(𝐽)→𝐽 and finite sums do not depend on the ordering of the terms.

b) Apply (3.2.52) to the partition 𝐼×𝐾=⨆𝑘∈𝐾𝐼×{𝑘}. The block (𝑔(𝑖,𝑘′))(𝑖,𝑘′)∈𝐼×{𝑘} is the reindexing of the row (𝑔(𝑖,𝑘))𝑖∈𝐼 along the bijection 𝑖↦(𝑖,𝑘), so by a) the rows have all but finitely many terms zero, with ∑(𝑖,𝑘′)∈𝐼×{𝑘}𝑔(𝑖,𝑘′)=∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔(𝑖,𝑘). Hence the partition formula reads ∑(𝑖,𝑘)∈𝐼×𝐾𝑔(𝑖,𝑘)=∑𝑘∈𝐾∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔(𝑖,𝑘), and (∑𝑖∈𝐼𝑔(𝑖,𝑘))𝑘∈𝐾 has all but finitely many terms zero. The other order is the same argument applied to the partition 𝐼×𝐾=⨆𝑖∈𝐼{𝑖}×𝐾. ∎
