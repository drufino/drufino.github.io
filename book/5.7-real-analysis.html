5.7 Real Analysis

5.7.1 Closed Intervals

Proposition 5.7.1 (𝑘-cells are closed)

Subsets of the form

[𝑎1,𝑏1]×⋯×[𝑎𝑘,𝑏𝑘]⊂ℝ𝑘

are closed.

Proof. By definition of the product topology we may reduce to the case 𝑘=1. Then [𝑎,𝑏] is sequentially closed by (5.1.21), which is equivalent to being closed by (4.1.19) as ℝ is first countable. ∎

5.7.2 Power Function

Proposition 5.7.2 (Power function)

Let 𝛼∈ℚ and 𝑥∈ℝ be non-negative numbers. Suppose 𝛼=𝑚𝑛 for integers 𝑚,𝑛, where 𝑛>0. Then the function

𝑥𝛼≔𝑥𝑚𝑛=(𝑥𝑛)𝑚

is continuous and strictly increasing/decreasing (according to the sign of 𝛼). Furthermore

𝑥𝛼𝑥𝛽=𝑥𝛼+𝛽

Proof. We first consider the case 𝛼>0 and 𝑚,𝑛>0. The two definitions are equivalent because 𝑥𝛼 is the unique function satisfying (𝑥𝛼)𝑛=𝑥𝑚, the latter being an injective function. We may consider the cases 𝛼=𝑚 and 𝛼=1𝑛 separately (since the composition of continuous and increasing functions is continuous and increasing). In light of (5.1.32) and (5.4.10) then it remains to show that 𝑥𝑛 is continuous. However by (5.1.31)

|𝑦𝑛−𝑥𝑛|≤|𝑦−𝑥|𝑛𝑥𝑛𝑛−1.

Suppose 𝛼=𝑚𝑛 and 𝛽=𝑝𝑞 then 𝛼+𝛽=𝑚𝑞+𝑛𝑝𝑛𝑞 and

(𝑥𝛼𝑥𝛽)𝑛𝑞=(𝑥𝛼)𝑛𝑞(𝑥𝛽)𝑛𝑞=𝑥𝑚𝑞𝑥𝑛𝑝=𝑥𝑚𝑞+𝑛𝑝

whence by uniqueness 𝑥𝛼𝑥𝛽=𝑥𝛼+𝛽.

We may define 𝑥0=1 and 𝑥−𝛼=(𝑥𝛼)−1, and verify that the addition formula still holds. ∎

5.7.3 Countability

Proposition 5.7.3 (ℝ is separable and second countable)

The subset ℚ⊂ℝ is dense. Furthermore ℝ is second countable, with a base formed by open balls with rational center and radius.

Similarly ℚ𝑘 is dense in ℝ𝑘, which is second countable.

Proof. We only need to show that ℚ⊂ℝ is dense, and the rest follows from (5.3.15) because ℚ is countable.

By (5.1.26) for every 𝑥∈ℝ there is a sequence 𝑥𝑛∈ℚ such that 𝑥𝑛→𝑥. Then (4.1.19) shows that 𝑥∈ℚ― as required. ∎

5.7.4 Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem

Proposition 5.7.4 (Monotone Subsequence Theorem)
Let (𝑎𝑛) be a sequence in an ordered field. Then it has a monotone subsequence.

Proof. Define the set of peaks to be

𝑋={𝑛∈ℕ|𝑚≥𝑛⟹𝑥𝑛≥𝑥𝑚}

If 𝑋 is infinite then the elements determine a decreasing subsequence. Suppose 𝑋 is finite and define 𝑛1≔max(𝑋)+1. We claim inductively that there is a sequence of integers

𝑛1<𝑛2<⋯<𝑛𝑘<⋯

such that 𝑎𝑛𝑘 is increasing. Evidently 𝑛𝑘∉𝑋 whence there exists 𝑛𝑘+1>𝑛𝑘 such that 𝑎𝑛𝑘+1>𝑎𝑛𝑘. ∎

Proposition 5.7.5 (Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem)
Let (𝑎𝑛) be a bounded sequence in ℝ. Then it contains a convergent subsequence.
Proof 1.. By (5.7.4) 𝑎𝑛 has a monotone subsequence. By (5.1.23) this subsequence is convergent. ∎

Proof 2.. Define two sequences of real numbers (𝑙𝑛), (𝑢𝑛) recursively as follows

(𝑙𝑛,𝑢𝑛)≔{(𝐿,𝑈)𝑛=1(𝑙𝑛−1,𝑙𝑛−1+𝑢𝑛−12){𝑎𝑛}∩[𝑙𝑛−1,𝑙𝑛−1+𝑢𝑛−12]is infinite(𝑙𝑛−1+𝑢𝑛−12,𝑢𝑛−1)otherwise

Then by definition

  1. (𝑙𝑛) is increasing and (𝑢𝑛) is decreasing
  2. 𝑙𝑛≤𝑢𝑚 for all 𝑛,𝑚
  3. |𝑢𝑛−𝑙𝑛|=𝑈−𝐿2𝑛→0
  4. [𝑙𝑛,𝑢𝑛] contains infinitely many elements of the sequence (𝑎𝑛)

We claim that

⋂𝑛∈ℕ[𝑙𝑛,𝑢𝑛]={sup𝑙𝑛}={inf𝑢𝑛}

Suppose 𝑥,𝑦 lie in the intersection then by 𝑐) we have |𝑥−𝑦|<𝜀 for all 𝜀, whence they are equal. So the set consists of at most one element. We claim sup𝑙𝑛 lies in the intersection. For 𝑙𝑛↑sup𝑙𝑛 by (…) and 𝑙𝑛≤𝑢𝑚 whence sup𝑙𝑛≤𝑢𝑚 by (…) as required.

Finally let (𝑎𝑛𝑘) be a subsequence such that 𝑎𝑛𝑘∈[𝑙𝑘,𝑢𝑘] consequently by 𝑐) we have |𝑎𝑛𝑘−𝛼|→0 where 𝛼≔sup𝑙𝑛. ∎

Corollary 5.7.6 (Bolzano-Weierstrass in ℝ𝑘)
Let (𝑎𝑛) be a bounded sequence in ℝ𝑘. Then it contains a convergent subsequence.
Proof. Observe that (𝑎𝑛)=(𝑎𝑛1,⋯,𝑎𝑛𝑘) where 𝑎𝑖 is a bounded sequence in ℝ. By applying (5.7.5) to each coordinate in turn we may find a subsequence 𝑛𝑗 such that 𝑎𝑛𝑗𝑖→𝑎𝑖 for 𝑖=1⋯𝑘. By definition of the product norm it is clear that 𝑎𝑛𝑗→(𝑎1,⋯,𝑎𝑘). ∎
Proposition 5.7.7 (Heine-Borel Theorem)

Let 𝑋 be a subset of ℝ𝑘. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑋 is closed and bounded
  2. 𝑋 is sequentially compact
  3. 𝑋 is compact

In particular every closed 𝑘-cell [𝒂,𝒃] is compact.

Proof 1.. Recall from (5.7.3) that ℝ𝑘 is second countable, and therefore so is 𝑋. Therefore the equivalences 𝑏)⟺𝑐) follows from (4.1.106).

𝑏),𝑐)⟹𝑎) We may use (4.1.110) to show that 𝑋 is closed, observing that ℝ𝑘 is both first countable and Kolmogorov. Alternatively we may observe ℝ𝑘 is Hausdorff and appeal to (4.1.107).

To show that it is bounded we may assume it is not to find a sequence 𝑥𝑛 such that ‖𝑥𝑛‖≥𝑛. By assumption this has a convergent subsequence 𝑥𝑛𝑗→𝑥. Further there is some 𝑁1 such that 𝑁1>‖𝑥‖ and some 𝑁2 such that 𝑛≥𝑁2⟹‖𝑥𝑛−𝑥‖<𝑁1−|𝑥|⟹‖𝑥𝑛‖<𝑁1. Then taking 𝑛=max(𝑁1,𝑁2) implies ‖𝑓(𝑥𝑛)‖ is both less and greater than 𝑁1, a contradiction.

Alternatively we may reduce to the case 𝑘=1 by noting that 𝜋𝑖(𝑋) is compact (4.1.109). The family {𝐵(𝑛;1)|𝑛∈ℕ} forms an open cover, for which there must exist a finite subcover. This immediately shows that 𝜋𝑖(𝑋), and therefore 𝑋, is bounded.

𝑎)⟹𝑏),𝑐) By assumption 𝑋⊂[−𝑀,𝑀]×⋯×[−𝑀,𝑀] for some 𝑀, which by (5.7.6) is sequentially compact. We may then use either (4.1.108) or (4.1.111). ∎

Proof 2.. Firstly 𝑋 is complete iff it is closed by (5.3.13). Evidently a subset of ℝ is totally bounded iff it is bounded, and the same argument may be extended to ℝ𝑘. Therefore the equivalence follows from (5.3.17). ∎

5.7.5 Boundedness Theorem

Proposition 5.7.8
Let 𝑋 be a compact topological space and 𝑓:𝑋→ℝ𝑘 be a continuous function. Then 𝑓 is bounded and attains its bounds.

Proof 1.. We may prove directly the case 𝑋=[𝑎,𝑏] and 𝑘=1.

Suppose 𝑓 is not bounded, then there is some sequence (𝑥𝑛) in 𝑋 such that |𝑓(𝑥𝑛)|≥𝑛. By choosing a convergent subsequence (5.7.5) we may assume that 𝑥𝑛→𝑥. Further 𝑋 is closed so 𝑥∈𝑋 (4.1.19). By continuity (5.4.13) 𝑓(𝑥𝑛)→𝑓(𝑥). Choose 𝑁1>|𝑓(𝑥)| and 𝑁2 such that 𝑛≥𝑁2⟹|𝑓(𝑥𝑛)−𝑓(𝑥)|<𝑁1−|𝑓(𝑥)|⟹|𝑓(𝑥𝑛)|<𝑁1. Then 𝑛≔max(𝑁1,𝑁2)⟹|𝑓(𝑥𝑛)|<𝑁1 and |𝑓(𝑥𝑛)|>𝑁1 a contradiction. ∎

Proof 2.. We may reduce to the case 𝑘=1 by considering the composition with the norm ‖⋅‖.

By (4.1.109) (or (4.1.112)) 𝑓(𝑋) is (sequentially) compact. Then by (5.7.7) 𝑓(𝑋) is closed and bounded.

Let 𝑦=sup𝑓(𝑋). By (5.1.18) there exists 𝑦𝑛→𝑦 with 𝑦𝑛∈𝑓(𝑋). Since 𝑓(𝑋) is closed we see 𝑦∈𝑓(𝑋) by (4.1.19) as required. ∎

5.7.6 Intermediate Value Theorem

Proposition 5.7.9 (Intervals are connected)

Let 𝑋⊂ℝ. Then the following are equivalent

  1. 𝑋 is connected
  2. For every 𝑥<𝑦<𝑧 such that 𝑥,𝑧∈𝑋, we have 𝑦∈𝑋

In particular (𝑎,𝑏],(𝑎,𝑏),[𝑎,𝑏) are connected.

Proof. 𝑏)⟹𝑎) Suppose 𝑋 is disconnected, then 𝑋=𝑈⊔𝑉 with 𝑈,𝑉 non-empty open and closed subsets of 𝑋. Let 𝑥∈𝑈 and 𝑧∈𝑉, and assume wlog that 𝑥<𝑧. Define

𝛼≔sup(𝑈∩(𝑥,𝑧))

By (5.1.21) 𝛼∈[𝑥,𝑧]⊂𝑋. By (5.1.18) there exists 𝛼𝑛∈𝑈∩(𝑥,𝑧) such that 𝛼𝑛↑𝛼. Then by (4.1.20) we have 𝛼∈clℝ(𝑈)∩𝑋=(4.1.21)cl𝑋(𝑈)=(4.1.19)𝑈. If 𝛼=𝑧 then we reach a contradiction as 𝑈 and 𝑉 were assumed to be disjoint. Similarly if 𝛼<𝑧, as 𝑈 is open in 𝑋, we may choose 0<𝜀<𝑧−𝛼 such that (𝛼−𝜀,𝛼+𝜀)∩𝑋⊂𝑈. By assumption 𝛼+𝜀∈𝑋 whence 𝛼+𝜀∈𝑈, which contradicts maximality of 𝛼.

𝑎)⟹𝑏) Suppose we have 𝑥<𝑦<𝑧 such that 𝑥,𝑧∈𝑋 but 𝑦∉𝑋. Then define the open subsets 𝑈=(−∞,𝑦)∩𝑋 and 𝑉=(𝑦,∞)∩𝑋. Then

𝑈≔(−∞,𝑦)∩𝑋=(−∞,𝑦]∩𝑋

is both open and closed. ∎

Proposition 5.7.10 (Intermediate Value Theorem)
Let 𝑎<𝑏 be real numbers and 𝑓:[𝑎,𝑏]→ℝ continuous such that 𝑓(𝑎)<𝑓(𝑏). Then for every 𝑓(𝑎)<𝑐<𝑓(𝑏) there exists 𝑥∈(𝑎,𝑏) such that 𝑓(𝑥)=𝑐.

Proof 1.. Consider

𝑋≔{𝑥′∈[𝑎,𝑏]|𝑓(𝑥′)<𝑐}

By definition 𝑋 is bounded, and 𝑎∈𝑋, so it is non-empty, so we may define 𝑥≔sup𝑋. We claim that 𝑓(𝑥)=𝑐. By (5.1.18) there is a sequence 𝑥𝑛∈𝑋 such that 𝑥𝑛→𝑥. By (5.4.13) 𝑓(𝑥𝑛)→𝑓(𝑥) and by (5.1.21) 𝑥≤𝑏 and 𝑓(𝑥)≤𝑐. Further by assumption 𝑥≠𝑏.

To show 𝑓(𝑥)≥𝑐 consider the sequence 𝑥𝑛≔𝑥+min(1𝑛,𝑏−𝑥)∈[𝑎,𝑏]. Evidently 𝑥𝑛→𝑥 so by continuity 𝑓(𝑥𝑛)→𝑓(𝑥). By construction 𝑓(𝑥𝑛)≥𝑐 whence 𝑓(𝑥)≥𝑐.

Therefore we conclude 𝑓(𝑥)=𝑐 as required. ∎

Proof 2.. By (5.7.9) [𝑎,𝑏] is connected, whence by (4.1.118) 𝑓([𝑎,𝑏]) is connected. The result follows from (5.7.9) again. ∎

5.7.7 Mean-Value Theorem

Proposition 5.7.11 (Mean-Value Theorem)

Let 𝑓:[𝑎,𝑏]→ℝ be a continuous function which is differentiable on (𝑎,𝑏). Then there exists 𝑐∈(𝑎,𝑏) such that

𝑓′(𝑐)=𝑓(𝑏)−𝑓(𝑎)𝑏−𝑎

Proof 1.. Define the function

𝑔(𝑥)≔𝑓(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑏)−𝑓(𝑎)𝑏−𝑎(𝑥−𝑎)

Then 𝑔 satisfies the same conditions with 𝑔(𝑎)=𝑔(𝑏)=𝑓(𝑎) and 𝑔′(𝑥)=𝑓′(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑏)−𝑓(𝑎)𝑏−𝑎. Evidently it is sufficient to find 𝑐∈(𝑎,𝑏) such that 𝑔′(𝑐)=0.

By (5.7.8) 𝑔 is bounded and attains its maximum and minimum, say at 𝑐,𝑑 respectively. If both 𝑐,𝑑∈{𝑎,𝑏} then 𝑔 must be the constant function whence 𝑔′ is exactly zero. Otherwise we may assume for example that 𝑐∈(𝑎,𝑏). By (5.5.2)

lim𝑛→∞𝑔(𝑐±1𝑛)−𝑔(𝑐)±1𝑛=𝑔′(𝑐)

As 𝑐 is an extremum we may deduce from (5.1.21) that both 𝑔′(𝑐)≤0 and 𝑔′(𝑐)≥0, whence 𝑔′(𝑐)=0. ∎

Corollary 5.7.12
Let 𝑓:[𝑎,𝑏]→ℝ a continuous function which is differentiable on (𝑎,𝑏). If 𝑓′(𝑥) is positive (resp. strictly positive, negative, strictly negative) then 𝑓(𝑥) is increasing (resp. strictly increasing, decreasing, strictly decreasing).
Proposition 5.7.13
Let 𝑓:(𝑎,𝑏)→ℝ be a strictly increasing (resp. decreasing) continuous function. Then 𝑓 is a homeomorphism onto its image.

Proof. Evidently 𝑓 is injective, therefore it is only required to show that the map

is continuous. Without loss of generality we may assume that 𝑓 is strictly increasing. Suppose 𝑦=𝑓(𝑥) then we require to show for every 𝜀>0 there exists 𝛿>0 such that

|𝑦′−𝑦|<𝛿⟹|𝑓−1(𝑦′)−𝑓−1(𝑦)|<𝜀

Define 𝜀′≤𝜀 such that (𝑥−𝜀′,𝑥+𝜀′)⊂(𝑎,𝑏). Define 𝛿≔min(𝑓(𝑥+𝜀′)−𝑓(𝑥),𝑓(𝑥)−𝑓(𝑥−𝜀′)). Then

|𝑦′−𝑦|<𝛿⟹𝑓(𝑥−𝜀′)<𝑦′<𝑓(𝑥+𝜀′)⟹𝑥−𝜀′<𝑓−1(𝑦′)<𝑥+𝜀′⟹|𝑓−1(𝑦′)−𝑓−1(𝑦)|<𝜀′≤𝜀

as 𝑓−1 is also monotonic. ∎
